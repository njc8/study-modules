Critical points and the second derivative test

Hills and valleys sit where the tangent plane is flat. Finding the flat spots is easy. Telling a hill from a valley from a mountain pass takes one determinant.

A function \(f\) has a local maximum at \((a,b)\) if \(f(x,y)\le f(a,b)\) for every \((x,y)\) in some disk around \((a,b)\). The number \(f(a,b)\) is the local maximum value. Swap the inequality and you get a local minimum. Notice the language: the point is where it happens, the value is the number \(f(a,b)\). Exams ask for one or the other, so read carefully.

If \(f\) has a local max or min at \((a,b)\) and both first partials exist there, then both must vanish. The reason is one-variable calculus twice over: freeze \(y=b\) and \(g(x)=f(x,b)\) has a local extremum at \(x=a\), so \(g'(a)=f_x(a,b)=0\). Freeze \(x=a\) and the same argument gives \(f_y(a,b)=0\).

\((a,b)\text{ is a }\textbf{critical point}\iff f_x(a,b)=0\text{ and }f_y(a,b)=0,\ \text{ or one of them does not exist}\)Equivalently \(\nabla f(a,b)=\mathbf{0}\). The tangent plane there is horizontal: \(z=f(a,b)\).
Necessary, not sufficient. Every local extremum is a critical point, but plenty of critical points are neither a max nor a min. The origin is a critical point of \(f=x^2-y^2\), yet \(f\) climbs along the \(x\)-axis and falls along the \(y\)-axis. That is a saddle point.

The second derivative test

To sort the critical points, look at the second partials. Build the discriminant \(D\), which is the determinant of the matrix of second partials (the Hessian):

\(D=D(a,b)=\begin{vmatrix}f_{xx}&f_{xy}\\ f_{xy}&f_{yy}\end{vmatrix}=f_{xx}(a,b)\,f_{yy}(a,b)-\big(f_{xy}(a,b)\big)^2\)Note the square on the mixed partial. Since \(f_{xy}=f_{yx}\) for the functions in this course, the matrix is symmetric and only one mixed partial is needed.

With \(f_x(a,b)=f_y(a,b)=0\), the four cases are:

  • \(D>0\) and \(f_{xx}(a,b)>0\): local minimum at \((a,b)\). The surface curves up in every direction.
  • \(D>0\) and \(f_{xx}(a,b)<0\): local maximum. The surface curves down in every direction.
  • \(D<0\): saddle point. It curves up in one direction and down in another, so it is neither.
  • \(D=0\): the test is inconclusive. It could be any of the three, and you need another argument.

Where does \(D\) come from? Near a critical point the surface looks like the quadratic \(f(a,b)+\tfrac12\big(f_{xx}h^2+2f_{xy}hk+f_{yy}k^2\big)\). That quadratic keeps one sign for all \((h,k)\ne(0,0)\) exactly when \(f_{xx}f_{yy}-(f_{xy})^2>0\), and then the sign it keeps is the sign of \(f_{xx}\). When \(D<0\) the quadratic takes both signs, which is a saddle.

\(D>0\) is only half the answer. A positive \(D\) tells you the point is an extremum but not which kind. You must then look at the sign of \(f_{xx}\) (or of \(f_{yy}\); when \(D>0\) they always agree). Writing "\(D>0\), so it is a minimum" is the single most common error on this topic.

The procedure

Finding and classifying local extrema

  1. Compute \(f_x\) and \(f_y\).
  2. Solve the system \(f_x=0,\ f_y=0\) for all solutions. Factor rather than divide, so you do not lose roots.
  3. Compute \(f_{xx}\), \(f_{xy}\), and \(f_{yy}\) once, as functions of \(x\) and \(y\).
  4. Evaluate \(D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-(f_{xy})^2\) at each critical point.
  5. Classify with the four cases, and evaluate \(f\) at the maxima and minima to get the extreme values.

Step-by-step: run the procedure

    Step 2 is where problems are lost. When \(f_x\) factors as a product, every factor gives a branch, and each branch must be carried through the other equation.

    Absolute extrema on closed regions

    On a closed bounded region the largest and smallest values are guaranteed to exist. Finding them is a search over a short list of candidates, with no second derivative test in sight.

    A region is closed if it contains all of its boundary points, and bounded if it fits inside some disk. A filled rectangle, a filled disk, and a filled triangle are all closed and bounded. The open square \(0<x<1,\ 0<y<1\) is not closed, and the whole plane is not bounded.

    \(\text{If }f\text{ is continuous on a closed, bounded set }R\text{, then }f\text{ attains an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on }D.\)The Extreme Value Theorem. It promises the answers exist, which is why a finite candidate list is enough.

    An absolute extremum happens either at an interior point, where it is also a local extremum and therefore a critical point, or somewhere on the boundary. That splits the search in two.

    Finding absolute extrema on a closed bounded region \(R\)

    1. Find the critical points of \(f\) inside \(R\) and evaluate \(f\) at each. A critical point outside \(R\) is irrelevant and must be dropped.
    2. Reduce the boundary to one-variable problems. Each edge or arc gets its own function of a single variable on a closed interval.
    3. On each piece, find the interior critical numbers of that one-variable function and also take the endpoints of the piece.
    4. Compare every value. The largest is the absolute maximum, the smallest is the absolute minimum.

    Reducing the boundary

    Rectangle \([p,q]\times[r,s]\): four edges. On the bottom edge \(y=r\), the function is \(g(x)=f(x,r)\) for \(p\le x\le q\). Do the same on the other three. The four corners are the endpoints shared by two edges, so they always land on the candidate list.

    Disk \(x^2+y^2\le r^2\): the boundary is the circle \(x^2+y^2=r^2\). Either parametrize it, \(x=r\cos t,\ y=r\sin t\) with \(0\le t\le 2\pi\), or substitute \(y^2=r^2-x^2\) when \(f\) only involves \(y^2\), which turns the boundary into a one-variable problem on \([-r,r]\). Lagrange multipliers (Chapter 3) handle the same job.

    Triangle: three edges, each a segment. Parametrize the slanted one, for instance \((x,y)=(1-t)P+tQ\) for \(0\le t\le 1\), and include the three vertices.

    No second derivative test here. Once the region is closed and bounded, you never classify anything. You build a candidate list and compare numbers. An interior saddle point still belongs on the list; it simply will not win.
    Three things get dropped. Forgetting the corners, forgetting to check that the interior critical point actually lies inside \(R\), and forgetting the endpoints of each boundary piece. Any one of them can hide the true maximum. Write the candidate list out in full before you compare.

    Lagrange multipliers

    Optimize \(f\) when the input is not free but locked onto a curve or a surface. One vector equation replaces the whole substitution mess.

    To find the extreme values of \(f(x,y)\) subject to a constraint \(g(x,y)=c\), you could try to solve the constraint for \(y\) and substitute. That works when it works, and fails the moment the constraint will not solve cleanly. The method of Lagrange multipliers never needs to.

    \(\nabla f(x,y)=\lambda\,\nabla g(x,y)\qquad\text{together with}\qquad g(x,y)=c\)Three equations in three unknowns \(x,y,\lambda\). The number \(\lambda\) is the Lagrange multiplier.

    Why parallel gradients

    Walk along the constraint curve and watch \(f\) change. The rate of change is the component of \(\nabla f\) along your direction of travel. At a point where \(f\) is largest or smallest along the curve, that rate is zero, so \(\nabla f\) has no component along the curve: it is perpendicular to the curve. But \(\nabla g\) is also perpendicular to the curve, because the curve is a level curve of \(g\). Two vectors perpendicular to the same curve at the same point are parallel, and parallel means one is a scalar multiple of the other. That scalar is \(\lambda\).

    The same statement in pictures: at the extreme point the level curve of \(f\) is tangent to the constraint curve. If they crossed instead of touching, you could slide a little along the constraint onto a higher level curve of \(f\), so you were not at the maximum.

    The procedure

    1. Write \(g(x,y)=c\) with everything moved to one side, and compute \(\nabla f\) and \(\nabla g\).
    2. Set up \(f_x=\lambda g_x\), \(f_y=\lambda g_y\), and \(g=c\).
    3. Solve the system. Standard tricks: divide one equation by the other to eliminate \(\lambda\), or solve each for \(\lambda\) and set the results equal. Before you divide, handle the case where the thing you are dividing by is zero, as a separate branch.
    4. Collect every solution point. Evaluate \(f\) at each one.
    5. The largest value is the maximum, the smallest is the minimum. If the constraint set is unbounded, check whether \(f\) actually runs off to \(\pm\infty\); if it does, that extreme is DNE.
    \(\lambda\) is not the answer. The multiplier is scaffolding. Nobody is asking for it. After you solve, you still have to plug each candidate point back into \(f\) and compare the values. Students who stop at \(\lambda=3\) have not answered the question.

    Step-by-step: maximize and minimize \(f(x,y)=xy\) on \(x^2+y^2=8\)

      Step 3 is the archetype: multiply the two gradient equations by \(y\) and \(x\) so both right sides become \(2\lambda xy\), then set the left sides equal. That eliminates \(\lambda\) without ever dividing by something that might be zero.

      Three variables, and two constraints

      Nothing changes in three variables: to extremize \(f(x,y,z)\) on the surface \(g(x,y,z)=c\), solve \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\) together with \(g=c\). That is four equations in \(x,y,z,\lambda\).

      With two constraints \(g=c\) and \(h=k\), the feasible set is a curve, the intersection of two surfaces. At an extreme point \(\nabla f\) must be perpendicular to that curve, and the vectors perpendicular to it are exactly the combinations of \(\nabla g\) and \(\nabla h\):

      \(\nabla f=\lambda\,\nabla g+\mu\,\nabla h,\qquad g=c,\qquad h=k\)Five equations in \(x,y,z,\lambda,\mu\).
      When an extreme is DNE. Lagrange finds the candidates, but only a closed bounded constraint set guarantees both extremes exist. On a circle or a sphere both always exist. On a line or a plane, \(f\) may be unbounded in one direction: \(f=x^2+y^2\) on \(x+y=4\) has a minimum of \(8\) and no maximum at all.
