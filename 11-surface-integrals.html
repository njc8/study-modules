Surface integrals, Stokes, and the divergence theorem

A surface is a two-parameter object. Once you can write it as \(\mathbf{r}(u,v)\), every integral over it becomes an ordinary double integral, and the last two big theorems of the course fall out.

How to use this

Work the chapters in order. Each one teaches an idea, gives you something to move around in 3D, walks through a computation step by step, then drills you with fresh random problems until you hit the goal. Progress is saved in this browser.

Answer formats: a vector whose components are expressions in \(u\) and \(v\) is typed like <-2u, -2v, 1> or <3cos(u), 3sin(u), 0>. Numbers can be exact, like 12pi/5, sqrt(14)*6, 2pi*sqrt(2)/3, or a decimal to three places. Equations of planes can be written in any equivalent form. Press Enter to check, and Enter again for a new question.

What you will be able to do at the end

  • Parametrize a plane, cylinder, sphere, cone, graph, or surface of revolution, and recognize a surface from its parametrization.
  • Compute \(\mathbf{r}_u\), \(\mathbf{r}_v\), and \(\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v\), write the tangent plane, and find surface area.
  • Evaluate \(\iint_S f\,dS\) for a scalar function: mass of a sheet, average value, area.
  • Evaluate the flux \(\iint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{S}\) through an oriented surface, and get the sign right.
  • Use Stokes' theorem to trade a line integral for a flux of curl, or the other way round.
  • Use the divergence theorem to trade a closed-surface flux for a triple integral, and say which of the five big theorems a problem wants.

Chapter 1 · Stewart 16.6

Parametric surfaces and surface area

A curve needs one parameter. A surface needs two. Everything else follows from that one fact.

A parametric surface is a vector function of two variables,

\(\mathbf{r}(u,v)=\langle x(u,v),\,y(u,v),\,z(u,v)\rangle,\qquad (u,v)\in D\)\(D\) is the parameter domain, a flat region in the \(uv\)-plane. The surface \(S\) is the image of \(D\).

Freezing one parameter gives a grid curve. Fix \(u=u_0\) and let \(v\) run: you get a curve lying in \(S\). Fix \(v=v_0\) and let \(u\) run: another curve. The two families of grid curves are the mesh you see drawn on every surface picture.

The parametrizations you should know cold

SurfaceParametrizationDomain
Plane through \(P\) spanned by \(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\)\(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{p}+u\,\mathbf{a}+v\,\mathbf{b}\)\(u,v\in\mathbb{R}\)
Graph \(z=f(x,y)\)\(\mathbf{r}=\langle x,\,y,\,f(x,y)\rangle\)\((x,y)\in D\)
Cylinder \(x^2+y^2=a^2\)\(\mathbf{r}=\langle a\cos u,\,a\sin u,\,v\rangle\)\(0\le u\le 2\pi\)
Sphere of radius \(a\)\(\mathbf{r}=\langle a\sin\phi\cos\theta,\,a\sin\phi\sin\theta,\,a\cos\phi\rangle\)\(0\le\phi\le\pi,\ 0\le\theta\le 2\pi\)
Cone \(z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\)\(\mathbf{r}=\langle u\cos v,\,u\sin v,\,u\rangle\)\(u\ge 0,\ 0\le v\le 2\pi\)
Revolving \(y=f(x)\), \(a\le x\le b\), about the \(x\)-axis\(\mathbf{r}=\langle u,\,f(u)\cos v,\,f(u)\sin v\rangle\)\(a\le u\le b,\ 0\le v\le 2\pi\)

Pattern: whenever a circle is involved, one parameter is the angle. Whenever the surface is a graph, the two parameters are just \(x\) and \(y\).

Tangent vectors and the normal

Differentiate along each grid curve:

\(\mathbf{r}_u=\left\langle \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u},\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial u},\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial u}\right\rangle,\qquad \mathbf{r}_v=\left\langle \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial v},\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial v},\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial v}\right\rangle\)\(\mathbf{r}_u\) is tangent to the curve where \(v\) is frozen; \(\mathbf{r}_v\) is tangent to the curve where \(u\) is frozen. Both are tangent to \(S\).

Two tangent vectors determine the tangent plane, and their cross product is perpendicular to it:

\(\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v\)The normal vector. The surface is smooth where \(\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v\neq\mathbf{0}\). The tangent plane at \(\mathbf{r}(u_0,v_0)\) is the plane through that point with this normal.

For a graph \(\mathbf{r}=\langle u,v,f(u,v)\rangle\) this collapses to something worth memorizing: \(\mathbf{r}_u=\langle 1,0,f_u\rangle\), \(\mathbf{r}_v=\langle 0,1,f_v\rangle\), so

\(\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v=\langle -f_u,\,-f_v,\,1\rangle\)The \(z\)-component is \(+1\), so this normal always points upward. You will use it constantly in the flux chapter.

Explore: grid curves, \(\mathbf{r}_u\), \(\mathbf{r}_v\), and \(\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v\)

Drag to rotate, scroll to zoom. Purple curves are the two grid curves through the marked point. Arrows are drawn to a common scale, not to true length.

Put the point on the sphere's north pole (\(u=0\)): \(\mathbf{r}_v\) collapses to zero and the normal dies with it, which is why the sphere's parametrization is not smooth at the poles. On the cone, do the same at \(u=0\): the vertex is the one bad point. On the helicoid, notice \(|\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v|=\sqrt{1+u^2}\) grows as you move out along the ramp.

The normal is not unique. Swapping the roles of \(u\) and \(v\) reverses the cross product: \(\mathbf{r}_v\times\mathbf{r}_u=-(\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v)\). For area it makes no difference, because area uses the length. For flux it flips the sign of the answer, so from Chapter 3 on you must check which way your normal points.

Surface area

A small rectangle \(\Delta u\times\Delta v\) in the parameter domain maps to a small patch on \(S\) that is almost the parallelogram spanned by \(\mathbf{r}_u\,\Delta u\) and \(\mathbf{r}_v\,\Delta v\). The area of that parallelogram is \(|\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v|\,\Delta u\,\Delta v\). Add up and take the limit:

\(A(S)=\displaystyle\iint_D |\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v|\,dA\)\(|\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v|\) is the stretch factor: how much area the map from \(D\) to \(S\) creates at each point.

For a graph \(z=f(x,y)\), \(|\langle -f_x,-f_y,1\rangle|=\sqrt{1+f_x^2+f_y^2}\), which gives the version you have probably seen first:

\(A(S)=\displaystyle\iint_D \sqrt{1+f_x^2+f_y^2}\;dA\)
Do not integrate over the surface's shadow and forget the stretch. \(dA\) is area in the parameter domain; \(dS=|\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v|\,dA\) is area on the surface. Dropping that factor is the single most common error in this chapter, and it always makes the answer too small.

Chapter 2 · Stewart 16.7

Surface integrals of scalar functions

Same idea as a line integral, one dimension up: chop the surface, weight each piece by \(f\), add.

Cut \(D\) into small rectangles, map them to patches on \(S\), multiply the value of \(f\) at a sample point by the patch's area, and add. The patch area is \(|\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v|\,\Delta u\,\Delta v\), so:

\(\displaystyle\iint_S f\,dS=\iint_D f\big(\mathbf{r}(u,v)\big)\,\big|\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v\big|\,dA\)Substitute the parametrization into \(f\), multiply by the stretch factor, integrate over the flat parameter domain.

For a graph \(z=g(x,y)\) over a region \(D\) in the \(xy\)-plane:

\(\displaystyle\iint_S f\,dS=\iint_D f\big(x,y,g(x,y)\big)\,\sqrt{1+g_x^2+g_y^2}\;dA\)The \(z\) inside \(f\) gets replaced by \(g(x,y)\). Nothing is left over the surface: everything is now a function of \(x\) and \(y\).

Three things this computes:

  • Area, when \(f=1\).
  • Mass of a thin sheet with surface density \(\rho(x,y,z)\): \(m=\iint_S \rho\,dS\). Centers of mass and moments work the same way as in earlier chapters.
  • Average value of \(f\) on \(S\): \(\dfrac{1}{A(S)}\iint_S f\,dS\).

If \(S\) is made of several smooth pieces (a box, a closed cylinder with its two lids), integrate over each piece and add. That is legal because area adds.

Step by step: \(\iint_S z\,dS\) over a piece of a plane

    Notice where the work actually is: the stretch factor is a constant for a plane, so it slides straight out of the integral, and what remains is a first-year double integral of a linear function over a rectangle.

    Explore: how one parameter cell stretches

    Color shows the value of \(f\) on the surface: blue is low, orange is high. The purple outline is the image of one parameter cell \(\Delta u\times\Delta v\).

    Move the cell around and watch the two areas in the readout. On the sphere the cell shrinks to nothing near the poles even though \(\Delta u\,\Delta v\) never changes: that is the \(\sin\phi\) in \(dS=a^2\sin\phi\,d\phi\,d\theta\). On the plane the ratio is the same everywhere, which is why a plane's stretch factor comes out of the integral.

    Orientation does not matter here. \(dS\) is an area, always positive. Reversing \(u\) and \(v\), or walking the surface the other way, changes nothing: \(\iint_S f\,dS\) has one value. That is the difference between this chapter and the next one.
    Substitute before you integrate. \(\iint_S z\,dS\) over \(z=g(x,y)\) is not \(\iint_D z\,dA\). Replace \(z\) by \(g(x,y)\) and attach \(\sqrt{1+g_x^2+g_y^2}\). If any \(z\) is still standing after you set up the double integral, you have made this mistake.

    Chapter 3 · Stewart 16.7

    Surface integrals of vector fields: flux

    How much of the field crosses the surface, and which way. This is the integral the last two theorems are about.

    An oriented surface comes with a choice of unit normal \(\mathbf{n}\) at every point, varying continuously. A smooth parametrized surface has two choices, and they are negatives of each other:

    \(\mathbf{n}=\dfrac{\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v}{|\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v|}\qquad\text{or}\qquad -\mathbf{n}\)

    The flux of \(\mathbf{F}\) through \(S\) is the surface integral of the normal component of \(\mathbf{F}\):

    \(\displaystyle\iint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{S}=\iint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS=\iint_D \mathbf{F}\big(\mathbf{r}(u,v)\big)\cdot\big(\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v\big)\,dA\)The lengths cancel: \(\mathbf{n}\,dS=(\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v)\,dA\). You never normalize anything. Just dot \(\mathbf{F}\) with the raw cross product.

    Only the component of \(\mathbf{F}\) along \(\mathbf{n}\) counts. Field sliding tangent to the surface contributes nothing. If \(\mathbf{F}\) is a fluid velocity times density, the flux is mass per unit time crossing \(S\) in the direction of \(\mathbf{n}\).

    For a graph \(z=g(x,y)\) with the upward normal, use \(\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v=\langle -g_x,-g_y,1\rangle\) and \(\mathbf{F}=\langle P,Q,R\rangle\):

    \(\displaystyle\iint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{S}=\iint_D\big(-P\,g_x-Q\,g_y+R\big)\,dA\)Everything inside is evaluated at \((x,y,g(x,y))\). For the downward normal, negate the whole thing.

    For a closed surface (a sphere, the skin of a box, a capped cylinder) the convention is fixed: positive orientation means the outward normal. Flux through a closed surface then measures net outflow.

    A box, or a cylinder with lids, is handled one face at a time: each face is its own parametrized surface with its own outward normal, and the fluxes add. On the face \(x=a\) of a box the outward normal is \(\mathbf{i}\), so that face contributes \(\iint P\,dA\) with \(x=a\) substituted; on the face \(x=0\) it is \(-\mathbf{i}\).

    Step by step: flux through a piece of a plane

      Step 2 is the one to slow down on. Write \(\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v\) first, then look at its \(z\)-component to see which way it points, and only then decide whether you need it or its negative.

      Explore: normals, the field, and the sign of the flux

      Green arrows are the chosen normals. Orange arrows are \(\mathbf{F}\) sampled at the same points. Where they lean the same way, that patch contributes positively.

      Try \(\mathbf{F}=\langle -y,x,0\rangle\) on every surface: it circulates around the \(z\)-axis, always tangent to the cylinder and never crossing it, so the flux is 0. Then try \(\mathbf{F}=\langle 0,0,1\rangle\) on the hemisphere and on the flat disk that shares its boundary circle: same answer, a first hint of the divergence theorem. Tick the box and every number changes sign.

      Flux depends on orientation. \(\iint_S f\,dS\) has one value, but \(\iint_S\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{S}\) has two, differing by a sign. An answer without a stated orientation is not an answer. If the problem says "upward", check that the \(z\)-component of your \(\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v\) is positive; if it says "outward" on a closed surface, check the normal at one easy point, for example the top of a sphere.
      Shortcut for spheres. On the sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2=a^2\) the outward unit normal is \(\mathbf{n}=\frac{1}{a}\langle x,y,z\rangle\), the position vector normalized. So for \(\mathbf{F}=\langle x,y,z\rangle\), \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}=\frac{x^2+y^2+z^2}{a}=a\) is constant and the flux is just \(a\cdot 4\pi a^2\). Look for that kind of collapse before setting up any integral.

      Chapter 4 · Stewart 16.8

      Stokes' theorem

      The circulation around the edge equals the total curl through the inside. Green's theorem, lifted off the plane.

      \(\displaystyle\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}=\iint_S \operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{S}\)\(S\) is an oriented piecewise-smooth surface whose boundary is the simple closed curve \(C\), oriented compatibly. \(\mathbf{F}\) needs continuous partials on an open region containing \(S\).

      Compatible orientation is the whole game. The right-hand rule: point your right thumb along the normal \(\mathbf{n}\); your fingers curl the way \(C\) must be traversed. Equivalently, walk along \(C\) with your head pointing along \(\mathbf{n}\), and the surface should be on your left.

      The flat case: if \(S\) is a region in the \(xy\)-plane with the upward normal \(\mathbf{k}\), then \(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{k}=Q_x-P_y\) and \(C\) is counterclockwise seen from above. Stokes reads \(\oint_C P\,dx+Q\,dy=\iint_D (Q_x-P_y)\,dA\), which is exactly Green's theorem.

      Two ways to use it, and you choose based on which side looks worse:

      1. Ugly line integral, nice surface. \(C\) is a circle or a triangle in space and \(\mathbf{F}\) is messy. Replace \(\oint_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) by the flux of \(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}\) through the flattest surface you can find with that boundary, usually a disk or a triangle. When \(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}\) is constant, the flux is just \(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\) times the area.
      2. Ugly surface, nice boundary. \(S\) is a hemisphere or a paraboloid cap and you are asked for \(\iint_S\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{S}\). Drop to the boundary circle and compute one line integral in \(t\).
      Any surface with the same boundary works. If \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) share the boundary curve \(C\) and are oriented compatibly with the same direction on \(C\), then \(\iint_{S_1}\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{S}=\oint_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}=\iint_{S_2}\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{S}\). A hemisphere, a paraboloid cap, and the flat disk over the same circle all give the same answer, so always take the flat one.

      Step by step: a line integral around a circle, done with Stokes

        Compare the work here with parametrizing the circle and grinding out \(\oint\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) directly. Same answer, a quarter of the algebra, because the curl turned out to be a constant vector.

        Explore: both sides of Stokes' theorem at once

        Purple is the boundary circle \(C\); the moving dot shows its direction. Green arrows are \(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}\) on the surface. Both integrals are computed numerically.

        Switch between the hemisphere, the paraboloid cap, and the flat disk without touching the field: the flux of curl does not move, because all three have the same boundary. Then flip the normal: the dot reverses and both sides change sign together, which is what "compatibly oriented" means.

        Curl, then dot, then integrate. Two sign traps. First, \(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}=\langle R_y-Q_z,\;P_z-R_x,\;Q_x-P_y\rangle\): the middle component is the one people get backwards, so expand the determinant instead of trusting memory. Second, if the normal points down, \(C\) runs clockwise seen from above; using the counterclockwise direction anyway costs you a minus sign.

        Chapter 5 · Stewart 16.9

        The divergence theorem

        Total outflow through the skin equals the sum of the sources inside.

        \(\displaystyle\iint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{S}=\iiint_E \operatorname{div}\mathbf{F}\,dV\)\(E\) is a solid region, \(S=\partial E\) is its closed boundary surface with the outward normal, and \(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{F}=P_x+Q_y+R_z\).

        The intuition: \(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{F}\) at a point is the rate at which the field is "created" there, outflow per unit volume. Add that over the whole solid and the interior contributions cancel in pairs, since whatever leaves one small box enters its neighbor. Only the outer skin survives, and that is the flux.

        Use it in either direction:

        • Surface to volume. A flux through six faces of a box, or through a sphere, becomes one triple integral. When \(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{F}\) is constant the answer is (that constant) \(\times\) (the volume), and there is nothing left to compute.
        • Volume to surface. Rarely, a hard triple integral is easier as a flux.
        • Surface to surface. If \(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{F}=0\), the flux out of any closed surface is 0, so the flux through a complicated surface equals the flux through any nicer one that traps no extra divergence between them.

        Explore: flux out, divergence in

        The solid is drawn faintly with its outward normals in green and \(\mathbf{F}\) in orange. Both sides of the theorem are computed numerically on a grid.

        Take \(\mathbf{F}=\langle -y,x,0\rangle\): the divergence is 0 and the flux is 0 on every solid, because the field only swirls. Now take \(\mathbf{F}=\langle x,y,z\rangle\): the divergence is 3 everywhere, so the flux must be exactly 3 times the volume, whichever solid you pick. And \(\langle x^2,0,0\rangle\) gives 0 on these symmetric solids: the positive outflow on one side is cancelled by inflow on the other.

        The surface must be closed. The divergence theorem applies to \(\partial E\), the entire boundary of a solid. For an open surface such as a paraboloid cap or a cylinder without lids, either close it with a disk and subtract the lid's flux at the end, or compute the surface integral directly. Using it on an open surface is the most common way to lose every point on a problem.
        Divergence zero means sourceless. If \(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{F}=0\) on a region, the flux through every closed surface inside that region is 0. This is the 3D analogue of a conservative field having zero circulation, and it is why magnetic fields have no net flux out of any closed surface.

        The family of fundamental theorems

        All five say the same sentence: the integral of a derivative over a region equals an integral of the original function over the boundary of that region.

        TheoremRegionStatementBoundary
        FTCinterval \([a,b]\)\(\int_a^b F'(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)\)two endpoints
        FTC for line integralscurve \(C\)\(\int_C \nabla f\cdot d\mathbf{r}=f(B)-f(A)\)two endpoints
        Greenplane region \(D\)\(\iint_D (Q_x-P_y)\,dA=\oint_C P\,dx+Q\,dy\)closed curve \(C\)
        Stokessurface \(S\) in space\(\iint_S \operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{S}=\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\)closed curve \(C\)
        Divergencesolid \(E\)\(\iiint_E \operatorname{div}\mathbf{F}\,dV=\iint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{S}\)closed surface \(S\)

        Reading the table by dimension: 1D region with 0D boundary, then curve with endpoints, then 2D with 1D, then surface with curve, then 3D with 2D. Picking the right theorem is mostly a matter of asking what kind of region you are integrating over and what its boundary is.
