Triple integrals

A triple integral adds up a density over a solid. The formula is never the hard part. Finding the limits is, and picking the right coordinate system is how you make the limits easy.

How to use this

Work through the chapters in order. Each one teaches an idea, gives you a solid to spin around, then drills you with fresh random problems until you hit the goal. Progress is saved in this browser.

Answer formats: numbers can be exact, like 4pi/3, 27/8, sqrt(2)/2, or 4*pi*(e-1)/3. Angles can be typed as pi/4, 7pi/4, or a decimal. Limits of integration that depend on another variable are typed as expressions, like 4 - r^2, 3(1 - x/2 - y/5), or sqrt(9-y^2). Press Enter to check, and Enter again for a new question.

Chapter 1 · Stewart 15.6

Triple integrals in rectangular coordinates

One more integral sign, one more variable, one more pair of limits. Everything that is hard here is bookkeeping about the limits.

Over a rectangular box \(B=[a,b]\times[c,d]\times[r,s]\), Fubini's theorem says the triple integral is an iterated integral and the order does not matter:

\(\displaystyle\iiint_B f\,dV=\int_r^s\!\!\int_c^d\!\!\int_a^b f(x,y,z)\,dx\,dy\,dz\)The innermost differential goes with the innermost integral sign. Over a box there are six orders and all six give the same number, because every limit is a constant.

Over a general solid the order matters, because the limits stop being constants. The workhorse description is a type 1 solid: the solid sits between two surfaces, a floor and a ceiling, over a flat shadow \(D\) in the \(xy\)-plane.

\(\displaystyle E=\{(x,y,z)\;:\;(x,y)\in D,\; u_1(x,y)\le z\le u_2(x,y)\}\)
\(\displaystyle\iiint_E f\,dV=\iint_D\left[\int_{u_1(x,y)}^{u_2(x,y)} f(x,y,z)\,dz\right]dA\)Do the \(z\) integral first, holding \(x\) and \(y\) fixed. What is left is an ordinary double integral over the shadow \(D\).

Then \(D\) itself is type I or type II, exactly as in double integrals, which fixes the last two pairs of limits. For a type I shadow you get the order \(dz\,dy\,dx\):

\(\displaystyle\iiint_E f\,dV=\int_a^b\!\!\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)}\!\!\int_{u_1(x,y)}^{u_2(x,y)} f(x,y,z)\,dz\,dy\,dx\)Read it inside out: \(z\) may depend on \(x\) and \(y\); \(y\) may depend on \(x\); \(x\) is trapped between two numbers.

The rule that never breaks. The outermost limits are always constants. Each limit may only use variables that are still to be integrated, that is, variables sitting further out.

A solid can be projected onto any of the three coordinate planes, giving a type 2 description (project onto the \(yz\)-plane, integrate \(x\) first) or a type 3 description (project onto the \(xz\)-plane, integrate \(y\) first). Pick whichever projection has the simplest shadow and whichever inner integral you can actually do.

\(\displaystyle V(E)=\iiint_E 1\,dV\qquad\qquad m=\iiint_E \rho(x,y,z)\,dV\)Volume is the triple integral of 1. Mass is the triple integral of the density. Same limits, different integrand.

Step-by-step: setting up \(\iiint_E z\,dV\) over a tetrahedron

\(E\) is bounded by the plane \(x+y+z=a\) and the three coordinate planes. Order: \(dz\,dy\,dx\).

    Every type 1 setup is these same four moves: ceiling minus floor for \(z\), set \(z\) to the floor to find the shadow, slice the shadow for \(y\), then read off the two numbers for \(x\).

    Explore: slice a solid

    Drag to rotate, scroll to zoom. The orange polygon is the cross-section of the solid at the chosen \(x\).

    Sweep \(x\) from one end to the other and watch the orange slice. Its left and right edges are the \(y\) limits, its bottom and top edges are the \(z\) limits, and the two \(x\) values where the slice vanishes are the \(x\) limits. That is the whole setup, drawn.

    Which order. Integrating \(z\) first works whenever the solid has a single floor surface and a single ceiling surface. If a vertical line enters and leaves the solid more than once, split the solid, or project onto a different plane instead.
    Constants on the outside. An answer like \(\int_0^{1-y}\!\int_0^1 \cdots\,dx\,dy\) is wrong no matter what the solid is: the outer limits contain \(y\), the very variable being integrated there. If a variable survives to the end, you have made a mistake.

    Cylindrical coordinates

    Polar coordinates in the \(xy\)-plane, with \(z\) left alone. Use them whenever the solid has an axis of symmetry along \(z\).

    \(x=r\cos\theta\qquad y=r\sin\theta\qquad z=z\)
    \(r^2=x^2+y^2\qquad \tan\theta=\dfrac{y}{x}\qquad z=z\)Left: cylindrical to rectangular, always safe. Right: rectangular to cylindrical. Take \(r\ge 0\) and read \(\theta\) from the quadrant of \((x,y)\).

    The point \((r,\theta,z)\) is "go out a distance \(r\) from the \(z\)-axis in the direction \(\theta\), then rise to height \(z\)". Nothing about \(z\) changes.

    \(dV=r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta\)The extra \(r\) is the polar area element \(r\,dr\,d\theta\) with \(dz\) along for the ride. A little box has sides \(dr\), \(r\,d\theta\), and \(dz\).
    \(\displaystyle\iiint_E f\,dV=\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\!\!\int_{h_1(\theta)}^{h_2(\theta)}\!\!\int_{u_1(r,\theta)}^{u_2(r,\theta)} f(r\cos\theta,\,r\sin\theta,\,z)\;r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta\)The same three-layer structure as before, with \(r,\theta\) replacing \(x,y\) and one extra factor of \(r\).

    The surfaces you should recognize on sight

    EquationSurfaceWhy
    \(r=a\)circular cylinder of radius \(a\)every point at distance \(a\) from the \(z\)-axis
    \(\theta=c\)half-plane hinged on the \(z\)-axisone fixed direction, all \(r\ge0\) and all \(z\)
    \(z=r\)coneheight equals distance from the axis, a \(45^\circ\) slope
    \(z=r^2\)paraboloid\(z=x^2+y^2\) written in polar
    \(z=c\)horizontal plane\(r,\theta\) free
    \(r^2+z^2=a^2\)sphere of radius \(a\)\(x^2+y^2+z^2=a^2\)
    \(r=2a\cos\theta\)off-center cylindermultiply by \(r\): \(x^2+y^2=2ax\), the circle \((x-a)^2+y^2=a^2\) swept vertically

    Explore: the point \((r,\theta,z)\)

    Orange: the radius \(r\) in the \(xy\)-plane. Blue: the angle \(\theta\), swept from the positive \(x\)-axis. Green: the rise \(z\).

    Set \(\theta\) and then sweep \(r\): the point traces a ray. Set \(r\) and sweep \(\theta\): a circle. Turn on a preset solid and notice that each one is described by holding one of \(r,\theta,z\) between constants, which is exactly why the limits come out clean.

    Do the \(z\) integral first, then polar. A cylindrical setup is a type 1 setup whose shadow \(D\) you describe in polar coordinates. Find the floor and ceiling in terms of \(r\) and \(\theta\), then sweep the shadow.
    Two standing mistakes. First, the missing \(r\): \(dV=r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta\), not \(dz\,dr\,d\theta\). Second, the quadrant: \(\tan\theta=y/x\) has two solutions in \([0,2\pi)\) and your calculator only ever returns one of them. For \((-1,-1)\) the answer is \(5\pi/4\), not \(\pi/4\).

    Spherical coordinates

    Locate a point by how far out it is and by two angles. Balls, cones through the origin, and anything built from \(x^2+y^2+z^2\) become trivial.

    The three coordinates are \(\rho\ge 0\), the distance from the origin; \(\theta\), the same angle as in cylindrical coordinates, measured in the \(xy\)-plane from the positive \(x\)-axis; and \(\phi\), the angle down from the positive \(z\)-axis, with \(0\le\phi\le\pi\).

    \(x=\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta\qquad y=\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta\qquad z=\rho\cos\phi\)
    \(\rho^2=x^2+y^2+z^2\)Read them as two steps. First drop to the \(xy\)-plane: the shadow has radius \(r=\rho\sin\phi\). Then \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\) as always, and the height is \(z=\rho\cos\phi\).

    So cylindrical and spherical are linked by \(r=\rho\sin\phi\) and \(z=\rho\cos\phi\), which is just polar coordinates in the \(rz\)-half-plane.

    \(dV=\rho^2\sin\phi\;d\rho\,d\theta\,d\phi\)The little box has sides \(d\rho\) outward, \(\rho\,d\phi\) along a meridian, and \(\rho\sin\phi\,d\theta\) along a parallel of latitude, because that circle has radius \(\rho\sin\phi\). Multiply: \(\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\).
    \(\displaystyle\iiint_E f\,dV=\int_{c}^{d}\!\!\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\!\!\int_{g_1(\theta,\phi)}^{g_2(\theta,\phi)} f\;\rho^2\sin\phi\;d\rho\,d\theta\,d\phi\)For a ball, a hemisphere, a shell, or an ice-cream cone every one of these six limits is a constant.

    The surfaces you should recognize on sight

    EquationSurfaceWhy
    \(\rho=a\)sphere of radius \(a\) centered at the originconstant distance from the origin
    \(\phi=c\), \(c\ne\pi/2\)cone with vertex at the originconstant angle from the \(z\)-axis; \(\phi=\pi/4\) is \(z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\)
    \(\phi=\pi/2\)the \(xy\)-planethe degenerate cone: the whole plane \(z=0\)
    \(\theta=c\)half-planesame as in cylindrical
    \(\rho=2a\cos\phi\)sphere of radius \(a\) through the originmultiply by \(\rho\): \(x^2+y^2+z^2=2az\), so \(x^2+y^2+(z-a)^2=a^2\)
    \(\rho\sin\phi=a\)circular cylinder \(r=a\)\(\rho\sin\phi\) is exactly the cylindrical \(r\)
    \(\rho\cos\phi=c\)horizontal plane \(z=c\)\(\rho\cos\phi\) is exactly \(z\)

    Explore: the point \((\rho,\theta,\phi)\) and the volume element

    Orange: the segment of length \(\rho\) from the origin. Blue: \(\phi\), swept down from the \(z\)-axis. Green: \(\theta\), swept in the \(xy\)-plane. The shaded patch is one curved face of the volume element.

    Hold \(\rho\) and \(\theta\) fixed and slide \(\phi\) from \(0\) to \(\pi\): the point runs down a meridian from the north pole to the south pole. Now watch the patch: near the poles it shrinks to nothing, and that shrinking is the \(\sin\phi\) in \(dV\). Near \(\phi=\pi/2\) the patch is at its widest.

    The same \(\theta\). \(\theta\) means exactly the same thing in cylindrical and in spherical coordinates: the angle in the \(xy\)-plane, measured from the positive \(x\)-axis. Everything you know about finding \(\theta\), including the quadrant trap, carries over unchanged.
    \(\phi\) is measured from the positive \(z\)-axis, not from the \(xy\)-plane. The north pole is \(\phi=0\), the equator is \(\phi=\pi/2\), the south pole is \(\phi=\pi\). An upper hemisphere is \(0\le\phi\le\pi/2\), never \(0\le\phi\le\pi\) with \(z\ge 0\) tacked on.
    Never drop \(\rho^2\sin\phi\). It is the single most common lost point on this material. Write \(dV=\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\theta\,d\phi\) first, before you write a single limit, and the rest of the problem is arithmetic.

    Chapter 4 · Stewart 15.6 to 15.8

    Choosing coordinates

    Three systems, one decision. Read the boundary first, the integrand second, then commit.

    UseWhen the solid isGive-aways in the integrand\(dV\)
    rectangulara box, a tetrahedron, anything cut out by planespolynomials in \(x,y,z\) with no \(x^2+y^2\) grouping\(dx\,dy\,dz\)
    cylindricala cylinder, a cone, a paraboloid, anything with an axis of symmetry along \(z\)\(x^2+y^2\) appears, with \(z\) sitting separately\(r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta\)
    sphericala ball, a hemisphere, a shell, a cone through the origin, an ice-cream cone\(x^2+y^2+z^2\) appears as a block, or \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}\)\(\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\theta\,d\phi\)

    The boundary surfaces usually decide it. A sphere plus a cone through the origin is spherical, every time. A cylinder plus two planes is cylindrical. Planes only is rectangular. The tie-breaker, when a solid could go either way, is which choice makes all six limits constants.

    The factors \(r\) and \(\rho^2\sin\phi\) are Jacobians: they measure how much a little box in the new coordinates gets stretched when it is mapped into space. That is why they cannot be left out, and why they carry a length and a length-squared respectively.

    Explore: each system's natural grid

    Rectangular: three families of flat sheets \(x=c\), \(y=c\), \(z=c\). The natural solid is a box.

    Cylindrical: nested cylinders \(r=c\), half-planes \(\theta=c\), horizontal planes \(z=c\). The natural solid is a can.

    Spherical: nested spheres \(\rho=c\), cones \(\phi=c\) (the parallels), half-planes \(\theta=c\) (the meridians). The natural solid is a ball.

    Drag any of the three. Ask yourself, for each solid you meet in the drill: can I build its boundary out of these sheets alone? If yes, every limit will be a constant and the integral will be short.

    Constant limits are the scoreboard. The right coordinate system is usually the one in which the most limits are plain numbers. A ball in rectangular coordinates needs \(\sqrt{a^2-x^2-y^2}\) twice; in spherical it needs \(0\le\rho\le a\), \(0\le\phi\le\pi\), \(0\le\theta\le 2\pi\), and nothing else.
    Do not mix the systems up in the integrand. \(x^2+y^2+z^2\) is \(\rho^2\) in spherical but \(r^2+z^2\) in cylindrical, not \(r^2\). And \(x^2+y^2\) is \(r^2\) in cylindrical but \(\rho^2\sin^2\phi\) in spherical, not \(\rho^2\). Convert the integrand deliberately, one term at a time.
