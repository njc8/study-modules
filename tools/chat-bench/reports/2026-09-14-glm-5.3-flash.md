## The key idea

For limits at a point in $\mathbb{R}^2$, you must approach along **every** path. So:
- **To show a limit does not exist:** find two paths giving different values. (One path is never enough to *prove* existence.)
- **To show a limit exists:** use the Squeeze Theorem — trap $|f(x,y)|$ between $0$ and something that $\to 0$.

---

### (a) $\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{xy}{x^2+y^2}$

Top and bottom are both degree 2, so paths have a chance of differing. Try straight lines $y = mx$:

\[
\frac{x(mx)}{x^2 + m^2x^2} = \frac{m}{1+m^2}\quad (x \neq 0).
\]

The result depends on the slope $m$:
- Along $y = 0$: the limit is $0$.
- Along $y = x$ ($m=1$): the limit is $\tfrac{1}{2}$.

Two paths, two different values $\Rightarrow$ **the limit does not exist.**

**Common mistake:** checking only the two coordinate axes. Both axes give $0$ here, which might tempt you to conclude the limit is $0$. Axes are just two of infinitely many paths.

---

### (b) $\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2}$

Now the numerator is degree 3, one degree higher than the denominator — that extra factor of $x$ should crush the function to $0$. Prove it with a squeeze.

Since $\dfrac{x^2}{x^2+y^2} \le 1$ (the denominator is at least as big as $x^2$),

\[
\left|\frac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2}\right| = \frac{x^2}{x^2+y^2}\,|y| \le |y| \;\longrightarrow\; 0.
\]

By the Squeeze Theorem, **the limit is $0$.**

**Sanity check (polar coordinates):** with $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$,

\[
\frac{r^2\cos^2\theta \cdot r\sin\theta}{r^2} = r\cos^2\theta\sin\theta,
\qquad |\,\cdot\,| \le r \to 0
\]

uniformly in $\theta$ — every approach path is covered. $\checkmark$

**Common mistake:** plugging in $y = mx$, getting $\dfrac{mx}{1+m^2} \to 0$ for **every** $m$, and declaring the limit exists. That only checks straight-line paths; paths like $y = x^2$ can still disagree. Path tests can *disprove* a limit, never prove one — you need a bound (squeeze) or a substitution where $r \to 0$ controls everything.

\[
\nabla f = \langle y, x \rangle, \qquad \nabla g = \langle 2x, 2y \rangle

**The problem:** Find the absolute max and min of \(f(x,y)=x^2+y^2-2x\) on the closed disk \(x^2+y^2\le 4\).

**The idea:** Extreme Value Theorem on a compact (closed, bounded) region. Since \(f\) is continuous on the closed disk, absolute extrema exist and can only occur at:
1. critical points in the interior, or
2. extrema of \(f\) restricted to the boundary circle.

## Step 1: Interior critical points

Solve \(\nabla f = \mathbf{0}\):
\[
f_x = 2x-2 = 0 \implies x=1, \qquad f_y = 2y = 0 \implies y=0.
\]
The point \((1,0)\) satisfies \(1^2+0^2 = 1 \le 4\), so it lies inside the disk. Value:
\[
f(1,0) = 1+0-2 = -1.
\]

## Step 2: Boundary \(x^2+y^2=4\)

On the circle, \(x^2+y^2 = 4\), so the function *simplifies*:
\[
f = 4 - 2x.
\]
This depends only on \(x\), and on the disk \(x\) ranges over \([-2,2]\). So:
- minimized at \(x=2\): \(f(2,0) = 4-4 = 0\),
- maximized at \(x=-2\): \(f(-2,0) = 4+4 = 8\).

(Equivalently, parametrize \(x=2\cos t,\ y=2\sin t\) and optimize \(4-4\cos t\), or use Lagrange multipliers \(\nabla f = \lambda\langle 2x,2y\rangle\). All give the same two points.)

## Step 3: Compare candidates

| Point | \(f\) value |
|---|---|
| \((1,0)\) interior | \(-1\) |
| \((2,0)\) boundary | \(0\) |
| \((-2,0)\) boundary | \(8\) |

\[
\boxed{\text{Absolute min} = -1 \text{ at } (1,0), \qquad \text{Absolute max} = 8 \text{ at } (-2,0)}
\]

## Sanity check

Complete the square:
\[
f = (x-1)^2 + y^2 - 1.
\]
This is (squared distance from \((1,0)\)) minus 1. So \(f \ge -1\) everywhere, with equality at \((1,0)\) — confirming the min. The farthest point in the disk from \((1,0)\) is \((-2,0)\), at distance 3, giving \(f = 9-1 = 8\) — confirming the max.

**Common mistakes here:**
- Checking only the boundary (or only the critical point) — you need both, then compare.
- Treating the boundary value \(f(2,0)=0\) as the minimum. It's the boundary minimum, but the interior point beats it.
- Sign slip: \(f(1,0) = 1 - 2 = -1\), not \(+1\).
