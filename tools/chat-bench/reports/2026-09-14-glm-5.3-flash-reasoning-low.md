Good question — the "why" is actually short once you see what the gradient is *made of*. Let me build it up.

## 1. What you actually want to know

"Steepest ascent" means: among all unit directions $\mathbf{u}$ you could step in, which one gives the biggest *rate of change* of $f$? The rate of change in direction $\mathbf{u}$ is the directional derivative:

\[
D_{\mathbf{u}} f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}
\]

This formula already tells you the answer — you just have to interpret the dot product.

## 2. The dot product is "alignment"

For unit vectors,

\[
\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u} = \|\nabla f\| \cos\theta
\]

where $\theta$ is the angle between them. So the rate of change is really just one number, $\|\nabla f\|$, scaled by **how well you line up with the gradient**:

- $\theta = 0$: you get $\|\nabla f\|$, the maximum — full alignment.
- $\theta = 90^\circ$: you get $0$ — no change. That's why the gradient is perpendicular to level curves.
- $\theta = 180^\circ$: you get $-\|\nabla f\|$ — steepest *descent*.

So there's no separate fact "the gradient points uphill." It's built into the dot product: $\nabla f$ is by definition the vector that, when you measure rates of change, makes $\mathbf{u} = \frac{\nabla f}{\|\nabla f\|}$ score highest.

## 3. Concrete intuition: $f$ as temperature

Suppose you're in a hot room and temperature is $f(x,y)$. Your temperature change after a small step $\Delta \mathbf{x} = \langle \Delta x, \Delta y\rangle$ is approximately

\[
\Delta f \approx f_x \Delta x + f_y \Delta y = \nabla f \cdot \Delta \mathbf{x}.
\]

Each partial $f_x$, $f_y$ tells you the gain per meter of moving in the $x$- or $y$-direction. Moving diagonally is just buying a mix of both. So the dot product is you "spending your step length" across the two available directions. To maximize the return on a fixed step length, put your whole budget into the direction whose per-meter payoff $\langle f_x, f_y\rangle$ is steepest — i.e., step in the direction of $\nabla f$ itself.

Think of it like: if walking east warms you by $3^\circ$ per meter and walking north by $4^\circ$ per meter, walking in direction $\langle 3,4\rangle$ (properly normalized) beats every other direction — any deviation is "wasting" step length on a direction with smaller payoff.

## 4. The usual mistake

Students compute $D_\mathbf{u} f$ with a **non-unit** $\mathbf{u}$, which lets them artificially inflate the rate. Steepest ascent only makes sense when comparing directions of the *same step length* — normalize first.

## 5. Sanity check

If $\nabla f = \mathbf{0}$, the formula says $D_\mathbf{u} f = 0$ in every direction — a flat spot. Consistent: at a peak, no direction is "uphill." That's exactly why setting $\nabla f = 0$ finds critical points.

## What the test actually says

The second derivative test for $f(x,y)$ uses

$$D = f_{xx}f_{yy} - f_{xy}^2.$$

At a critical point:
- $D > 0$ and $f_{xx} > 0$: local min
- $D > 0$ and $f_{xx} < 0$: local max
- $D < 0$: saddle point
- $D = 0$: **inconclusive** — the test tells you nothing

So $D = 0$ means the test fails, not that no extremum exists. All three possibilities can actually happen:

## Examples with $D = 0$

**Local min:** $f(x,y) = x^4 + y^4$. At $(0,0)$, all second derivatives are $0$, so $D = 0$. But $f \ge 0$ with equality only at the origin — clearly a local (and global) min.

**Saddle:** $f(x,y) = x^4 - y^4$. Again $D = 0$ at the origin, but along the $x$-axis it increases and along the $y$-axis it decreases — a saddle.

**Neither (flat in a direction):** $f(x,y) = x^4 + y^3$. Here $D = 0$, and it's neither: no max (it grows along $+y$), and no min since $y^3 < 0$ for $y < 0$.

Go back to the definition and test the sign of $f$ near the point directly, usually along curves:

1. Look at $f$ along lines through the point: if two lines give opposite signs, it's a saddle.
2. If lines don't settle it, try parabolas like $y = kx^2$ (the monkey saddle $f = x^3 - 3xy^2$ is the classic case where lines all look flat).
3. Often a single-variable Taylor expansion along a path resolves it.
