**Idea:** For a vector curve, arc length uses the *speed*:
\[
L=\int_a^b |\mathbf r'(t)|\,dt,

and curvature is

## 1. Arc length

\[
\mathbf r(t)=\langle \cos t,\sin t,t\rangle
\]
so
\[
\mathbf r'(t)=\langle -\sin t,\cos t,1\rangle.
\]

The speed is
\[
|\mathbf r'(t)|
=\sqrt{(-\sin t)^2+(\cos t)^2+1^2}
=\sqrt{\sin^2t+\cos^2t+1}
=\sqrt2.
\]

Thus
\[
L=\int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt2\,dt

\[
\sqrt{(2\pi)^2+(2\pi)^2}=2\sqrt2\,\pi.

## 2. Curvature

Using \(\mathbf r''(t)=\langle -\cos t,-\sin t,0\rangle\),

\[
\mathbf r'(t)\times \mathbf r''(t)
=
\begin{vmatrix}
\mathbf i&\mathbf j&\mathbf k\\
-\sin t&\cos t&1\\
-\cos t&-\sin t&0
\end{vmatrix}
=\langle \sin t,-\cos t,1\rangle.
\]

Therefore
\[
|\mathbf r'\times \mathbf r''|
=\sqrt{\sin^2t+\cos^2t+1}
=\sqrt2.
\]

Since \(|\mathbf r'|=\sqrt2\),
\[
\kappa
=\frac{|\mathbf r'\times \mathbf r''|}{|\mathbf r'|^3}
=\frac{\sqrt2}{(\sqrt2)^3}
=\frac{\sqrt2}{2\sqrt2}
=\boxed{\frac12}.
\]

So the curvature is constant:
\[
\boxed{\kappa(t)=\frac12 \quad \text{for all } t.}
\]

\[
\mathbf T(t)=\frac{\mathbf r'(t)}{|\mathbf r'(t)|}

\mathbf T'(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\langle -\cos t,-\sin t,0\rangle,

## (a) \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{xy}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\) — does not exist

Two paths:

- Along the \(x\)-axis (\(y=0\), \(x\neq 0\)): \(f=\dfrac{0}{x^{2}}=0\), so the limit along this path is \(0\).
- Along the line \(y=x\) (\(x\neq 0\)): \(f=\dfrac{x^{2}}{x^{2}+x^{2}}=\dfrac{1}{2}\), so the limit along this path is \(\tfrac12\).

Since \(0\neq\tfrac12\), the limit **does not exist**.

Polar coordinates show *why*: with \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta\),

\[
f=\frac{r^{2}\cos\theta\sin\theta}{r^{2}}=\cos\theta\sin\theta=\tfrac12\sin 2\theta .
\]

The \(r\) cancels completely — every ray gives its own constant, so the "limit" would have to equal all of \(\bigl[-\tfrac12,\tfrac12\bigr]\) at once. The surface in the graph is a fan of rays at different heights: that is exactly what a failed limit looks like.

## (b) \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^{2}y}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\) — exists and equals \(0\)

Here we must *prove* it, and one path (or three) proves nothing. Squeeze instead. Write

\[
\left|\frac{x^{2}y}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\right|=|y|\cdot\underbrace{\frac{x^{2}}{x^{2}+y^{2}}}_{\le\, 1}\;\le\; |y| .
\]

Because \(|y|\le\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}=r\to 0\), the squeeze theorem gives

\[
\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{x^{2}y}{x^{2}+y^{2}}=0 .
\]

Same answer in polar form: \(\;f=r\cos^{2}\theta\sin\theta\), and \(|f|\le r\to 0\) **uniformly in \(\theta\)** (the \(\theta\)-part is bounded by 1). The difference from part (a): numerator degree \(3\) beats denominator degree \(2\), leaving an extra factor of \(r\) that kills the angular dependence.

**Sanity checks.** Along \(y=x\): \(f=\frac{x^{3}}{2x^{2}}=\frac{x}{2}\to 0\), consistent with \(0\) but not sufficient. Along \(x=0\): \(f=0\). Also \(f\) is odd in \(y\), so approaching from \(y<0\) gives negatives tending to \(0\) — consistent with the limit being \(0\), not a nonzero value.

**The mistake students usually make here:** in (b), testing \(y=x\), \(y=x^{2}\), \(y=2x\) and seeing \(0\) each time, then declaring the limit is \(0\). Coincidence on finitely many paths is not proof; you must bound \(|f - L|\) by something that goes to \(0\) independently of direction. Conversely in (a), students sometimes compute \(\cos\theta\sin\theta\) and stop — the point is that this depends on \(\theta\), so no single limit exists.

## 1. What a directional derivative actually is

A partial derivative answers one question: *if I step in the \(x\)-direction at unit speed, how fast does \(f\) change?* Same for \(y\). But you could step in any direction.

Take a unit vector \(u\) and a step of size \(h\) in that direction. Separating that step into its components, the step is \((h\cos\theta,\, h\sin\theta)\). The linearization (tangent plane) of \(f\) then gives

\[ f(P + hu) \approx f(P) + h\big(f_x\cos\theta + f_y\sin\theta\big). \]

So the rate of change in direction \(u\) is

\[ D_u f = f_x\cos\theta + f_y\sin\theta = \langle f_x, f_y\rangle\cdot u = \nabla f \cdot u. \]

That's the whole content: **the gradient is the vector whose dot product with any direction gives the rate of change in that direction.** Check it on the coordinates directions: \(u=(1,0)\) gives \(f_x\) ✓, \(u=(0,1)\) gives \(f_y\) ✓. So \(\nabla f\) is forced — it's the only vector that reproduces all the directional derivatives.

## 2. Why the dot product maximizes when aligned

With \(u\) a unit vector, \(\nabla f\cdot u = |\nabla f|\,|u|\cos\varphi = |\nabla f|\cos\varphi\), where \(\varphi\) is the angle between them.

- \(\varphi = 0\): \(D_u f = |\nabla f|\), the largest possible value. → steepest ascent.
- \(\varphi = \pi/2\): \(D_u f = 0\). Moving that way doesn't change \(f\) — you're sliding along a level curve.
- \(\varphi = \pi\): \(D_u f = -|\nabla f|\), steepest descent.

This is not a theorem about gradients, it's a fact about dot products: \(\cos\varphi \le 1\) with equality only at \(\varphi=0\). Slide the angle in the graph and watch the readout: the value of \(D_u f\) is literally \(2.154\cos\varphi\), a cosine curve.

## 3. The picture that makes it stick

Look at the purple level curves. Where \(f\) doesn't change, \(D_u f = 0\), i.e. \(u \perp \nabla f\). So the gradient is **perpendicular to the level curve**, and "perpendicular to the contour" is exactly the direction that leaves the contour fastest. Walking along a contour is wasted effort; walking across it is progress. The gradient points straight across, toward the next contour up.

The purple arrow field shows this globally: every arrow is \(\nabla f\) evaluated at that point, and every arrow crosses its level curve at a right angle, pointing toward larger \(f\) (larger \(0.4x^2+y^2\)).

## 4. Mistakes students usually make here

- **Forgetting \(u\) must be a unit vector.** If you plug a non-unit \(v\) into \(\nabla f\cdot v\), you get a rate scaled by \(|v|\) — meaningless as a slope. Normalize first.
- **"The gradient points uphill on the surface."** \(\nabla f\) is a vector in the \(xy\)-plane, not on the graph in 3D. It tells you which way to walk in the domain; the steepness is \(|\nabla f|\).
- **Assuming \(\nabla f\) points away from the origin / always along \((x,y)\).** That's an accident of \(f=x^2+y^2\), which is radially symmetric. For \(f=xy\), \(\nabla f = (y,x)\), which is not radial.
- **Confusing "gradient is zero" with "minimum."** \(\nabla f = 0\) means the tangent plane is horizontal: max, min, or saddle. The gradient tells you nothing at that point.

## 5. Cheap check

For \(f=x^2+y^2\) at \((1,2)\): \(\nabla f = (2,4)\). The level curve through \((1,2)\) is \(x^2+y^2=5\), tangent line there is \(x+2y=5\), direction \((-2,1)\). ## The statement of the test (Stewart 14.7)

For a critical point \((a,b)\) with continuous second partials, set
\[ D = f_{xx}f_{yy} - (f_{xy})^2. \]
Then \(D>0,\ f_{xx}>0\) gives a local min; \(D>0,\ f_{xx}<0\) gives a local max; \(D<0\) gives a saddle point. **\(D=0\): no conclusion.**

## Why "no conclusion" is genuinely the case

Three functions, all with \((0,0)\) as a critical point and all with \(D=0\) there:

- \(f=x^4+y^4\): \(f_x=4x^3,\ f_y=4y^3\), so \((0,0)\) is critical. \(f_{xx}=12x^2\), \(f_{yy}=12y^2\), \(f_{xy}=0\), hence \(D=-144x^2y^2\), which is \(0\) at the origin. Yet \(f\ge 0 = f(0,0)\) everywhere, so it is a **local minimum**.
- \(f=-(x^4+y^4)\): same computation, \(D=0\), but now \(f\le 0\), a **local maximum**.
- \(f=x^4-y^4\): again \(D=0\) at the origin, but \(f(x,0)=x^4>0\) while \(f(0,y)=-y^4<0\). Sign changes in every neighborhood, so it is a **saddle point**.

Same \(D=0\), all three outcomes. That is exactly why the test is silent.

## What to do when you hit \(D=0\)

Drop back to the definition: compare \(f\) at the point with \(f\) at nearby points.

- Test paths through the point: the axes, the lines \(y=x\), \(y=-x\), \(y=mx\), or a curve like \(y=x^2\). Two paths with different limiting behavior \(\Rightarrow\) saddle.
- Use algebra: complete the square, factor, or bound, e.g. \(x^4+y^4 = (x^2)^2+(y^2)^2 \ge 0\) settles the first example instantly.

## Two mistakes to avoid

1. Writing "\(D=0\), so no local extremum." Wrong — it may well be one. This loses points on exams and causes students to discard a correct answer in WeBWorK.
2. Forgetting the hypothesis that \((a,b)\) is already a critical point. The test is only ever applied where \(f_x=f_y=0\) (or where a partial fails to exist, in which case the test does not apply at all).

Sanity check on your own work: if you conclude \(D=0\) and then claim a saddle, your justification must be a path argument, not the test — the test can never produce a saddle verdict when \(D=0\).
