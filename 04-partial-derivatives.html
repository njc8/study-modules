Functions of several variables

One input is a point in the plane, one output is a number, and the graph is a surface. This module teaches you to read that surface from a contour map, to test limits along paths, to differentiate one variable at a time, and to flatten the surface into a tangent plane.

How to use this

Work through the chapters in order. Each one teaches an idea, gives you something to drag, and then drills you with fresh random problems until you hit the goal. Progress is saved in this browser.

Answers are typed as plain text. Expressions look like 2xy + 3x^2, y*e^(xy), cos(x+5z), 1/(2sqrt(x+y)); implicit multiplication (2xy, x^2y) works, and e^(xy) and exp(xy) are the same thing. Equations look like z = 2x + 3y - 4, and any equivalent form is accepted. Numbers can be exact: 3/5, 2e^2, sqrt(13), pi/2. Press Enter to check, and Enter again for a new question.

What you will be able to do at the end

  • Evaluate \(f(x,y)\), and describe its domain as a region of the plane.
  • Sketch and read level curves and contour maps, and match a contour map to its surface.
  • Show a limit does not exist with two paths, and evaluate the limits that do exist.
  • Compute \(f_x\), \(f_y\), \(f_z\), and the second partials, and use Clairaut's theorem.
  • Write the tangent plane and the linearization at a point, and use them to approximate.

Chapter 1 · Stewart 14.1

Functions of two variables

Feed in a point of the plane, get out a number. The picture is a surface floating over the \(xy\)-plane, and a contour map is its topographic map.

A function of two variables \(f\) assigns to each ordered pair \((x,y)\) in a set \(D\) a single number \(f(x,y)\). The set \(D\) is the domain, a region of the \(xy\)-plane, and the set of output values is the range. We often write \(z=f(x,y)\) and call \(x\) and \(y\) the independent variables and \(z\) the dependent variable.

Domain: a region, not an interval

Unless a domain is stated, it is every \((x,y)\) for which the formula makes sense. Three rules cover almost every problem:

\(\sqrt{\;\cdot\;}\) needs its argument \(\ge 0\)  ·  \(\ln(\;\cdot\;)\) needs its argument \(>0\)  ·  a denominator must be \(\ne 0\)Translate each condition into an inequality in \(x\) and \(y\), then describe the region: a half-plane, a disk, the outside of a circle, the plane minus a line.

For example \(f(x,y)=\sqrt{9-x^2-y^2}\) needs \(9-x^2-y^2\ge 0\), so the domain is the closed disk \(x^2+y^2\le 9\). And \(g(x,y)=\ln(y-x)\) needs \(y-x>0\), the open half-plane above the line \(y=x\).

Graph and level curves

The graph of \(f\) is the surface \(z=f(x,y)\) in space: the point \((x,y)\) in the plane is lifted to height \(f(x,y)\). Surfaces are hard to draw, so we slice them. Cut the surface with the horizontal plane \(z=k\) and drop the slice straight down into the \(xy\)-plane. What you get is a level curve:

\(f(x,y)=k\)The level curve of value \(k\): the set of points in the plane where \(f\) equals \(k\). Several of them together make a contour map.

A contour map tells you everything about the shape of the surface. Where the level curves crowd together the surface is steep, because a small step in the plane changes \(f\) a lot. Where they spread apart the surface is nearly flat. Closed loops shrinking toward a point mark a peak or a pit; curves that cross themselves in an X mark a saddle.

Slide \(k\) up through the paraboloid and watch the orange circle grow, then look at the map: equal steps in \(k\) give rings that get closer together, which says the bowl gets steeper as you climb. Switch to \(x^2-y^2\) and pass \(k\) through 0 to see the hyperbolas flip from one pair to the other. Then try \(\sqrt{x+y}\): half the map is blank because the domain is only the half-plane \(x+y\ge 0\).

Spacing is slope. Level curves drawn for equally spaced values of \(k\) are close together where the surface is steep and far apart where it is flat. Evenly spaced parallel lines mean the graph is a plane.
A level curve lives in the plane. The level curve of \(f(x,y)=x^2+y^2\) at \(k=9\) is the circle \(x^2+y^2=9\), an equation in \(x\) and \(y\) only. Writing \(z=9\) describes the horizontal plane you cut with, not the curve. Keep the two apart.

Three variables: level surfaces

A function \(f(x,y,z)\) of three variables cannot be graphed (you would need four dimensions), so instead we look at its level surfaces \(f(x,y,z)=k\). For \(f(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2\) the level surfaces are concentric spheres of radius \(\sqrt{k}\); for \(f(x,y,z)=x+2y+3z\) they are parallel planes. Same idea, one dimension up.

Chapter 2 · Stewart 14.2

Limits and continuity

In one variable a point has two sides. In two variables it has infinitely many directions, and the limit must come out the same along every one of them.

Saying \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)}f(x,y)=L\) means \(f(x,y)\) gets arbitrarily close to \(L\) for every point \((x,y)\) close enough to \((a,b)\) - no matter how the point approaches. That "no matter how" is the whole difficulty, and it gives a test that is easy to use:

If \(f\to L_1\) along one path to \((a,b)\) and \(f\to L_2\ne L_1\) along another, then \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)}f(x,y)\) does not exist.The two-path test. It can only ever prove a limit fails. It can never prove one exists.

The standard paths to try, in this order: \(y=0\) (along the \(x\)-axis), \(x=0\) (along the \(y\)-axis), \(y=mx\) (any line through the origin), and \(y=x^2\) or \(x=y^2\) (a parabola). Substitute the path into \(f\), simplify to a function of one variable, and take the one-variable limit. If the answer along \(y=mx\) still contains \(m\), different lines give different values and the limit does not exist.

Explore: approach the origin along a path

Heat map of \(f\) near the origin: orange is positive, blue is negative, white is near zero. Thin gray curves are level curves. The orange line is \(y=mx\); the dashed purple curve, when there is one, is the path that breaks the limit.

Turn the slope \(m\) and watch the value of \(f\) along that line. For the first two functions the value changes with \(m\), so those limits fail on lines alone. For \(x^2y/(x^4+y^2)\) every line gives 0, but the purple parabola gives \(\tfrac12\): lines are not enough. For \(x^2y/(x^2+y^2)\) every path gives 0, and the squeeze below proves it.

When the limit does exist

Two tools. First, continuity: polynomials are continuous everywhere, and a rational function is continuous wherever its denominator is not zero. Sums, products, quotients, and compositions of continuous functions are continuous. So if the point is in the domain, the limit is just the value:

\(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)}f(x,y)=f(a,b)\) whenever \(f\) is continuous at \((a,b)\)Plug in. Most limit problems on an exam are this, disguised.

Second, a squeeze. To show \(\dfrac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2}\to 0\), note \(0\le\dfrac{x^2}{x^2+y^2}\le 1\), so \(\left|\dfrac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2}\right|\le |y|\to 0\). Polar coordinates do the same job mechanically: put \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\), so that \(x^2+y^2=r^2\) and \((x,y)\to(0,0)\) becomes \(r\to 0^+\). If every \(\theta\) disappears or is trapped by a factor of \(r\), the limit exists.

\(\dfrac{x^3}{x^2+y^2}=\dfrac{r^3\cos^3\theta}{r^2}=r\cos^3\theta,\qquad |r\cos^3\theta|\le r\to 0\)Polar substitution: the bound no longer depends on \(\theta\), so the limit is 0 along every path.
Polar trick. Any time you see \(x^2+y^2\) in a denominator, switch to polar. The limit exists and equals \(L\) exactly when the polar expression tends to \(L\) with a bound that depends only on \(r\).
Two paths that agree prove nothing. Getting 0 along \(y=0\) and 0 along \(x=0\) does not make the limit 0; \(xy/(x^2+y^2)\) does exactly that and still has no limit (try \(y=x\)). Matching values along a few paths is evidence to keep going, not a proof.

Chapter 3 · Stewart 14.3

Partial derivatives

Freeze one variable, differentiate the other. Every rule from Calculus I still applies, word for word.

Fix \(y=b\). Then \(g(x)=f(x,b)\) is an ordinary function of one variable, and its derivative at \(x=a\) is the partial derivative of \(f\) with respect to \(x\):

\(f_x(a,b)=\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a+h,b)-f(a,b)}{h}\qquad\qquad f_y(a,b)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a,b+h)-f(a,b)}{h}\)Only one slot moves. The other stays exactly where it was.

In practice you never use the limit. To find \(f_x\), treat \(y\) as a constant and differentiate normally. To find \(f_y\), treat \(x\) as a constant. All the usual notations mean the same thing:

\(f_x=f_x(x,y)=\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}f(x,y)=\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}=D_xf\)The curly \(\partial\) is there only to remind you that other variables are present and being held fixed.

What it means on the surface

The plane \(y=b\) cuts the surface \(z=f(x,y)\) in a curve, the trace \(C_1\). The number \(f_x(a,b)\) is the slope of the tangent line to that trace at the point \((a,b,f(a,b))\), measured in the \(x\) direction. Likewise \(f_y(a,b)\) is the slope of the trace \(C_2\) cut by the plane \(x=a\). Two numbers, two slopes, two directions.

Explore: the two traces and their slopes

Orange: the trace \(y=y_0\) and the tangent vector \(\langle 1,0,f_x\rangle\). Blue: the trace \(x=x_0\) and \(\langle 0,1,f_y\rangle\). Drag to rotate.

On \(x^2+y^2\), park the point at \(x_0=0\): the orange tangent goes flat, because the trace is at the bottom of its parabola and \(f_x=0\). On \(xy\), slide \(y_0\) and watch \(f_x\) follow it exactly - that is \(f_x=y\). Look for the points where both slopes are zero at once.

The other variable is a constant, not a variable. Differentiating with respect to \(x\), \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}(xy^2)=y^2\), not \(2xy\); \(y^2\) is just a number multiplying \(x\). And \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}(y^3)=0\), the same way \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(8)=0\).

Step-by-step: a product and a chain in the same function

Ask yourself at every step which letter is the variable right now. In \(g_x\) the leading \(y\) is a constant factor that just comes along for the ride; in \(g_y\) that same \(y\) is a factor being differentiated, which is why the product rule appears there and not in \(g_x\).

Higher derivatives

\(f_x\) and \(f_y\) are themselves functions of \(x\) and \(y\), so you can differentiate again. There are four second partials:

\((f_x)_x=f_{xx}=\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2}\qquad (f_x)_y=f_{xy}=\dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x}\qquad (f_y)_x=f_{yx}\qquad (f_y)_y=f_{yy}=\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial y^2}\)In the subscript notation you read left to right: \(f_{xy}\) means differentiate by \(x\) first, then by \(y\). In the \(\partial\) notation you read right to left. They mean the same thing.

Clairaut's theorem. If \(f_{xy}\) and \(f_{yx}\) are both continuous near \((a,b)\), then \(f_{xy}(a,b)=f_{yx}(a,b)\). For every function in this course the mixed partials are equal, so compute whichever order is easier - and use the other order as a free check on your algebra.

Mixed partials are a checksum. Compute \(f_{xy}\) from \(f_x\), then \(f_{yx}\) from \(f_y\). If the two disagree, one of your first partials is wrong. This catches more errors than re-reading your work.

Three or more variables

Nothing changes. For \(f(x,y,z)\), \(f_z\) means differentiate with respect to \(z\) while \(x\) and \(y\) are constants. For \(u=5xy/z\): \(u_x=5y/z\), \(u_y=5x/z\), and \(u_z=-5xy/z^2\), since \(1/z=z^{-1}\) has derivative \(-z^{-2}\).

Chapter 4 · Stewart 14.4

Tangent planes and linear approximation

Zoom in far enough on a smooth surface and it looks flat. That flat thing is the tangent plane, and using it instead of \(f\) is linear approximation.

At the point \(P(x_0,y_0,z_0)\) on \(z=f(x,y)\), the two traces have tangent lines with direction vectors \(\langle 1,0,f_x(x_0,y_0)\rangle\) and \(\langle 0,1,f_y(x_0,y_0)\rangle\). The plane containing both of those lines is the tangent plane:

\(z-z_0=f_x(x_0,y_0)\,(x-x_0)+f_y(x_0,y_0)\,(y-y_0)\)Here \(z_0=f(x_0,y_0)\), and \(f_x(x_0,y_0)\), \(f_y(x_0,y_0)\) are numbers: evaluate the partials at the point before you write the plane.

Rearranged, the plane is \(f_x(x-x_0)+f_y(y-y_0)-(z-z_0)=0\), so a normal vector is \(\langle f_x,\,f_y,\,-1\rangle\) (or \(\langle -f_x,\,-f_y,\,1\rangle\), which points up).

Explore: the plane that hugs the surface

The green plane touches the surface at the purple point. Drag to rotate, scroll to zoom.

Look at how good the approximation is half a unit away, then pick a point where the surface is nearly flat and watch the error collapse. On \(x^2-y^2\) at the origin the plane is \(z=0\) and it cuts straight through the surface - a tangent plane is allowed to do that at a saddle.

Linearization

Write the tangent plane as a function instead of an equation and you get the linearization of \(f\) at \((x_0,y_0)\):

\(L(x,y)=f(x_0,y_0)+f_x(x_0,y_0)(x-x_0)+f_y(x_0,y_0)(y-y_0)\)Then \(f(x,y)\approx L(x,y)\) for \((x,y)\) near \((x_0,y_0)\). This is the linear approximation.

This is the exact analogue of \(f(x)\approx f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)\) from one variable: a constant plus a slope times a displacement, once for each variable. A function whose linearization really is a good approximation near the point is called differentiable there, and having \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) continuous near the point is enough to guarantee it.

Differentials

For small changes \(dx=\Delta x\) and \(dy=\Delta y\), the change in height along the tangent plane is the total differential:

\(dz=f_x(x,y)\,dx+f_y(x,y)\,dy=\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}\,dx+\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}\,dy\)\(dz\) estimates the true change \(\Delta z=f(x+\Delta x,y+\Delta y)-f(x,y)\). Each partial says how much that variable contributes per unit of change.
Same object, three costumes. The tangent plane, the linearization, and the differential are one idea written three ways: an equation in \(x,y,z\), a function \(L(x,y)\), and a rule for small changes. If you can write one you can write all three, so learn \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) at the point and the rest is bookkeeping.
Evaluate before you build. The most common error is leaving \(x\) and \(y\) inside the partials: writing \(z-z_0=2x(x-x_0)+2y(y-y_0)\) instead of \(z-z_0=2x_0(x-x_0)+2y_0(y-y_0)\). A plane has constant coefficients. If a variable is still stuck to your coefficient, you have not plugged in the point.
