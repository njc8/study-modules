Double integrals

One integral adds up strips. Two integrals add up columns. Everything in this module is the same move: turn a region into limits, then integrate twice, inside first.

How to use this

Work through the chapters in order. Each one teaches an idea, gives you something to drag or slide, then drills you with fresh random problems until you hit the goal. Progress is saved in this browser.

Answers are exact unless a problem says otherwise. Type numbers like 32/3, pi/2, (e^2-1)/2, 4sqrt(3), 3pi/2, or a decimal when the problem asks for one. Integration limits are typed as expressions in the outer variable: x^2, 2x, sqrt(4-x^2), y/2, 2cos(theta). Press Enter to check, and Enter again for a new question.

the moving piece: a strip, a box, a sample cell the region of integration, the surface the result: a value, a limit, a total boundary curves and marked points

What you will be able to do at the end

  • Read a double integral over a rectangle as a volume, estimate it with the midpoint rule, and evaluate it in either order.
  • Sketch a general region, write its type I and type II limits, and reverse the order of integration.
  • Convert an integral to polar coordinates, including the \(r\) in \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\), and use it for disks, annuli, sectors, and cardioids.
  • Find the mass, the moments, and the center of mass of a lamina, and the area of a piece of a surface.
  • Change variables with a Jacobian to turn an awkward region into a rectangle.

Chapter 1 · Stewart 15.1

Double integrals over rectangles

Chop the rectangle into cells, multiply each cell's area by the height above it, add. That sum is the whole idea.

Let \(f(x,y)\ge 0\) on the rectangle \(R=[a,b]\times[c,d]\). Split \([a,b]\) into \(m\) pieces and \([c,d]\) into \(n\) pieces. Each small cell \(R_{ij}\) has area \(\Delta A=\Delta x\,\Delta y\). Pick a sample point \((x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\) in each cell and build a column of height \(f(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\) over it. The total volume of the columns is a double Riemann sum, and the integral is its limit.

\(\displaystyle\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA=\lim_{m,n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n} f(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\,\Delta A\)Read it as: height times cell area, summed. When \(f\ge 0\) this is the volume under the surface \(z=f(x,y)\) and above \(R\).

The midpoint rule takes the sample point at the center of each cell. It is the estimate you compute by hand:

\(\displaystyle\iint_R f\,dA\approx\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n} f(\bar x_i,\bar y_j)\,\Delta A,\qquad \Delta A=\frac{(b-a)(d-c)}{mn}\)\(\bar x_i\) is the midpoint of the \(i\)th \(x\)-subinterval, \(\bar y_j\) the midpoint of the \(j\)th \(y\)-subinterval. With \(m=n=2\) there are four cells and four midpoints.

Try the saddle \(z=xy+4\), where the cancellation is exact.

Fubini's theorem: integrate twice, inside first

You never compute the limit. You compute an iterated integral instead, and Fubini's theorem says either order gives the same number.

\(\displaystyle\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA=\int_a^b\!\!\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\,dx=\int_c^d\!\!\int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx\,dy\)The inner differential tells you which variable moves first. In \(\int_a^b\!\int_c^d f\,dy\,dx\) you integrate in \(y\) with \(x\) frozen, which leaves a function of \(x\), then you integrate that.

Freezing a variable is the whole trick. While you do the inner integral, the other variable is a constant and can be pulled out front exactly like a 5.

Step-by-step: an iterated integral over a rectangle

Check yourself at step 2: the result of the inner integral must be a function of \(x\) alone. If a \(y\) is still there, you either forgot to substitute a limit or integrated the wrong variable.

The inner answer has no inner variable left. After \(\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\) you substitute \(y=d\) and \(y=c\), so every \(y\) disappears. A \(y\) surviving into the outer integral is the single most common slip in this chapter.

A product of a function of x and a function of y

If the integrand factors as \(f(x,y)=g(x)h(y)\) and the region is a rectangle, the double integral factors too:

\(\displaystyle\iint_R g(x)h(y)\,dA=\left(\int_a^b g(x)\,dx\right)\left(\int_c^d h(y)\,dy\right)\)True only over a rectangle, and only when the integrand actually separates. \(\int_c^d g(x)h(y)\,dy=g(x)\int_c^d h(y)\,dy\) because \(g(x)\) is constant in \(y\), and that constant then comes out of the outer integral.
Pick the easier order. Fubini gives you a free choice over a rectangle. \(\int_0^1\!\int_0^1 x e^{xy}\,dy\,dx\) is a one-line substitution in \(y\) and an unpleasant integration by parts in \(x\). Scan both orders before you start.

Average value

The average value of \(f\) over \(R\) is the total divided by the area, exactly as for one variable:

\(\displaystyle f_{\text{avg}}=\frac{1}{A(R)}\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA,\qquad A(R)=(b-a)(d-c)\)It is the height of the flat box with the same volume over the same base.

Chapter 2 · Stewart 15.2

General regions and order of integration

Real regions are not rectangles. The fix: let the inner limits depend on the outer variable.

A type I region is trapped between two graphs over an interval of \(x\):

\(D=\{(x,y)\;:\;a\le x\le b,\;\; g_1(x)\le y\le g_2(x)\}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \displaystyle\iint_D f\,dA=\int_a^b\!\!\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)} f(x,y)\,dy\,dx\)Read it off a sketch: fix \(x\), slide a vertical line across. It enters the region at \(y=g_1(x)\) and leaves at \(y=g_2(x)\). Those are the inner limits. The outer limits are the smallest and largest \(x\) the region reaches.

A type II region is trapped between two graphs over an interval of \(y\):

\(D=\{(x,y)\;:\;c\le y\le d,\;\; h_1(y)\le x\le h_2(y)\}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \displaystyle\iint_D f\,dA=\int_c^d\!\!\int_{h_1(y)}^{h_2(y)} f(x,y)\,dx\,dy\)Same procedure with a horizontal line: it enters at \(x=h_1(y)\) and leaves at \(x=h_2(y)\).

To find the outer limits, find where the two boundary curves meet. For \(y=x^2\) and \(y=2x\), set \(x^2=2x\) to get \(x=0\) and \(x=2\).

Outer limits are always constants. The inner limits may contain the outer variable; the outer limits may not contain anything. If you end up with \(\int_{0}^{y}\!\int\cdots dx\,dy\) with a \(y\) outside, you have described the region wrong, and the answer would still contain \(y\), which is nonsense for a number.

Reversing the order

An integral handed to you in one order sometimes cannot be done in that order. \(\int_0^1\!\int_x^1 e^{y^2}dy\,dx\) is hopeless as written, because \(e^{y^2}\) has no elementary antiderivative. Reversed it is easy.

  1. Read the limits as a description of the region and sketch it.
  2. Describe the same region the other way: slide a horizontal line instead of a vertical one.
  3. Write the new iterated integral. The integrand does not change at all.
Reversing means redrawing. You cannot swap the limits mechanically. \(\int_0^1\!\int_x^1\) does not become \(\int_x^1\!\int_0^1\). Sketch the region, then read the new limits off the sketch. If the region needs two pieces in the new order, write a sum of two integrals.

Area as a double integral

Take \(f=1\). Every column has height 1, so the volume equals the base area:

\(\displaystyle A(D)=\iint_D 1\,dA=\int_a^b\!\!\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)}1\,dy\,dx=\int_a^b\big(g_2(x)-g_1(x)\big)\,dx\)The inner integral collapses to the height of the strip, and you are back to the area between two curves from first-year calculus.

Chapter 3 · Stewart 15.3

Polar coordinates

Circles are ugly in \(x\) and \(y\) and trivial in \(r\) and \(\theta\). The price is one extra factor of \(r\).

The conversion is the one from trigonometry:

\(x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta,\qquad x^2+y^2=r^2,\qquad \tan\theta=\frac{y}{x}\)Substitute these into the integrand. Anything built from \(x^2+y^2\) collapses: \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=r\), and \(e^{-(x^2+y^2)}=e^{-r^2}\).

A polar rectangle is \(\{(r,\theta): a\le r\le b,\ \alpha\le\theta\le\beta\}\). It looks like a curved tile. Its area is not \(\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\). A circular sector of radius \(r\) and angle \(\Delta\theta\) has area \(\tfrac12 r^2\Delta\theta\), so the tile between radii \(r\) and \(r+\Delta r\) has area

\(\tfrac12\big[(r+\Delta r)^2-r^2\big]\Delta\theta=\tfrac12(2r\,\Delta r+\Delta r^2)\Delta\theta\approx r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\)The tile is \(\Delta r\) thick and about \(r\,\Delta\theta\) long, so its area grows with \(r\). That is the whole reason for the extra \(r\).
\(\displaystyle\iint_D f(x,y)\,dA=\int_\alpha^\beta\!\!\int_a^b f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\;r\,dr\,d\theta\)Three changes at once: the integrand converts, the limits become \(r\) and \(\theta\) limits, and \(dA\) becomes \(r\,dr\,d\theta\).
Never drop the \(r\). Writing \(dr\,d\theta\) instead of \(r\,dr\,d\theta\) is the mistake in this chapter, and it is silent: the answer still looks reasonable. Check it on the unit disk. With the \(r\) you get \(\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^1 r\,dr\,d\theta=\pi\), the correct area. Without it you get \(2\pi\).

Faint circles are \(r=\) constant, faint rays are \(\theta=\) constant. The orange tile is one \(r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\) cell.

Drag the orange cell outward with the \(r\) sliders while keeping \(\Delta r\) and \(\Delta\theta\) fixed: same \(\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\), visibly more area. That factor of \(r\) is what makes the tiles near the rim count more.

Which regions are polar regions

  • Disk \(x^2+y^2\le a^2\): \(0\le r\le a\), \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\).
  • Annulus \(a^2\le x^2+y^2\le b^2\): \(a\le r\le b\), \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\).
  • Sector or a quarter disk: \(0\le r\le a\) with \(\theta\) between the two bounding rays.
  • Inside \(r=f(\theta)\): \(0\le r\le f(\theta)\), and \(\theta\) runs over the values where the curve is traced. The inner \(r\)-limit may depend on \(\theta\); the \(\theta\)-limits are constants.

Two curves worth knowing. The circle \(r=2a\cos\theta\) is the circle of radius \(a\) centered at \((a,0)\): multiply by \(r\) to get \(x^2+y^2=2ax\). It is traced once for \(-\pi/2\le\theta\le\pi/2\). The cardioid \(r=a(1+\cos\theta)\) is traced once for \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\).

\(\displaystyle A=\iint_D 1\,dA=\int_\alpha^\beta\!\!\int_0^{f(\theta)} r\,dr\,d\theta=\int_\alpha^\beta \tfrac12 f(\theta)^2\,d\theta\)The inner integral of \(r\,dr\) produces the \(\tfrac12 r^2\) in the polar area formula you saw in second-semester calculus. Same formula, one level down.
When to switch to polar. Two signals: the region is a disk, annulus, sector, or is bounded by a circle, and the integrand contains \(x^2+y^2\). Either one alone is often enough.

Chapter 4 · Stewart 15.4 and 15.6

Applications: mass, moments, center of mass

Give the region a density and the same integral starts weighing things.

A lamina is a flat plate occupying a region \(D\) with density \(\rho(x,y)\) in mass per unit area. Mass is density times area, added up cell by cell:

\(\displaystyle m=\iint_D \rho(x,y)\,dA\)Constant density makes this \(\rho\) times the area. Varying density is why you need the integral.

The moments measure how the mass is distributed about the axes:

\(\displaystyle M_x=\iint_D y\,\rho(x,y)\,dA,\qquad M_y=\iint_D x\,\rho(x,y)\,dA\)Each bit of mass is weighted by its distance to an axis. The moment about the \(x\)-axis uses \(y\), because \(y\) is the distance to the \(x\)-axis.
\(\displaystyle \bar x=\frac{M_y}{m},\qquad \bar y=\frac{M_x}{m}\)The center of mass \((\bar x,\bar y)\) is the density-weighted average position. The plate balances there.
Do not cross the subscripts. \(M_x\) integrates \(y\rho\) and lands in \(\bar y\); \(M_y\) integrates \(x\rho\) and lands in \(\bar x\). Also check plausibility: the center of mass always lies inside the convex hull of the plate, and it shifts toward the heavy side.

Surface area

The piece of the surface \(z=f(x,y)\) lying above a region \(D\) has area

\(\displaystyle A(S)=\iint_D\sqrt{1+f_x^2+f_y^2}\;dA\)The square root is the factor by which a flat cell \(dA\) stretches when it is tilted up onto the surface. For a plane \(z=ax+by+c\) the factor is the constant \(\sqrt{1+a^2+b^2}\), so the area is just that constant times the area of \(D\).
Tilt factor. A plane with slopes \(a\) and \(b\) covers its shadow \(\sqrt{1+a^2+b^2}\) times over. A horizontal plane gives 1, and a steep plane gives a large number, which is the sanity check to run on any surface-area answer.

Chapter 5 · Stewart 15.9

Change of variables

Substitution, in two dimensions. You pick new coordinates that straighten the region, and pay for it with a Jacobian.

A transformation \(T\) takes a region \(S\) in the \(uv\)-plane to a region \(R\) in the \(xy\)-plane through \(x=x(u,v)\), \(y=y(u,v)\). A small rectangle in \(S\) maps to a small parallelogram in \(R\). The factor by which its area changes is the Jacobian:

\(\displaystyle\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=\begin{vmatrix}\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u}&\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial v}\\[6pt] \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial u}&\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial v}\end{vmatrix}=\frac{\partial x}{\partial u}\frac{\partial y}{\partial v}-\frac{\partial x}{\partial v}\frac{\partial y}{\partial u}\)A \(2\times2\) determinant of partial derivatives. Top row is \(x\), bottom row is \(y\); first column is \(u\), second is \(v\).
\(\displaystyle\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA=\iint_S f\big(x(u,v),y(u,v)\big)\left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|du\,dv\)Three changes, same as polar: rewrite the integrand in \(u\) and \(v\), replace \(R\) by \(S\), and multiply by the absolute value of the Jacobian.

Polar coordinates are exactly this with \(u=r\), \(v=\theta\). From \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\):

\(\displaystyle\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(r,\theta)}=\begin{vmatrix}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&r\cos\theta\end{vmatrix}=r\cos^2\theta+r\sin^2\theta=r\)The \(r\) in \(r\,dr\,d\theta\) was a Jacobian all along.

Choosing the substitution

Two things can go wrong with an integral, and either one can motivate the change:

  1. The region is awkward. Pick \(u\) and \(v\) so that the bounding curves become \(u=\) constant and \(v=\) constant. A region bounded by \(x-y=0\), \(x-y=2\), \(x+y=0\), \(x+y=3\) begs for \(u=x-y\), \(v=x+y\); then \(S\) is the rectangle \([0,2]\times[0,3]\).
  2. The integrand is awkward. Pick \(u\) and \(v\) so that the ugly combination becomes a single variable, as with \(e^{(x+y)/(x-y)}\).

When you define \(u\) and \(v\) in terms of \(x\) and \(y\), the easy determinant to compute is \(\partial(u,v)/\partial(x,y)\). It is the reciprocal of the one you need:

\(\displaystyle\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=\left(\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}\right)^{-1}\)For \(u=x-y\), \(v=x+y\) the determinant \(\partial(u,v)/\partial(x,y)=2\), so \(\partial(x,y)/\partial(u,v)=\tfrac12\) and \(dA=\tfrac12\,du\,dv\).
Absolute value, and the right way up. The formula uses \(\left|\partial(x,y)/\partial(u,v)\right|\). A negative Jacobian only means the map flips orientation; areas stay positive. And the Jacobian rides with \(du\,dv\), never with \(dx\,dy\).
Why the determinant. The image of the cell is the parallelogram spanned by \(\mathbf{r}_u\,du\) and \(\mathbf{r}_v\,dv\). The area of a parallelogram spanned by two vectors is the absolute value of the \(2\times2\) determinant of their components, which is exactly \(|J|\,du\,dv\).
