Chapter 1

What is a matrix?

A rectangular grid of numbers, plus a precise way to name its size and each entry.

A matrix is just numbers arranged in rows and columns. Its dimensions (or size, or shape) are written rows × columns, in that order, always. A matrix with 2 rows and 3 columns is a "2 by 3" matrix.

Rows first, then columns. Rows run left to right (like rows of seats). Columns run top to bottom (like the columns holding up a building). "R before C" applies to dimensions and to entry names.

Each number in the matrix is an entry. The entry in row i, column j of matrix A is written aij. So a23 lives in row 2, column 3.

Vocabulary you will see constantly

A square matrix has the same number of rows as columns (2×2, 3×3, ...). A row vector is a 1×n matrix and a column vector is an n×1 matrix. The main diagonal of a square matrix is the entries a11, a22, a33, ... running from top left to bottom right.

Chapter 2

Transpose, identity, and zero

Three things you will meet in every chapter that follows.

The transpose

The transpose of A, written AT, flips A over its main diagonal: row 1 becomes column 1, row 2 becomes column 2, and so on. Formally, (AT)ij = aji. A 2×3 matrix transposes to a 3×2 matrix.

Rows Columns Hover an entry of either matrix to see where it goes.

The identity matrix

The identity matrix In is the n×n square matrix with 1s on the main diagonal and 0s everywhere else. It is the matrix version of the number 1: multiplying by it changes nothing (Chapter 6 shows this).

The zero matrix

The zero matrix has every entry equal to 0. It can be any size. Adding it changes nothing, and multiplying by it gives zero.

Chapter 3

Adding and scaling matrices

The easy operations. They work entry by entry.

Addition. Two matrices can be added only if they have exactly the same dimensions. Then you add matching entries: (A + B)ij = aij + bij. Subtraction works the same way.

Scalar multiplication. A scalar is an ordinary number. Multiplying a matrix by a scalar k multiplies every entry by k: (kA)ij = k·aij.

Combine them freely. Expressions like 2A − B are computed by scaling first, then adding entry by entry. This is called a linear combination of A and B.

Chapter 4

When can you multiply two matrices?

Before computing anything, check the shapes. This check takes two seconds and prevents most mistakes.

To compute the product AB, the number of columns of A must equal the number of rows of B. Write the dimensions side by side and look at the inner pair:

(m × n) · (n × p)  =  m × p

The inner numbers must match. The outer numbers give the size of the answer.

Chapter 5

The row-column rule

Every entry of a product is a row of the left matrix "dotted" with a column of the right matrix.

To find entry (i, j) of AB: take row i of A and column j of B. Multiply them pairwise (first with first, second with second, ...) and add the results. That is:

(AB)ij = ai1b1j + ai2b2j + ... + ainbnj

A physical way to do it: put your left index finger on the start of the row, your right index finger on the top of the column. Multiply the two numbers under your fingers, then slide left finger right and right finger down together. Keep multiplying and adding until you run out. The "matching" pairs are why the inner dimensions had to agree.

Chapter 7

Rules and surprises

Matrix multiplication keeps some habits from ordinary numbers and breaks others.

AB is usually not BA

For numbers, 3 × 5 = 5 × 3. For matrices this fails almost always. Roll a few random pairs and compare. Cells that differ are marked.

The identity really is the identity

Try the row-column rule on any entry: only one term in the sum survives, the one that multiplies by the 1.

The rules that do hold

  • Associative: (AB)C = A(BC). You can group however you like, as long as you keep the order.
  • Distributive: A(B + C) = AB + AC and (A + B)C = AC + BC.
  • Scalars slide through: k(AB) = (kA)B = A(kB).
  • Identity: IA = A and AI = A whenever the sizes work.
  • Transpose reverses order: (AB)T = BTAT. Not ATBT.

The rules that break

  • Not commutative: AB ≠ BA in general (and one side may not even be defined).
  • No cancellation: AB = AC does not force B = C.

Chapter 8

A matrix is a transformation of space

This is the picture that makes everything else click. A 2×2 matrix moves every point in the plane.

Multiplying a column vector v = [x, y] by a 2×2 matrix M gives a new vector Mv. Do that to every point and the whole plane stretches, rotates, shears, or flips. The key fact:

The columns of M are where the basis vectors land. The first column is the image of î = [1, 0]. The second column is the image of ĵ = [0, 1]. Everything else follows, because Mv = x·(column 1) + y·(column 2).

Multiplying matrices = composing transformations

Applying M1 and then M2 is the same as applying the single matrix M2M1. Read right to left: the matrix nearest the vector acts first. This is the deep reason the row-column rule looks the way it does, and the geometric reason AB ≠ BA: rotating then shearing is a different picture than shearing then rotating.

Determinant and inverse (2×2)

Two numbers-worth of information that tell you whether a matrix can be undone.

Down-right product minus up-right product. Geometrically, it is the factor by which M scales area, with a negative sign if M flips orientation.
