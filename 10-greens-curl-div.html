Chapter 1 · Stewart 16.4

Green's theorem

A line integral around a closed curve equals a double integral over the region the curve encloses. Ugly boundary, nice region: use the region.

Let \(C\) be a positively oriented, piecewise-smooth, simple closed curve in the plane, and let \(D\) be the region it bounds. If \(P\) and \(Q\) have continuous partial derivatives on an open set containing \(D\), then:

\(\displaystyle\oint_C P\,dx+Q\,dy=\iint_D\left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)dA\)Read it right to left too: the double integral of \(Q_x-P_y\) over a region equals the loop integral around its edge. Both sides are written \(\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) when \(\mathbf{F}=\langle P,Q\rangle\).

Positive orientation means counterclockwise: as you walk along \(C\), the region \(D\) stays on your left. Simple means the curve does not cross itself. Closed means it ends where it started. Stewart writes \(\oint\) for a line integral around a positively oriented closed curve.

When to reach for it

  • The boundary is made of several pieces (three sides of a triangle, an arc plus a segment) but the region is a single easy double integral.
  • \(Q_x-P_y\) collapses to something simple: a constant, or \(x^2+y^2\), which polar coordinates eat instantly.
  • You want an area and you only know the boundary curve.

That last one comes from choosing \(P\) and \(Q\) so that \(Q_x-P_y=1\). Three standard choices give three formulas for the same number:

\(A=\oint_C x\,dy=-\oint_C y\,dx=\tfrac12\oint_C x\,dy-y\,dx\)Take \(\langle P,Q\rangle=\langle 0,x\rangle\), then \(\langle -y,0\rangle\), then the average of the two. The symmetric third form is the one to use on a parametrized curve.

On a curve given by \(x=x(t)\), \(y=y(t)\) for \(a\le t\le b\), that last formula reads \(A=\tfrac12\int_a^b\left(x\,y'-y\,x'\right)dt\).

Orientation and holes

Reversing the direction of travel reverses the sign of every line integral: if \(C\) is positively oriented and \(-C\) is the same curve clockwise, then \(\oint_{-C}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}=-\oint_{C}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\). So a clockwise problem is a counterclockwise problem with a minus sign in front.

If \(D\) has a hole, the full boundary is the outer curve counterclockwise plus each inner curve clockwise, so that the region is on your left the whole way around. Green's theorem still holds with that combined boundary, which is how you handle \(\mathbf{F}=\left\langle \frac{-y}{x^2+y^2},\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\right\rangle\): cut out a small circle about the origin and compare the two loops.

Check the hypotheses first. Green's theorem needs a closed curve with positive orientation, and \(P,Q\) must have continuous partials on all of \(D\), holes included. An open arc gets no \(\oint\). A clockwise loop needs a minus sign. A field that blows up inside \(D\) (like \(1/(x^2+y^2)\) at the origin) is not covered until you cut the bad point out.

Step by step: Green's theorem on a rectangle

    Notice how much of \(P\) and \(Q\) never matters. Any term of \(P\) with no \(y\) in it dies under \(\partial/\partial y\), and any term of \(Q\) with no \(x\) in it dies under \(\partial/\partial x\). Those terms are free.

    Curl and divergence

    Two derivatives of a vector field. One is a vector and measures spin. One is a number and measures spreading.

    Treat \(\nabla\) as a vector of operators, \(\nabla=\left\langle \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x},\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y},\dfrac{\partial}{\partial z}\right\rangle\). Applied to a scalar function it gives the gradient \(\nabla f\). Crossed into a vector field it gives the curl. Dotted into a vector field it gives the divergence.

    For \(\mathbf{F}=P\,\mathbf{i}+Q\,\mathbf{j}+R\,\mathbf{k}\):

    \(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}=\nabla\times\mathbf{F}=\begin{vmatrix}\mathbf{i}&\mathbf{j}&\mathbf{k}\\[2pt] \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}&\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}&\dfrac{\partial}{\partial z}\\[4pt] P&Q&R\end{vmatrix}=\left\langle \frac{\partial R}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial Q}{\partial z},\;\frac{\partial P}{\partial z}-\frac{\partial R}{\partial x},\;\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right\rangle\)Same determinant as a cross product, with the middle row replaced by derivative operators. Each component skips its own letter: the \(\mathbf{i}\) slot uses only \(y\) and \(z\) derivatives, the \(\mathbf{j}\) slot only \(z\) and \(x\), the \(\mathbf{k}\) slot only \(x\) and \(y\).
    \(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{F}=\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}=\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial Q}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial R}{\partial z}\)Each component differentiated by its own variable, then added. The output is a scalar function, not a vector.
    The middle component is the one people lose. Expanding the determinant along the top row puts a minus in front of the \(\mathbf{j}\) term, so the middle slot is \(-\left(R_x-P_z\right)=P_z-R_x\), with the derivative of \(P\) written first. The \(\mathbf{i}\) and \(\mathbf{k}\) slots run in the order \(R_y-Q_z\) and \(Q_x-P_y\); only the middle one is reversed. If your \(\mathbf{j}\) component came out as \(R_x-P_z\), you dropped the sign.

    What they mean

    • Curl is rotation. Drop a tiny paddle wheel into the flow at a point. It spins about the axis \(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}\), with angular speed \(|\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}|/2\), in the right-hand sense about that axis. Where \(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0}\) the field is called irrotational: the wheel drifts without turning.
    • Divergence is expansion. Put a tiny balloon at a point. Positive divergence means more flow out than in, a source; negative means a sink. Where \(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{F}=0\) the field is incompressible: whatever enters leaves.

    Two identities

    \(\operatorname{curl}(\nabla f)=\mathbf{0}\qquad\qquad \operatorname{div}(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F})=0\)Both are just Clairaut's theorem: the mixed second partials cancel in pairs. The first output is the zero vector, the second is the number zero.

    The first identity gives the curl test: if \(\mathbf{F}\) is conservative then \(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0}\). The converse needs a condition on the domain: if \(\mathbf{F}\) has continuous partials on all of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) and \(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{0}\), then \(\mathbf{F}\) is conservative, so a potential \(f\) with \(\nabla f=\mathbf{F}\) exists.

    Not every combination is legal. \(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{F}\) is a scalar, so \(\operatorname{curl}(\operatorname{div}\mathbf{F})\) is meaningless. \(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}\) is a vector, so \(\operatorname{div}(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F})\) is fine. And \(\operatorname{div}(\nabla f)=f_{xx}+f_{yy}+f_{zz}\) is the Laplacian \(\nabla^2 f\), not zero.

    Curl of a gradient is always zero. \(\operatorname{curl}(\nabla f)=\langle f_{zy}-f_{yz},\,f_{xz}-f_{zx},\,f_{yx}-f_{xy}\rangle=\mathbf{0}\) whenever the second partials are continuous. So the fastest conservative test in 3D is: compute the curl, and if any component survives, the field is not conservative and no potential exists.

    Back to the plane

    A plane field \(\langle P,Q\rangle\) becomes \(\langle P,Q,0\rangle\) in space. Its curl has only one surviving slot:

    \(\operatorname{curl}\langle P,Q,0\rangle=\left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)\mathbf{k}\)The Green's theorem integrand is exactly the \(\mathbf{k}\)-component of the curl. It is called the scalar curl of the plane field.

    That rewrites Green's theorem twice. The circulation form and the flux form:

    \(\displaystyle\oint_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}=\iint_D(\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{k}\,dA\qquad\qquad \oint_C\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,ds=\iint_D\operatorname{div}\mathbf{F}\,dA\)Left: circulation around the edge equals total spin inside. This is Stokes' theorem in the plane. Right: flux out through the edge equals total expansion inside. This is the divergence theorem in the plane. Here \(\mathbf{n}\) is the outward unit normal.

    Step by step: expanding the curl determinant

    Cover the column of the basis vector you are computing. What is left is a 2×2 determinant of operators over functions: down-right minus up-right. The signs across the top row go \(+,-,+\), which is what reverses the middle component.
