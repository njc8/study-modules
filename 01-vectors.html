Vectors in space

Everything in multivariable calculus is built on vectors. This module makes the four core operations automatic: components, arithmetic, the dot product, and the cross product.

Chapter 1 · Stewart 12.1 to 12.2

Points and vectors in space

Three coordinates locate a point. Two points define a vector. One formula gives every length.

A point in space is an ordered triple \(P(x,y,z)\). The axes are drawn so that \(x\) comes toward you, \(y\) goes to the right, and \(z\) goes up (the right-handed convention: curl the fingers of your right hand from \(x\) to \(y\) and your thumb points along \(z\)).

Explore: locate a point

Drag to rotate, scroll to zoom. Dashed lines drop the point to the \(xy\)-plane and then to the axes.

The distance from the origin uses the Pythagorean theorem twice: once in the \(xy\)-plane, once up the \(z\) direction. That is why the formula has three squares.

\(|P_1P_2| = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2+(z_2-z_1)^2}\)Distance between two points. The same expression is the length of the vector from one point to the other.

Vectors: displacement, not location

A vector has a magnitude and a direction but no fixed position. The vector from \(P(x_1,y_1,z_1)\) to \(Q(x_2,y_2,z_2)\) is found by subtracting terminal minus initial:

\(\overrightarrow{PQ}=\langle x_2-x_1,\; y_2-y_1,\; z_2-z_1\rangle\)Angle brackets mean components. The position vector of a point is the vector from the origin to it, so its components equal the coordinates.

The magnitude (length) of \(\mathbf{a}=\langle a_1,a_2,a_3\rangle\) is \(|\mathbf{a}|=\sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2}\). A vector of length 1 is a unit vector.

Chapter 2 · Stewart 12.2

Vector arithmetic

Addition is tip to tail. Scaling stretches. Everything happens one component at a time.

For \(\mathbf{a}=\langle a_1,a_2,a_3\rangle\), \(\mathbf{b}=\langle b_1,b_2,b_3\rangle\), and a scalar \(c\):

\(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}=\langle a_1+b_1,\,a_2+b_2,\,a_3+b_3\rangle \qquad c\,\mathbf{a}=\langle ca_1,\,ca_2,\,ca_3\rangle \qquad |c\,\mathbf{a}| = |c|\,|\mathbf{a}|\)

Geometrically, \(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}\) is found by placing the tail of \(\mathbf{b}\) at the tip of \(\mathbf{a}\) (the triangle law), or equivalently as the diagonal of the parallelogram spanned by both. The difference \(\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\) is the vector that goes from the tip of \(\mathbf{b}\) to the tip of \(\mathbf{a}\), because \(\mathbf{b}+(\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b})=\mathbf{a}\).

Explore: drag the tips of a and b

Notice that a + b and a − b are the two diagonals of the same parallelogram. Drag b onto the same line as a to see what happens to the parallelogram's area (Chapter 4 explains why).

Standard basis vectors

\(\mathbf{i}=\langle 1,0,0\rangle\), \(\mathbf{j}=\langle 0,1,0\rangle\), \(\mathbf{k}=\langle 0,0,1\rangle\). Any vector is a combination of them: \(\langle a_1,a_2,a_3\rangle = a_1\mathbf{i}+a_2\mathbf{j}+a_3\mathbf{k}\). A missing letter means that component is 0: \(\mathbf{i}-7\mathbf{k}=\langle 1,0,-7\rangle\).

Unit vectors

To get a unit vector in the direction of \(\mathbf{a}\), divide by the length: \(\mathbf{u}=\dfrac{\mathbf{a}}{|\mathbf{a}|}\). This is used constantly: directional derivatives, projections, and the tangent vector all need a direction with the length stripped out.

Two-step habit. First compute the length. Then divide every component by it. Leave the answer as fractions or with a square root, for example \(\langle \tfrac{2}{3}, -\tfrac{1}{3}, \tfrac{2}{3}\rangle\) or \(\langle \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{5}}, \tfrac{2}{\sqrt{5}}\rangle\).

Chapter 3 · Stewart 12.3

The dot product

A product of two vectors that gives a number. It measures how much the two vectors point the same way.

\(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=a_1b_1+a_2b_2+a_3b_3 \qquad\text{and also}\qquad \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=|\mathbf{a}|\,|\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta\)Multiply matching components and add. The second form connects that number to the angle \(\theta\) between the vectors (\(0\le\theta\le\pi\)).

Because the two formulas agree, you can solve for the angle:

\(\cos\theta=\dfrac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}|\,|\mathbf{b}|}\)
  • \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}>0\): the angle is acute, the vectors lean the same way.
  • \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=0\): the vectors are orthogonal (perpendicular). This is the single most used test in the course.
  • \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}<0\): the angle is obtuse.
  • \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a}=|\mathbf{a}|^2\). A dot product of a vector with itself is its length squared.

Parallel vectors are a different test: \(\mathbf{b}\) is parallel to \(\mathbf{a}\) when \(\mathbf{b}=c\,\mathbf{a}\) for some scalar \(c\), which means every component ratio \(b_i/a_i\) is the same number.

Projections

The scalar projection of \(\mathbf{b}\) onto \(\mathbf{a}\) is the signed length of the shadow. The vector projection is that shadow as a vector along \(\mathbf{a}\):

\(\operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b}=\dfrac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}|}\qquad\qquad \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{b}=\left(\dfrac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}|^2}\right)\mathbf{a}=\left(\dfrac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}|}\right)\dfrac{\mathbf{a}}{|\mathbf{a}|}\)Scalar projection: dot product divided by the length of the thing you project onto. Vector projection: that scalar times the unit vector of \(\mathbf{a}\).

Work. A constant force \(\mathbf{F}\) moving an object along displacement \(\mathbf{D}\) does work \(W=\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{D}\). Only the part of the force along the motion counts, which is exactly what the dot product extracts.

Chapter 4 · Stewart 12.4

The cross product

A product of two 3D vectors that gives a vector: perpendicular to both, with length equal to the area of the parallelogram they span.

\(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}=\begin{vmatrix}\mathbf{i}&\mathbf{j}&\mathbf{k}\\ a_1&a_2&a_3\\ b_1&b_2&b_3\end{vmatrix}=\langle a_2b_3-a_3b_2,\; a_3b_1-a_1b_3,\; a_1b_2-a_2b_1\rangle\)Expand the determinant along the top row. The middle component has a minus sign built in: it is \(-(a_1b_3-a_3b_1)\), which is the same as \(a_3b_1-a_1b_3\).

Step-by-step: expanding the determinant

Cover the column of the basis vector you are working on. What is left is a 2×2 determinant: down-right product minus up-right product. The signs across the top row go +, −, +.

What the cross product means

  • Direction: \(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\) is orthogonal to both \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\). Which of the two perpendicular directions? The right-hand rule: curl your fingers from \(\mathbf{a}\) toward \(\mathbf{b}\); your thumb gives \(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\).
  • Length: \(|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}|=|\mathbf{a}|\,|\mathbf{b}|\sin\theta\), the area of the parallelogram with sides \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\). Half of it is the area of the triangle.
  • Parallel test: \(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{0}\) exactly when the vectors are parallel (\(\sin\theta=0\)).
  • Order matters: \(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{a}=-(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b})\). Also \(\mathbf{i}\times\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{k}\), \(\mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{i}\), \(\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{i}=\mathbf{j}\) (cyclic), and \(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{0}\).

Three standard uses

  1. A normal vector to a plane through three points \(P,Q,R\): compute \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\times\overrightarrow{PR}\). Any nonzero multiple works equally well. (Module 2 uses this to write plane equations.)
  2. Area of triangle \(PQR\): \(\tfrac12\,|\overrightarrow{PQ}\times\overrightarrow{PR}|\).
  3. Volume of the parallelepiped with edges \(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\): the scalar triple product \(|\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c})|\), which is the absolute value of the 3×3 determinant with rows \(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\). If it is 0, the three vectors are coplanar.
Dot vs cross. Dot: any dimension, output is a number, zero means perpendicular, measures cos. Cross: 3D only, output is a vector, zero means parallel, length measures sin. When a problem says "orthogonal to both", reach for the cross product. When it says "angle" or "perpendicular to one", reach for the dot product.
