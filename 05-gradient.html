The gradient

Collect the partial derivatives into a vector. That vector knows which way is uphill, how steep it is, and which way the level curve runs.

The gradient of \(f\) is the vector whose components are the partial derivatives:

\(\nabla f=\langle f_x,\,f_y\rangle \qquad\qquad \nabla f=\langle f_x,\,f_y,\,f_z\rangle\)Read \(\nabla f\) as "grad f" or "del f". It is a vector built out of a scalar function, and it has one component per input variable.

Evaluating it at a point \(P\) gives a vector of numbers, \(\nabla f(P)\). Three facts make it the most useful object in this chapter.

  • It is perpendicular to the level set. In two variables \(\nabla f(P)\) is perpendicular to the level curve \(f(x,y)=k\) through \(P\). In three variables \(\nabla F(P)\) is perpendicular to the level surface \(F(x,y,z)=k\) through \(P\).
  • It points in the direction of fastest increase, and its length \(|\nabla f(P)|\) is that maximum rate of change.
  • The minimum rate is \(-|\nabla f(P)|\), in the direction \(-\nabla f(P)\), and the rate is zero in the two directions perpendicular to \(\nabla f(P)\): exactly the directions along the level curve.

Perpendicular to the level set. This one fact does the work of three formulas. It gives the tangent plane (the plane through \(P\) with normal \(\nabla F(P)\)), the normal line (the line through \(P\) with direction \(\nabla F(P)\)), and the tangent line to a level curve in the plane.

Tangent planes, normal lines, tangent lines

Let \(S\) be the level surface \(F(x,y,z)=k\) and let \(P(x_0,y_0,z_0)\) be a point on it. Every curve lying in \(S\) through \(P\) has a tangent vector perpendicular to \(\nabla F(P)\), so all those tangent vectors sweep out a plane with normal \(\nabla F(P)\):

\(F_x(P)(x-x_0)+F_y(P)(y-y_0)+F_z(P)(z-z_0)=0\)Tangent plane to \(F(x,y,z)=k\) at \(P\). The coefficients are numbers: evaluate the partials at \(P\) first.

The normal line at \(P\) is the line through \(P\) in the direction \(\nabla F(P)\), so \(\;x=x_0+F_x(P)\,t,\;\; y=y_0+F_y(P)\,t,\;\; z=z_0+F_z(P)\,t\).

In two variables the same argument gives the tangent line to a level curve \(f(x,y)=k\) at \(P(x_0,y_0)\):

\(f_x(P)(x-x_0)+f_y(P)(y-y_0)=0\)Same shape, one dimension down. The gradient is the normal vector of the tangent line.

A surface given as a graph \(z=g(x,y)\) is a level surface too: set \(F(x,y,z)=g(x,y)-z\), which has \(F=0\) on the surface and \(\nabla F=\langle g_x,\,g_y,\,-1\rangle\). That recovers the tangent plane formula from the previous module.

Evaluate before you write. The tangent plane coefficients are \(F_x(P)\), not \(F_x\). If an \(x\) or a \(y\) is still sitting in front of the parenthesis in your final answer, you skipped the evaluation step and the answer is not a plane. Also keep \(f\) and \(\nabla f\) straight: \(f\) is a number, \(\nabla f\) is a vector, and asking for "the rate" means a number while asking for "the direction" means a vector.

Directional derivatives

The partial derivatives give the rate of change along the axes. The directional derivative gives it along any direction you like, and it is just a dot product.

The directional derivative of \(f\) at \(P\) in the direction of a unit vector \(\mathbf{u}\) is the rate at which \(f\) changes as you step away from \(P\) along \(\mathbf{u}\):

\(D_{\mathbf{u}}f(P)=\nabla f(P)\cdot\mathbf{u}\)A number, not a vector. It is positive when \(f\) increases in that direction, negative when \(f\) decreases, zero when \(f\) holds steady.

Notice the two special cases: \(\mathbf{u}=\langle 1,0\rangle\) gives \(D_{\mathbf{u}}f=f_x\) and \(\mathbf{u}=\langle 0,1\rangle\) gives \(f_y\). The partials are directional derivatives along the axes.

Getting \(\mathbf{u}\): three ways a problem states the direction

  1. "In the direction of the vector \(\mathbf{v}\)": \(\mathbf{v}\) is almost never a unit vector, so normalize it. \(\mathbf{u}=\dfrac{\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|}\).
  2. "In the direction that makes angle \(\theta\) with the positive \(x\)-axis": \(\mathbf{u}=\langle\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta\rangle\), which already has length 1.
  3. "Toward the point \(Q\)": the direction is \(\mathbf{v}=\overrightarrow{PQ}=Q-P\), then normalize. Subtract in that order, terminal minus initial, or you will walk away from \(Q\).
Forgetting to normalize is the number one error in this section. \(\nabla f(P)\cdot\mathbf{v}\) is not the directional derivative unless \(|\mathbf{v}|=1\). If the problem hands you \(\mathbf{v}=\langle 3,4\rangle\), the answer is \(\nabla f(P)\cdot\langle \tfrac35,\tfrac45\rangle\), which is one fifth of \(\nabla f(P)\cdot\langle 3,4\rangle\). Every time you write \(D_{\mathbf{u}}f\), check that the vector you dotted with has length 1.

Why the gradient is the steepest direction

Write the dot product with the angle: \(D_{\mathbf{u}}f=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf{u}=|\nabla f|\,|\mathbf{u}|\cos\phi=|\nabla f|\cos\phi\), where \(\phi\) is the angle between \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\nabla f\). As \(\mathbf{u}\) rotates, the only thing that changes is \(\cos\phi\), which swings between \(1\) and \(-1\). So:

\(-|\nabla f(P)|\ \le\ D_{\mathbf{u}}f(P)\ \le\ |\nabla f(P)|\)Maximum \(|\nabla f|\) when \(\mathbf{u}\) points along \(\nabla f\) (\(\phi=0\)). Minimum \(-|\nabla f|\) in the opposite direction (\(\phi=\pi\)). Zero when \(\mathbf{u}\perp\nabla f\) (\(\phi=\pi/2\)), which is along the level curve.

In three variables nothing changes: \(D_{\mathbf{u}}f(P)=\nabla f(P)\cdot\mathbf{u}\) with \(\mathbf{u}\) a unit vector in space, and the same three bounds hold.

The three answers you will be asked for over and over. Fastest increase: direction \(\nabla f(P)\), rate \(|\nabla f(P)|\). Fastest decrease: direction \(-\nabla f(P)\), rate \(-|\nabla f(P)|\). No change: any direction perpendicular to \(\nabla f(P)\), rate \(0\).

Step-by-step: computing \(D_{\mathbf{u}}f(P)\)

Four steps, always the same four: gradient as a function, gradient at the point, unit vector, dot product. The third step is the one people skip.

The chain rule

When the inputs of a function are themselves functions, the derivative collects one term for every route the dependency can travel.

In one variable, \(\frac{d}{dt}f(x(t))=f'(x)\,\frac{dx}{dt}\): the outer derivative times the inner derivative. With two inputs there are two routes from \(z\) down to \(t\), one through \(x\) and one through \(y\), so there are two terms.

\(\dfrac{dz}{dt}=\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}\dfrac{dx}{dt}+\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}\dfrac{dy}{dt}\)Case 1. \(z=f(x,y)\) with \(x=x(t)\) and \(y=y(t)\). Everything ends up depending on the single variable \(t\), so the answer is an ordinary derivative \(dz/dt\).

If the inner functions depend on two parameters instead of one, run the same rule once for each parameter, holding the other fixed. The outer derivatives \(\partial z/\partial x\) and \(\partial z/\partial y\) do not change; only the inner ones do.

\(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial s}=\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial s}+\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial s} \qquad\qquad \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial t}=\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial t}+\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial t}\)Case 2. \(z=f(x,y)\) with \(x=x(s,t)\) and \(y=y(s,t)\). Two parameters, so two formulas, and every derivative is a partial.

The general chain rule is one sentence: draw the tree, multiply along each path, add the paths. Write the output on top, the intermediate variables below it, the independent variables at the bottom, and label every edge with the derivative of the upper variable with respect to the lower one. To get the derivative of the top with respect to a bottom variable, find every path from the top down to it, multiply the labels along each path, and add.

Explore: the tree diagram

Click a variable at the bottom of the tree to light up every path that reaches it.

Count the paths before you read the formula. Two intermediate variables give two terms, three give three. The number of terms never depends on how many parameters are at the bottom, only on how many variables sit in the middle layer.

One term per path. That is the whole rule. If your answer has fewer terms than the tree has paths, you dropped one. If it has more, you counted a path twice.

Implicit differentiation

Suppose an equation in \(x\) and \(y\) defines \(y\) implicitly as a function of \(x\). Move everything to one side so the equation reads \(F(x,y)=0\). Now differentiate both sides with respect to \(x\), using the chain rule on the left with \(x=x\) and \(y=y(x)\):

\(F_x\dfrac{dx}{dx}+F_y\dfrac{dy}{dx}=0\), and \(dx/dx=1\), so:

\(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac{F_x}{F_y}\)Valid wherever \(F_y\ne 0\). The minus sign comes from moving \(F_x\) to the other side.

The same argument with three variables, where \(F(x,y,z)=0\) defines \(z\) as a function of \(x\) and \(y\), gives two formulas. Differentiating with respect to \(x\) holds \(y\) fixed, so \(\partial y/\partial x=0\) and that path dies:

\(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}=-\dfrac{F_x}{F_z} \qquad\qquad \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}=-\dfrac{F_y}{F_z}\)Valid wherever \(F_z\ne 0\). Note which letter sits in the denominator: always the variable you solved for.
Two ways to lose points here. First, forgetting the inner derivative: \(\frac{d}{dt}(x^2)\) is \(2x\,\frac{dx}{dt}\), not \(2x\). Second, dropping the minus sign in \(dy/dx=-F_x/F_y\). Before you use the implicit formulas, check that you actually moved everything to one side, so that the equation really reads \(F=0\).
