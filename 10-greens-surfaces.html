Chapter 1 · Stewart 16.4

Green's theorem

A line integral around a closed curve equals a double integral over the region the curve encloses. Ugly boundary, nice region: use the region.

Let \(C\) be a positively oriented, piecewise-smooth, simple closed curve in the plane, and let \(D\) be the region it bounds. If \(P\) and \(Q\) have continuous partial derivatives on an open set containing \(D\), then:

\(\displaystyle\oint_C P\,dx+Q\,dy=\iint_D\left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)dA\)Read it right to left too: the double integral of \(Q_x-P_y\) over a region equals the loop integral around its edge. Both sides are written \(\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) when \(\mathbf{F}=\langle P,Q\rangle\).

Positive orientation means counterclockwise: as you walk along \(C\), the region \(D\) stays on your left. Simple means the curve does not cross itself. Closed means it ends where it started. Stewart writes \(\oint\) for a line integral around a positively oriented closed curve.

When to reach for it

  • The boundary is made of several pieces (three sides of a triangle, an arc plus a segment) but the region is a single easy double integral.
  • \(Q_x-P_y\) collapses to something simple: a constant, or \(x^2+y^2\), which polar coordinates eat instantly.
  • You want an area and you only know the boundary curve.

That last one comes from choosing \(P\) and \(Q\) so that \(Q_x-P_y=1\). Three standard choices give three formulas for the same number:

\(A=\oint_C x\,dy=-\oint_C y\,dx=\tfrac12\oint_C x\,dy-y\,dx\)Take \(\langle P,Q\rangle=\langle 0,x\rangle\), then \(\langle -y,0\rangle\), then the average of the two. The symmetric third form is the one to use on a parametrized curve.

On a curve given by \(x=x(t)\), \(y=y(t)\) for \(a\le t\le b\), that last formula reads \(A=\tfrac12\int_a^b\left(x\,y'-y\,x'\right)dt\).

Orientation and holes

Reversing the direction of travel reverses the sign of every line integral: if \(C\) is positively oriented and \(-C\) is the same curve clockwise, then \(\oint_{-C}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}=-\oint_{C}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\). So a clockwise problem is a counterclockwise problem with a minus sign in front.

If \(D\) has a hole, the full boundary is the outer curve counterclockwise plus each inner curve clockwise, so that the region is on your left the whole way around. Green's theorem still holds with that combined boundary, which is how you handle \(\mathbf{F}=\left\langle \frac{-y}{x^2+y^2},\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\right\rangle\): cut out a small circle about the origin and compare the two loops.

Check the hypotheses first. Green's theorem needs a closed curve with positive orientation, and \(P,Q\) must have continuous partials on all of \(D\), holes included. An open arc gets no \(\oint\) until you close it off, which is the next trick. A clockwise loop needs a minus sign. A field that blows up inside \(D\) (like \(1/(x^2+y^2)\) at the origin) is not covered until you cut the bad point out.

Closing an open path

Green's theorem only speaks about closed curves, but you can still use it on an open path \(C\). Add a segment \(L\) from the end of \(C\) back to its start. Now \(C\) followed by \(L\) is a closed loop around a region \(D\), so

\(\displaystyle\int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}=\pm\iint_D\left(Q_x-P_y\right)dA-\int_L\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\)Plus if \(C\) then \(L\) runs counterclockwise around \(D\), minus if it runs clockwise. You trade one hard line integral for a double integral and one easy segment.

Pick \(L\) along a line where \(\mathbf{F}\) simplifies. On a horizontal segment \(dy=0\) and \(y\) is a constant, so only \(P\) matters, and any messy \(Q\) (like \(e^{y^2}\)) never has to be integrated. That is usually the whole reason the problem gave you a \(Q\) you cannot integrate.

Example: \(\mathbf{F}=\langle x^2+3y,\;e^{y^2}+5x\rangle\), and \(C\) is three sides of the square \([0,2]\times[0,2]\), running from \((0,2)\) down to \((0,0)\), right to \((2,0)\), then up to \((2,2)\).

  1. \(Q_x-P_y=5-3=2\), a constant.
  2. Close with \(L\), the top edge from \((2,2)\) back to \((0,2)\). Walking down the left side, the square is on your left, so \(C\) then \(L\) is counterclockwise. The double integral is \(+2\cdot\operatorname{area}=+2\cdot 4=8\).
  3. On \(L\), \(y=2\) and \(dy=0\), so \(\displaystyle\int_L\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}=\int_2^0\left(x^2+6\right)dx=-\left(\tfrac83+12\right)=-\tfrac{44}{3}\).
  4. \(\displaystyle\int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}=8-\left(-\tfrac{44}{3}\right)=\tfrac{68}{3}\).
Walk the loop before you write ±area. Decide the direction of the closed loop \(C+L\), not of \(C\) alone. A path listed by its vertices hides the direction: \((0,0)\to(0,3)\to(2,0)\to(0,0)\) goes up the \(y\)-axis first, which puts the triangle on your right, so it is clockwise and Green's theorem picks up a minus sign. Losing that sign is the most common way to get these problems wrong.

    Notice how much of \(P\) and \(Q\) never matters. Any term of \(P\) with no \(y\) in it dies under \(\partial/\partial y\), and any term of \(Q\) with no \(x\) in it dies under \(\partial/\partial x\). Those terms are free.

    Parametric surfaces and surface area

    A curve needs one parameter. A surface needs two. Everything else follows from that one fact.

    A parametric surface is a vector function of two variables,

    \(\mathbf{r}(u,v)=\langle x(u,v),\,y(u,v),\,z(u,v)\rangle,\qquad (u,v)\in D\)\(D\) is the parameter domain, a flat region in the \(uv\)-plane. The surface \(S\) is the image of \(D\).

    Freezing one parameter gives a grid curve. Fix \(u=u_0\) and let \(v\) run: you get a curve lying in \(S\). Fix \(v=v_0\) and let \(u\) run: another curve. The two families of grid curves are the mesh you see drawn on every surface picture.

    The parametrizations you should know cold

    SurfaceParametrizationDomain
    Plane through \(P\) spanned by \(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\)\(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{p}+u\,\mathbf{a}+v\,\mathbf{b}\)\(u,v\in\mathbb{R}\)
    Graph \(z=f(x,y)\)\(\mathbf{r}=\langle x,\,y,\,f(x,y)\rangle\)\((x,y)\in D\)
    Cylinder \(x^2+y^2=a^2\)\(\mathbf{r}=\langle a\cos u,\,a\sin u,\,v\rangle\)\(0\le u\le 2\pi\)
    Sphere of radius \(a\)\(\mathbf{r}=\langle a\sin\phi\cos\theta,\,a\sin\phi\sin\theta,\,a\cos\phi\rangle\)\(0\le\phi\le\pi,\ 0\le\theta\le 2\pi\)
    Cone \(z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\)\(\mathbf{r}=\langle u\cos v,\,u\sin v,\,u\rangle\)\(u\ge 0,\ 0\le v\le 2\pi\)
    Revolving \(y=f(x)\), \(a\le x\le b\), about the \(x\)-axis\(\mathbf{r}=\langle u,\,f(u)\cos v,\,f(u)\sin v\rangle\)\(a\le u\le b,\ 0\le v\le 2\pi\)

    Pattern: whenever a circle is involved, one parameter is the angle. Whenever the surface is a graph, the two parameters are just \(x\) and \(y\).

    Tangent vectors and the normal

    Differentiate along each grid curve:

    \(\mathbf{r}_u=\left\langle \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u},\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial u},\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial u}\right\rangle,\qquad \mathbf{r}_v=\left\langle \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial v},\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial v},\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial v}\right\rangle\)\(\mathbf{r}_u\) is tangent to the curve where \(v\) is frozen; \(\mathbf{r}_v\) is tangent to the curve where \(u\) is frozen. Both are tangent to \(S\).

    Two tangent vectors determine the tangent plane, and their cross product is perpendicular to it:

    \(\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v\)The normal vector. The surface is smooth where \(\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v\neq\mathbf{0}\). The tangent plane at \(\mathbf{r}(u_0,v_0)\) is the plane through that point with this normal.

    For a graph \(\mathbf{r}=\langle u,v,f(u,v)\rangle\) this collapses to something worth memorizing: \(\mathbf{r}_u=\langle 1,0,f_u\rangle\), \(\mathbf{r}_v=\langle 0,1,f_v\rangle\), so

    \(\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v=\langle -f_u,\,-f_v,\,1\rangle\)The \(z\)-component is \(+1\), so this normal always points upward. You will use it constantly in the flux chapter of Module 11.

    Where two surfaces meet

    Two surfaces that cross meet in a curve. That curve lies in both surfaces, so its tangent direction is perpendicular to both normals. For a level surface \(G(x,y,z)=c\) the normal is \(\nabla G\); for a parametric surface it is \(\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\). So a direction vector for the tangent line is their cross product:

    \(\mathbf{v}=\nabla G(P)\times\left(\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\right)(u_0,v_0)\)Find the \((u_0,v_0)\) that lands on \(P\) first; then the line is \(P+t\,\mathbf v\).

    Drag to rotate, scroll to zoom. Purple curves are the two grid curves through the marked point. Arrows are drawn to a common scale, not to true length.

    Put the point on the sphere's north pole (\(u=0\)): \(\mathbf{r}_v\) collapses to zero and the normal dies with it, which is why the sphere's parametrization is not smooth at the poles. On the cone, do the same at \(u=0\): the vertex is the one bad point. On the helicoid, notice \(|\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v|=\sqrt{1+u^2}\) grows as you move out along the ramp.

    The normal is not unique. Swapping the roles of \(u\) and \(v\) reverses the cross product: \(\mathbf{r}_v\times\mathbf{r}_u=-(\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v)\). For area it makes no difference, because area uses the length. For flux it flips the sign of the answer, so once you reach flux in Module 11 you must check which way your normal points.

    Surface area

    A small rectangle \(\Delta u\times\Delta v\) in the parameter domain maps to a small patch on \(S\) that is almost the parallelogram spanned by \(\mathbf{r}_u\,\Delta u\) and \(\mathbf{r}_v\,\Delta v\). The area of that parallelogram is \(|\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v|\,\Delta u\,\Delta v\). Add up and take the limit:

    \(A(S)=\displaystyle\iint_D |\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v|\,dA\)\(|\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v|\) is the stretch factor: how much area the map from \(D\) to \(S\) creates at each point.

    For a graph \(z=f(x,y)\), \(|\langle -f_x,-f_y,1\rangle|=\sqrt{1+f_x^2+f_y^2}\), which gives the version you have probably seen first:

    \(A(S)=\displaystyle\iint_D \sqrt{1+f_x^2+f_y^2}\;dA\)
    Do not integrate over the surface's shadow and forget the stretch. \(dA\) is area in the parameter domain; \(dS=|\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v|\,dA\) is area on the surface. Dropping that factor is the single most common error in this chapter, and it always makes the answer too small.

    Pieces of a sphere

    On the sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2=a^2\), use \(\phi\) and \(\theta\). Since \(z=a\cos\phi\), a cut \(z\ge h\) becomes \(0\le\phi\le\phi_0\) with \(\cos\phi_0=h/a\), and \(z\le h\) becomes \(\phi_0\le\phi\le\pi\). Sign conditions on \(x\) and \(y\) choose the \(\theta\) interval: \(x\ge 0\) and \(y\ge 0\) is \(0\) to \(\pi/2\), \(x\le 0\) and \(y\ge 0\) is \(\pi/2\) to \(\pi\), and so on around the circle. Since \(|\mathbf r_\phi\times\mathbf r_\theta|=a^2\sin\phi\), the area is a product of two easy integrals:

    \(A=a^2\,(\theta_2-\theta_1)\,(\cos\phi_1-\cos\phi_2)\)The \(\theta\) integral is the length of the \(\theta\) interval. The \(\phi\) integral is \(\int_{\phi_1}^{\phi_2}\sin\phi\,d\phi=\cos\phi_1-\cos\phi_2\).

    Surface integrals of scalar functions

    Same idea as a line integral, one dimension up: chop the surface, weight each piece by \(f\), add.

    Cut \(D\) into small rectangles, map them to patches on \(S\), multiply the value of \(f\) at a sample point by the patch's area, and add. The patch area is \(|\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v|\,\Delta u\,\Delta v\), so:

    \(\displaystyle\iint_S f\,dS=\iint_D f\big(\mathbf{r}(u,v)\big)\,\big|\mathbf{r}_u\times\mathbf{r}_v\big|\,dA\)Substitute the parametrization into \(f\), multiply by the stretch factor, integrate over the flat parameter domain.

    For a graph \(z=g(x,y)\) over a region \(D\) in the \(xy\)-plane:

    \(\displaystyle\iint_S f\,dS=\iint_D f\big(x,y,g(x,y)\big)\,\sqrt{1+g_x^2+g_y^2}\;dA\)The \(z\) inside \(f\) gets replaced by \(g(x,y)\). Nothing is left over the surface: everything is now a function of \(x\) and \(y\).

    Three things this computes:

    • Area, when \(f=1\).
    • Mass of a thin sheet with surface density \(\rho(x,y,z)\): \(m=\iint_S \rho\,dS\). Centers of mass and moments work the same way as in earlier chapters.
    • Average value of \(f\) on \(S\): \(\dfrac{1}{A(S)}\iint_S f\,dS\).

    If \(S\) is made of several smooth pieces (a box, a closed cylinder with its two lids), integrate over each piece and add. That is legal because area adds.

    Step by step: \(\iint_S z\,dS\) over a piece of a plane

      Notice where the work actually is: the stretch factor is a constant for a plane, so it slides straight out of the integral, and what remains is a first-year double integral of a linear function over a rectangle.

      Explore: how one parameter cell stretches

      Color shows the value of \(f\) on the surface: blue is low, orange is high. The purple outline is the image of one parameter cell \(\Delta u\times\Delta v\).

      Move the cell around and watch the two areas in the readout. On the plane the ratio is the same everywhere, which is why a plane's stretch factor comes out of the integral.

      Orientation does not matter here. \(dS\) is an area, always positive. Reversing \(u\) and \(v\), or walking the surface the other way, changes nothing: \(\iint_S f\,dS\) has one value. That is the difference between this chapter and the flux chapter in Module 11.
      Substitute before you integrate. \(\iint_S z\,dS\) over \(z=g(x,y)\) is not \(\iint_D z\,dA\). Replace \(z\) by \(g(x,y)\) and attach \(\sqrt{1+g_x^2+g_y^2}\). If any \(z\) is still standing after you set up the double integral, you have made this mistake.
