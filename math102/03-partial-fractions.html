Chapter 1 · Stewart 7.4

Proper first: polynomial long division

Partial fractions only works on a proper fraction. If the top is not smaller than the bottom, divide.

A rational function \(f(x)=P(x)/Q(x)\) is proper when \(\deg P<\deg Q\), and improper otherwise. Improper fractions have no decomposition into simple pieces, because the pieces all die off at infinity and an improper fraction does not. The fix is division:

\(\dfrac{P(x)}{Q(x)}=S(x)+\dfrac{R(x)}{Q(x)},\qquad \deg R<\deg Q\)\(S\) is the quotient, \(R\) the remainder. \(S\) is a polynomial, so you integrate it on sight. Only \(R/Q\) needs partial fractions, and it is proper by construction.

The division itself is the long division you learned for numbers. At each step you ask what the leading term of the divisor must be multiplied by to match the leading term of what is left, write that down as the next term of the quotient, multiply back, and subtract. Take \((x^3+x)/(x-1)\):

\(\dfrac{x^3+x}{x-1}=x^2+x+2+\dfrac{2}{x-1}\)Check it by multiplying out: \((x^2+x+2)(x-1)+2=x^3-1+2\cdot 1+\ldots\) or faster, plug in a number. At \(x=2\): left \(=10/1=10\), right \(=4+2+2+2=10\).
Degree check first, every time. Before you write a single \(A\) or \(B\), compare the degrees. Decomposing an improper fraction produces an unsolvable system, and the usual symptom is that the coefficients cannot be made to match no matter what you try.

Explore: the quotient is the shape at infinity

Slide anything and watch the two curves pull together at the edges of the window. The gap between them is exactly the remainder term \(R/(x-c)\), which goes to zero as \(|x|\) grows. Set the sliders so the remainder is 0 and the purple curve lands exactly on the orange line: the division came out even and there is nothing left to decompose.

Step-by-step: long division

Only the leading terms decide the next quotient term. Everything else just comes along for the subtraction. Stop as soon as what is left has lower degree than the divisor: that is the remainder.

Integrating the quotient is free. Once you have \(P/Q=S+R/Q\), the \(S\) part integrates term by term with the power rule. Only \(R/Q\) goes on to the next chapter.

Chapter 2 · Stewart 7.4

Distinct linear factors

When the denominator factors into different linear pieces, each piece gets one constant over it, and each constant falls out of a single substitution.

If \(Q(x)=(x-a)(x-b)\) with \(a\ne b\) and the fraction is proper, then there are numbers \(A\) and \(B\) with

\(\dfrac{px+q}{(x-a)(x-b)}=\dfrac{A}{x-a}+\dfrac{B}{x-b}\)One term per factor, and the numerator over a linear factor is a plain constant. The claim that such \(A\) and \(B\) always exist is the theorem; finding them is arithmetic.

Multiply both sides by \(Q(x)\) to clear denominators:

\(px+q=A(x-b)+B(x-a)\)This is an identity: it holds for every \(x\), not just for some special one. That is what lets you substitute whatever value you like.

Now the trick. Substituting \(x=a\) kills the \(B\) term, because \((x-a)\) is zero there:

\(A=\dfrac{pa+q}{a-b},\qquad B=\dfrac{pb+q}{b-a}\)The cover-up (Heaviside) method: to get \(A\), cover the factor \((x-a)\) in the original fraction and evaluate everything else at \(x=a\). To get \(B\), cover \((x-b)\) and evaluate at \(x=b\).

The fallback that always works is equating coefficients: expand the right side into \((A+B)x-(Ab+Ba)\) and match it against \(px+q\), giving \(A+B=p\) and \(Ab+Ba=-q\). Cover-up is faster; coefficient matching is what you use once quadratic factors show up.

Explore: the cover-up method, step by step

Purple: the original rational function. Orange and blue: the two simple pieces, drawn as you find them. Gray dashed: the vertical asymptotes.

Watch the picture as the two pieces appear. Each one has a single vertical asymptote, and away from the asymptotes the orange and blue heights add up to the purple height at every \(x\). That addition is what the decomposition says.

Proper first. The numerator degree must already be smaller than the denominator degree. If it is not, go back to chapter 1 and divide, then decompose the remainder term.

Integrating the pieces

Each piece is a log, and that is the whole point of the method:

\(\displaystyle\int\frac{px+q}{(x-a)(x-b)}\,dx=A\ln|x-a|+B\ln|x-b|+C\)Every \(\int dx/(x-r)\) is \(\ln|x-r|\). The absolute value matters: the antiderivative has to work on both sides of the asymptote.

For a definite integral, evaluate and then combine the logs. An answer like \(2\ln 3-2\ln 2\) is correct but not finished; \(\ln\tfrac94\) is the form WebAssign wants.

Not every linear factor is monic. \(2x^2+3x+1=(2x+1)(x+1)\), and the piece over \(2x+1\) integrates to \(\tfrac12\ln|2x+1|\), not \(\ln|2x+1|\). The chain rule charges you a \(1/2\) for the inner derivative.

Chapter 3 · Stewart 7.4

Repeated and irreducible quadratic factors

Two new cases, two new rules. Get the form right and the rest is the same algebra.

A repeated linear factor \((x-a)^k\) does not contribute one term. It contributes \(k\) of them, one for every power up to \(k\):

\((x-a)^k\;\Longrightarrow\;\dfrac{A_1}{x-a}+\dfrac{A_2}{(x-a)^2}+\cdots+\dfrac{A_k}{(x-a)^k}\)You need all \(k\) terms. Dropping the lower powers leaves a system with no solution, which is the usual way this mistake announces itself.

An irreducible quadratic \(x^2+bx+c\) (one with \(b^2-4c<0\), so it has no real roots) gets a linear numerator, not a constant:

\(x^2+bx+c\;\Longrightarrow\;\dfrac{Bx+C}{x^2+bx+c}\)Two unknowns for one factor. The rule of thumb: the numerator over a factor of degree \(d\) has degree \(d-1\).

A repeated quadratic \((x^2+bx+c)^m\) follows the same pattern as the repeated linear factor, with a linear numerator over each power:

\((x^2+bx+c)^m\;\Longrightarrow\;\dfrac{B_1x+C_1}{x^2+bx+c}+\cdots+\dfrac{B_mx+C_m}{(x^2+bx+c)^m}\)Count before you solve: the number of unknown constants always equals the degree of the denominator.

Explore: how many unknowns?

One curve per unknown constant. These are the building blocks the decomposition is made of, and every one of them has an antiderivative you know.

Build a denominator and read off the form. Notice that the count of unknowns always matches the degree of the denominator, and that pushing the repeated power up by one adds exactly one curve, while adding a quadratic adds two.

Integrating the quadratic pieces

A term \(\dfrac{Bx+C}{x^2+a^2}\) is two integrals wearing one coat. Split it:

\(\displaystyle\int\frac{Bx+C}{x^2+a^2}\,dx=\frac{B}{2}\ln(x^2+a^2)+\frac{C}{a}\arctan\frac{x}{a}+K\)The \(Bx\) part is a substitution \(u=x^2+a^2\) and gives a log, with no absolute value needed since \(x^2+a^2>0\). The constant part is the arctangent formula. Two different antiderivatives, so you must separate them.

Repeated linear pieces are just the power rule:

\(\displaystyle\int\frac{dx}{(x-a)^j}=\frac{-1}{(j-1)(x-a)^{j-1}}+K\quad (j\ge 2)\)Only \(j=1\) gives a logarithm. Every higher power gives a negative power back.

When the quadratic has a middle term, complete the square first: \(x^2+4x+13=(x+2)^2+9\), then substitute \(u=x+2\) and you are back to the standard shapes above.

Do not force a quadratic to factor. Check the discriminant. \(x^2+4\) is irreducible and takes \((Bx+C)/(x^2+4)\); \(x^2-4\) factors into \((x-2)(x+2)\) and takes two constants. Treating one like the other is the most expensive slip in this section.

Chapter 4 · Stewart 7.4

Rationalizing substitutions

A radical of a linear expression is not a rational function. One substitution turns it into one, and then this whole chapter applies.

When the integrand contains \(\sqrt[n]{ax+b}\), let \(u\) be that radical. Everything becomes polynomial:

\(u=\sqrt{ax+b}\;\Longrightarrow\; u^2=ax+b,\quad x=\dfrac{u^2-b}{a},\quad dx=\dfrac{2u}{a}\,du\)Solve for \(x\) and differentiate. The \(2u\) in \(dx\) is what cancels the radical in the denominator and leaves a rational function of \(u\).

Then decompose in \(u\), integrate in \(u\), and back-substitute at the very end. The worked example is Stewart's:

\(\displaystyle\int\frac{\sqrt{x+4}}{x}\,dx=2\sqrt{x+4}+2\ln\left|\frac{\sqrt{x+4}-2}{\sqrt{x+4}+2}\right|+C\)With \(u=\sqrt{x+4}\) the integrand becomes \(\dfrac{2u^2}{u^2-4}\), which is improper: divide to get \(2+\dfrac{8}{u^2-4}\), then decompose \(\dfrac{8}{(u-2)(u+2)}=\dfrac{2}{u-2}-\dfrac{2}{u+2}\).

Step-by-step: the whole substitution

Three families run through here. Two of them need partial fractions after the substitution and one does not: watch for the step where the \(u\) in \(dx=2u\,du\) cancels something and the integrand collapses to a polynomial. Recognizing which case you are in before you start saves the most time.

Change the limits or change back. For a definite integral you may either convert the limits to \(u\) values right away, or keep them in \(x\) and back-substitute before evaluating. Mixing the two, evaluating a \(u\) antiderivative at \(x\) limits, is the classic error.
