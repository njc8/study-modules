Chapter 1 · Review sections 1 and 2

Asymptotics and little-o

Your course compares sizes with two symbols, \(\sim\) and \(o\). Learn to read them, and the hard comparison problems become two or three lines.

\(f\sim g\iff\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=1,\qquad\qquad f=o(g)\iff\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=0\)\(f\sim g\) says they are the same size. \(f=o(g)\) says \(f\) is negligible next to \(g\). A leading term is a simpler function asymptotic to the original one.

If the ratio tends to some other positive constant, \(f/g\to c\), then \(f\sim cg\), not \(f\sim g\). So \(3x^2+x\sim 3x^2\), and \(3x^2+x\) is not asymptotic to \(x^2\). The theorems in the next chapter accept \(f\sim cg\), so constants never affect convergence, but they do matter when you write the symbol.

Who wins as \(x\to\infty\)

  • Powers: higher powers dominate lower ones. \(x^p\) grows if \(p>0\), and tends to \(0\) if \(p\lt 0\).
  • Exponentials: \(a^x\) grows if \(a>1\), and tends to \(0\) if \(0\lt a\lt 1\). Exponential growth dominates powers and logs. Exponential decay beats inverse powers.
  • The three facts: \((\ln x)^q=o(x^p)\) for \(p,q>0\); \(\;x^p=o(a^x)\) for \(a>1\); \(\;x^pr^x\to 0\) for \(0\lt r\lt 1\).
  • Bounded pieces: \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) stay bounded, and \(\arctan x\to\pi/2\).
  • Logs of products: \(\ln(xa^x)=\ln x+x\ln a\), so simplify first. The leading term is \(x\ln a\).

The five-step procedure

  1. Separate the expression at its outermost sums.
  2. Compare whole summands, not isolated factors. In \(x^5(0.5)^x\), the \(x^5\) does not get to compete alone.
  3. Choose a candidate leading term and divide the whole expression by it.
  4. Preserve outer operations. A dominant term inside a square root still gets square-rooted.
  5. Verify that the ratio tends to \(1\), and restore a missing constant if it does not.

Try each candidate on the first two presets, both from the worked quiz on your review sheet. The right one heads for the green line, slowly when logs are involved, since \(\arctan x/\ln x\) shrinks only as fast as \(\ln x\) grows. A candidate that is too small makes the ratio run off the top, and one that is too big sends it to \(0\), which is exactly the statement that the expression is \(o\) of it. Then watch the readout: a term that looks huge at \(x=5\), like \(x^5(0.5)^x\), is gone by \(x=60\).

Two facts your practice exam needs that the sheet does not list. As \(u\to 0\): \(\ln(1+u)\sim u\) and \(\sin u\sim u\). L'Hospital proves both. So \(\ln\dfrac{k^2+1}{k^2-1}=\ln\!\left(1+\dfrac{2}{k^2-1}\right)\sim\dfrac{2}{k^2}\). That one line is the whole key to problem 7(b).
\(o\) is one-directional, and it is a lot of functions. Every function is \(o\) of many things: \(x^2=o(x^3)\) and \(x^2=o(e^x)\) are both true. So a question that matches \(f\) to \(g\) with \(f=o(g)\) can have several right answers. Also \(x^2\) is not \(o(x^2)\): the ratio is \(1\), not \(0\). And asymptotics do not survive subtraction, which the Series Toolkit covers.

The comparison theorems, by their course names

Three theorems, each with an integral version and a series version. The exam wants the name and the conditions, so learn them as a set.

TheoremConditionsConclusion
Direct Comparison TestIntegrals: \(f,g\) continuous, \(f(x)\ge g(x)\ge 0\) on \([a,\infty)\). Series: \(0\le a_n\le b_n\) for all \(n\ge M\).Larger converges \(\Rightarrow\) smaller converges. Smaller diverges \(\Rightarrow\) larger diverges.
Asymptotic Comparison Test
Stewart: Limit Comparison Test		Integrals: \(f,g\) continuous and positive on \([a,\infty)\), \(f\sim cg\) with \(c>0\). Series: both eventually positive, \(a_n\sim cb_n\) with \(c\ne 0\).Both converge or both diverge.
Little-o Comparison TestIntegrals: \(f,g\) continuous and positive on \([a,\infty)\), \(f=o(g)\). Series: both eventually positive, \(a_n=o(b_n)\).Big one converges \(\Rightarrow\) little one converges. Little one diverges \(\Rightarrow\) big one diverges.
Directions. Direct and little-o comparison each give two useful statements and two useless ones. Convergence of the smaller says nothing about the larger, and divergence of the larger says nothing about the smaller. \(\dfrac{1}{n^2}\le\dfrac1n\) and \(\dfrac1{n^2}=o\!\left(\dfrac1n\right)\), yet the first series converges and the second diverges.

Comparison also works at a blow-up

The sheet states direct comparison for Type I and adds that a similar theorem holds for Type II: use continuous nonnegative functions on the relevant one-sided interval, and compare them as \(x\to c^-\) or \(x\to c^+\). Your benchmark then flips, because near \(0\) a small exponent is the safe one:

\(\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^p}\text{ converges}\iff p>1,\qquad\qquad\int_0^1\frac{dx}{x^p}\text{ converges}\iff p\lt 1\)\(x^{-1/2}\) is integrable near \(0\) but not near infinity. \(x^{-1}\) fails at both ends.

Example, from your Week 7 miniexam: on \((0,\pi]\), \(\;0\le\dfrac{\sin^2x}{\sqrt x}\le\dfrac{1}{\sqrt x}\). Since \(\int_0^\pi x^{-1/2}\,dx\) converges (\(p=\tfrac12\lt 1\)), the Direct Comparison Test says \(\int_0^\pi\frac{\sin^2 x}{\sqrt x}\,dx\) converges.

Little-o is the tool for logarithms

\(\dfrac{\ln n}{n^2}\) is not asymptotic to any \(1/n^p\), so Asymptotic Comparison has nothing to grab. But you only need room to spare. Pick an exponent strictly between \(1\) and \(2\): \(\dfrac{\ln n}{n^2}\Big/\dfrac{1}{n^{3/2}}=\dfrac{\ln n}{n^{1/2}}\to 0\), so \(\dfrac{\ln n}{n^2}=o\!\left(n^{-3/2}\right)\). The \(p\)-series with \(p=\tfrac32\) converges, so the Little-o Comparison Test finishes it.

For divergence, run it the other way: \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt n\,\ln n}\) beats \(\dfrac1n\), since \(\dfrac1n\Big/\dfrac{1}{\sqrt n\ln n}=\dfrac{\ln n}{\sqrt n}\to 0\). So \(\dfrac1n=o\!\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt n\ln n}\right)\), the little one diverges, and so does the big one.

The family on your practice exam. \(\displaystyle\sum\frac{1}{k^p\ln k}\) converges exactly when \(p>1\). For \(p>1\) compare directly with \(1/k^p\), since \(\ln k\ge 1\) for \(k\ge 3\). At \(p=1\) use the Integral Test, with \(u=\ln x\) giving \(\ln\ln x\to\infty\). For \(p\lt 1\) compare directly from below with the \(p=1\) case. A log changes the verdict only at \(p=1\) exactly.

Sequences and partial sums

Before you pick a test, ask what object you are looking at. A sequence, a series, and a partial sum take different questions, and the exam will hand you each one.

A sequence can diverge by growing or by oscillating: \((-1)^n\) is bounded and diverges, while \((-1)^n/n\to 0\). A series is defined through its partial sums \(S_N=\sum_{n=1}^N a_n\), and it converges to \(S\) exactly when \(S_N\to S\). The limit of the terms is not the sum.

Recovering terms from partial sums

\(a_1=S_1,\qquad a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}\ \ (n\ge 2),\qquad\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n=\lim_{k\to\infty}S_k\)The sum comes straight from the partial sum formula. You never need to add up the \(a_n\).

Given \(S_k=\dfrac{k-8}{k+8}\): \(\;a_1=S_1=-\dfrac79\), and for \(n\ge 2\), \(\;a_n=\dfrac{n-8}{n+8}-\dfrac{n-9}{n+7}=\dfrac{16}{(n+7)(n+8)}\). The sum is \(\lim S_k=1\).

The trap. The difference formula needs \(S_{n-1}\), and the given formula does not define \(S_0\). So \(a_n\) for \(n\ge 2\) may not extend to \(n=1\): here it would give \(\tfrac{16}{72}=\tfrac29\), while the real \(a_1\) is \(-\tfrac79\). Always compute \(a_1\) separately.

Recursive sequences: generate terms first

For \(a_1=1,\ a_2=2,\ a_{n+2}=a_{n+1}-a_n\), do not start by taking a limit. Write the terms out: \(1,2,1,-1,-2,-1,1,2,\dots\) The pair \((a_7,a_8)=(1,2)\) is the same as the first pair, so the recurrence produces the same terms again. The sequence is periodic and nonconstant, so it diverges.

Each dot is one term \(a_n\).

Drag \(a_1\) and \(a_2\) on the first rule. The size changes, but the period is always \(6\) unless you start at \((0,0)\). Then switch rules. The averaging rule settles down, and the Fibonacci rule blows up. Nothing about the look of a rule tells you which it is, which is why you generate terms first. Solving \(L=g(L)\) from Module 9 only helps once you know the limit exists.

Writing a closed formula

Your practice exam asks for a closed formula for \(\sin(1),\ \ln(2),\ \sin(3),\ \ln(4),\dots\) The tool is a switch built from \((-1)^n\):

\(\dfrac{1-(-1)^n}{2}=\begin{cases}1&n\text{ odd}\\0&n\text{ even}\end{cases}\qquad\qquad\dfrac{1+(-1)^n}{2}=\begin{cases}0&n\text{ odd}\\1&n\text{ even}\end{cases}\)Multiply each pattern by the switch for its positions, then add. Here \(a_n=\frac{1-(-1)^n}{2}\sin n+\frac{1+(-1)^n}{2}\ln n\).

Building a sequence on demand

"Find \(f(n)\) with \(f(n)e^n\to 0\)" is a little-o question in disguise: you need \(f=o(e^{-n})\). Anything that dies faster than \(e^{-n}\) works, such as \(f(n)=e^{-2n}\), since \(e^{-2n}e^{n}=e^{-n}\to 0\). The justification is that one computed limit. Note that \(f(n)=e^{-n}\) fails, because the product is \(1\).

Remainder estimates

"Find the smallest \(N\) you can" is asking which theorem gives you the best bound you can justify. Name the theorem, check its conditions, solve one inequality.

For a convergent series with sum \(S\), the remainder is \(R_N=S-S_N=\sum_{k=N+1}^\infty a_k\), and the absolute error is \(|R_N|\). To guarantee a tolerance \(\varepsilon\), find a justified upper bound \(U_N\ge|R_N|\) and require \(U_N\le\varepsilon\).

\(|R_N|\le b_{N+1}\)Alternating Series Remainder Theorem. Conditions: \(a_k=(-1)^kb_k\) or \((-1)^{k+1}b_k\), with \(b_k>0\), \(b_{k+1}\le b_k\), and \(b_k\to 0\). Also \(S\) lies between \(S_N\) and \(S_{N+1}\), and the error has the sign of the first omitted term.
\(\displaystyle\int_{n+1}^\infty f(x)\,dx\le R_n\le\int_n^\infty f(x)\,dx\)Integral Test Remainder Theorem. Conditions: \(\sum a_k\) converges, \(f(k)=a_k\), and \(f\) is positive, continuous, and decreasing for \(x\ge n\).
\(\displaystyle R^f_n\le R^g_n\le\int_n^\infty g(x)\,dx\)Direct Comparison Remainder Theorem. Conditions: \(\sum f(k)\) and \(\sum g(k)\) converge, \(g(k)\ge f(k)\ge 0\) for \(k\ge N\), and \(g\) is positive, continuous, and decreasing on \([N,\infty)\). This is for an \(f\) you cannot integrate.
Sign-changing terms: the point of practice problem 6. \(\dfrac{\cos k+\cos^2k+\cos^3k}{k^3}\) can be negative, so the Direct Comparison Remainder Theorem does not apply to it directly, and neither does the alternating theorem, because the signs of \(\cos k\) do not alternate regularly. Pass to absolute values first. The tail of a series is at most the tail of its absolute values: \(|R_N|\le\sum_{k>N}|a_k|\). Now \(|a_k|\le\dfrac{3}{k^3}\), so \(|R_N|\le\displaystyle\int_N^\infty\frac{3}{x^3}\,dx=\frac{3}{2N^2}\le 0.001\) gives \(N\ge\sqrt{1500}\approx 38.7\), and \(N=39\).

Sufficient, or actually smallest?

A bound tells you when you are safe. It does not say smaller \(N\) fail. Be exact about which you are claiming:

  • For \(\sum(-1)^k/k^2\) with tolerance \(0.001\): \(N=31\) is the smallest \(N\) certified by the theorem. It need not be the actual smallest \(N\) whose error meets the tolerance.
  • For \(\sum k^{-2}\): \(\frac{1}{N+1}\lt R_N\lt\frac1N\). So \(N=1000\) works, and \(R_{999}>\frac{1}{1000}\). Positive tails decrease as \(N\) grows, so every smaller index fails too. Here \(N=1000\) is the actual minimum, because you also have a lower bound.
  • For \(\sum e^{1/k}/k^3\): \(e^{1/k}\le e\) gives \(R_N\le\frac{e}{2N^2}\), so \(N\ge\sqrt{500e}\approx 36.87\) and \(N=37\) suffices. This does not prove \(37\) is the minimum.

Explore: the true error against the bound

Log scales on both axes. Purple dots: the actual \(|R_N|\). Orange: the theorem's upper bound \(U_N\). Green dashed: your tolerance \(\varepsilon\).

On \(\sum k^{-2}\) the dots sit right under the bound, so the certified \(N\) is within one of the true one. Now try the alternating preset: the bound is still honest, but the true error is about half of it, so the true smallest \(N\) is lower. On the last two the gap is wider, because the comparison threw information away. That gap is what "smallest \(N\) you can" means: the smallest you can justify, not the smallest that exists.

Chapter 5 · Review sections 5 and 7

Writing full-credit answers

The sheet's first line is the instruction that matters most: state the theorem name for any used theorem, and mention its conditions. A correct verdict with no theorem behind it is not the answer they want.

The final answer template

Five short lines, in this order, on every comparison problem:

  1. State the simple comparison object \(b_n\) or \(g(x)\).
  2. State the theorem you are using, and mention all its conditions.
  3. Show the decisive inequality or ratio limit.
  4. State why the benchmark converges or diverges, naming its test.
  5. State the conclusion.

Step-by-step: the template on real problems

Cycles through the two worked quizzes on your review sheet and two practice exam problems. Before pressing Next, say out loud what the next line has to be.

Which test? The sheet's order

  1. Identify the object. Sequence, series, partial sum, improper integral, or remainder? Do not apply a series test to a sequence.
  2. Look at \(a_n\). If \(a_n\not\to 0\), the Divergence Test finishes it. If \(a_n\to 0\), no conclusion follows yet.
  3. Recognize a benchmark. Constant ratio: geometric, check \(|r|\lt 1\). \(1/n^p\): \(p\)-series, check \(p>1\). Alternating sign: test absolute convergence first, then check all three AST conditions if you need to.
  4. Fractions and composites: find leading behavior first. Numerator and denominator separately, whole summands, keep roots and logs. Then \(a_n\sim C/n^p\) compares with a \(p\)-series, and \(a_n\sim Cr^n\) with a geometric series. Use the Integral Test when \(f\) is manageable.
  5. Factorials or adjacent exponential powers: Ratio Test. If \(L=1\), choose another test.
  6. Error or number-of-terms questions: name the remainder theorem first, check conditions, solve, and say whether your \(N\) is merely sufficient or provably the minimum.
A failed test is not a verdict. If an AST condition fails, or an Integral Test condition fails, the test is unavailable. That alone does not prove divergence. For divergence you need the Divergence Test or a comparison.
\(\cos(n\pi)=(-1)^n\) and \(\cos(2n\pi)=1\). Rewrite these before choosing a test. The signs of \(\cos n\) do not alternate regularly, so \(\sum\cos(n)/n^2\) is an absolute convergence problem with direct comparison, not an AST problem.
