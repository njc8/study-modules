Chapter 1 · Orientation

What this unit is actually for

Every problem in this unit asks one question: if you keep adding terms forever, does the running total settle on a number? Every test is a shortcut for answering that without doing the infinite addition.

The honest case for caring comes in three parts.

1. Your calculator runs on series

When you press \(\sin(0.3)\), the calculator does not look anything up. It adds a few terms of a series and stops:

\(\sin x = x-\dfrac{x^3}{3!}+\dfrac{x^5}{5!}-\dfrac{x^7}{7!}+\dfrac{x^9}{9!}-\cdots\)Five terms give \(\sin(0.3)\) correct to 12 decimal places. Knowing that five is enough, and that the infinite sum really equals \(\sin x\), is exactly what this unit and the next one teach.

The same goes for \(e^x\), \(\ln x\), \(\arctan x\), and the digits of \(\pi\). That is the next unit (Taylor series). This unit is the safety check underneath it: it tells you when an infinite sum is an actual number, and how many terms you need before you can stop. Without it, "add terms until the answer looks stable" will sometimes hand you a confident wrong answer. The harmonic series \(1+\tfrac12+\tfrac13+\cdots\) looks like it is slowing to a stop, and it never stops growing.

2. Anything that repeats with a fixed fraction left over is a geometric series

A drug your body clears by a fixed fraction each day, money that loses a fixed fraction of its value each year, a ball that bounces back to a fixed fraction of its height. Each one is a sum \(a+ar+ar^2+\cdots\), and the long-run total takes one line of algebra: it is \(a/(1-r)\).

Explore: a daily dose that never quite clears

You take a dose \(D\) every day, and a fraction \(r\) of what is in your body is still there a day later. Blue dots: the amount right after each dose. Orange dots: the amount just before the next one. The dashed green line is the ceiling \(\dfrac{D}{1-r}\).

After day \(n\) the amount is \(D+Dr+\cdots+Dr^{n-1}\), a partial sum of a geometric series. Set \(r=0.5\): the level climbs fast and stops at twice the dose. Now drag \(r\) toward \(1\) (a drug the body clears slowly). The ceiling shoots up, and it takes far longer to get there. At \(r=1\) nothing ever clears and the total grows forever: that is the edge of \(|r|<1\), where geometric series stop converging. Pharmacists call this ceiling the steady state.

3. The unit is smaller than it looks

It feels like an endless list of tests. It is really about seven shapes of term, and each shape has the one test that fits it. Once you can see the shape, most problems take three lines. This table is the whole unit. The last chapter of this module is the same table with every theorem's fine print.

Adding two series together

You can add convergent series term by term. The interesting questions are what happens when one or both of them diverge.

Remember what \(\sum a_n\) means: the limit of the partial sums \(A_N=a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_N\). Write \(B_N\) for the partial sums of \(\sum b_n\) and \(C_N\) for those of \(\sum(a_n+b_n)\). A finite sum can be regrouped any way you like, so for every \(N\):

\(C_N=(a_1+b_1)+\cdots+(a_N+b_N)=A_N+B_N\)This is the only fact the whole chapter needs. Every question about \(\sum(a_n+b_n)\) becomes a question about the limit of \(A_N+B_N\), which is an ordinary limit of sequences.

That gives three cases.

Start with the third preset. The orange total climbs forever and the blue total falls forever, yet the green total, their sum, levels off at \(\pi^2/6\): two divergent series whose sum converges. Then try the second preset. The convergent blue total flattens out, so it has nothing left to cancel the orange climb with, and the green total climbs too. No choice of series can change that.

The proof template: run linearity backwards

The "convergent plus divergent" fact, and a whole family like it, are proved with one move. Linearity says combinations of convergent series converge. Run it backwards: if a series you know diverges could be written as a combination of series that converge, it would have to converge, which is impossible.

The step-by-step card below runs this template on relatives of the homework statement. Each one needs the same four steps.

Step-by-step: a linearity proof

The creative step is always step 2: write the series you know about as a sum or difference of series you are assuming converge. After that, linearity does the work.

Linearity needs both pieces to converge first. \(\sum\left(\frac1n-\frac1n\right)=0\), but you cannot write that as \(\sum\frac1n-\sum\frac1n\), because that is \(\infty-\infty\). Splitting a series into pieces is only legal once you know each piece converges. Chapter 3 leans on this: split, check every piece, and if exactly one piece diverges, the whole series diverges by the theorem above.

Building examples on purpose

To show that two divergent series can have a convergent sum, do not hunt for an example. Build one. Pick any convergent series \(\sum c_n\) and any divergent series \(\sum a_n\), and set \(b_n=c_n-a_n\). Then \(\sum b_n\) diverges (a convergent series minus a divergent one), and \(a_n+b_n=c_n\), which converges. Every example of this kind is this recipe in disguise.

Starting points do not matter for convergence. Changing or dropping finitely many terms never changes whether a series converges. It only changes the sum. So \(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n\) and \(\sum_{n=7}^{\infty}a_n\) converge or diverge together, and \(\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}a_n=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n-a_1-a_2\).

Exact values: hunting for geometric series

Only two kinds of series in this course have sums you can compute exactly: geometric and telescoping. When a problem asks for an exact value, expect geometric pieces in disguise.

\(\displaystyle\sum_{n=n_0}^{\infty} C\,r^{\,n}=\frac{\text{first term}}{1-r}=\frac{C\,r^{\,n_0}}{1-r}\qquad\text{when }|r|<1\)Get the first term by plugging the starting index into the term. Do not memorize separate versions for \(n=0\) and \(n=1\). For \(|r|\ge 1\) the series diverges.

The recipe

  1. Split the numerator. \(\dfrac{p+q+s}{D}=\dfrac{p}{D}+\dfrac{q}{D}+\dfrac{s}{D}\).
  2. Force every piece into the form \(C\,r^{n}\). The exponent rules do all the work: \(b^{-n}=\left(\tfrac1b\right)^{n}\), \(\;b^{2n}=(b^2)^{n}\), \(\;b^{n+k}=b^k\cdot b^{n}\), \(\;\dfrac{p^n}{q^n}=\left(\dfrac pq\right)^{n}\). A plain constant counts: \(\dfrac{1}{b^{2n}}=\left(\dfrac{1}{b^2}\right)^{n}\).
  3. Check \(|r|<1\) for every piece. If one piece has \(|r|\ge 1\) and the others converge, the whole series diverges (Chapter 2). Stop there.
  4. Sum each piece with \(\dfrac{\text{first term}}{1-r}\), then add.

Pick base \(e\) with power \(2n\), so the denominator is \(e^{2n}\approx 7.389^{\,n}\). Then slide \(d\) from \(7\) to \(8\): the green piece \(\left(\frac{d}{e^2}\right)^n\) jumps outside the band, and the partial sums take off. Next change only the starting index and watch the total move while every ratio stays put. The start changes the first term, never \(r\).

Step-by-step: an exact value

Press New series a few times. Some of them diverge on purpose: one piece's ratio is at least \(1\), and step 3 is where you catch it.

The first term is where the points go. \(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\tfrac14\right)^{n}\) starts at \(\tfrac14\), so it sums to \(\dfrac{1/4}{1-1/4}=\dfrac13\). Writing \(\dfrac{1}{1-1/4}=\dfrac43\) is the most common mistake in this whole chapter. It quietly assumes the series starts at \(n=0\).
Nothing in the numerator is left over. A \(1\) on top of \(b^{n}\) is the geometric piece \(\left(\frac1b\right)^{n}\). A \(c^{-n}\) on top of \(b^{n}\) is \(\left(\frac{1}{cb}\right)^{n}\). Every piece of the numerator becomes its own geometric series.

Telescoping: write it out and watch it collapse

In a telescoping series nearly every piece cancels against a later piece. Partial fractions create the cancellation, and writing out the terms shows you what survives.

The pattern is a term of the form \(a_n=f(n)-f(n+k)\). In the partial sum, the \(-f(n+k)\) from row \(n\) cancels the \(+f(n+k)\) that appears \(k\) rows later. Only the ends survive:

\(\displaystyle s_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\big[f(n)-f(n+k)\big]=\underbrace{f(1)+\cdots+f(k)}_{\text{first }k\text{ pieces}}\;-\;\underbrace{\big[f(N+1)+\cdots+f(N+k)\big]}_{\text{last }k\text{ pieces}}\)\(k\) survivors at the front, \(k\) at the back. If \(f(N)\to 0\), the back ones vanish as \(N\to\infty\), and the front ones are the sum.

Step 1: partial fractions with a gap

\(\dfrac{1}{(n+a)(n+b)}=\dfrac{1}{b-a}\left[\dfrac{1}{n+a}-\dfrac{1}{n+b}\right]\)The gap is \(k=b-a\), and it decides how many survivors there are. The factor \(\frac{1}{b-a}\) in front is the part people forget. Check it by recombining: \(\frac{1}{n+a}-\frac{1}{n+b}=\frac{b-a}{(n+a)(n+b)}\).

The fast way to get the constants is the cover-up method. For \(\dfrac{1}{(n+1)(n+4)}=\dfrac{A}{n+1}+\dfrac{B}{n+4}\): cover the factor \((n+1)\) and set \(n=-1\) in what is left, so \(A=\frac{1}{-1+4}=\frac13\). Cover \((n+4)\) and set \(n=-4\): \(B=\frac{1}{-4+1}=-\frac13\). So the term is \(\frac13\left[\frac{1}{n+1}-\frac{1}{n+4}\right]\), a gap of \(3\).

Explore: the cancellation grid

The series \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n+s)(n+s+k)}=\frac1k\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{1}{n+s}-\frac{1}{n+s+k}\right]\), written out row by row.

survives at the frontsurvives at the backthe pair cancelling now

Press Cancel pairs and watch each negative piece meet its positive twin exactly \(k\) rows down. Then raise the gap \(k\): the number of survivors at each end is always \(k\). Raise \(N\): the front survivors never change, the back survivors just shift along and shrink toward \(0\). That is why the sum is the front survivors times \(\frac1k\).

Step-by-step: sum a telescoping series

Write the first few rows and the last few rows, never the middle. The pattern of cancellation is decided entirely by the gap.

Find \(s_N\) first, then take the limit. Never cancel inside an infinite sum by splitting it into two separate series: \(\sum\frac{1}{n+1}-\sum\frac{1}{n+4}\) is \(\infty-\infty\) (both pieces diverge, see Chapter 2). The finite partial sum is where cancelling is legal.

Chapter 5 · Week 9 HW 3

Asymptotics, and the one place they lie

Two sequences are asymptotic when they are the same size up to a percentage error that shrinks to zero. That is perfect for products and quotients, and useless the moment you subtract.

\(a_n\sim b_n\quad\text{means}\quad\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n}{b_n}=1\)Read it as "\(a_n\) and \(b_n\) agree to within a percentage that goes to \(0\)". It does not say \(a_n-b_n\to 0\). To show two sequences are not asymptotic, show the ratio tends to something other than \(1\), or has no limit, or is not even defined.

Finding the simplest asymptotic form

Keep the dominant piece of every factor and throw the rest away:

  • \(\dfrac{3n^2+5n}{n+7}\sim\dfrac{3n^2}{n}=3n\)
  • \(\sqrt{4n^2+n}\sim\sqrt{4n^2}=2n\)
  • \(\sqrt{n+2}\,\sqrt{n+7}\sim\sqrt{n}\,\sqrt{n}=n\)
  • \(\dfrac{2^n+n^5}{3^n}\sim\dfrac{2^n}{3^n}=\left(\dfrac23\right)^{n}\) (exponentials beat powers)

Asymptotics are safe for products, quotients, and powers: if \(a_n\sim b_n\) and \(c_n\sim d_n\), then \(a_nc_n\sim b_nd_n\) and \(\frac{a_n}{c_n}\sim\frac{b_n}{d_n}\). They are safe for limits: if \(a_n\sim b_n\) and \(b_n\to L\), then \(a_n\to L\), because \(a_n=\frac{a_n}{b_n}\cdot b_n\to 1\cdot L\). And the limit comparison test is this idea applied to series: for positive terms, \(a_n\sim b_n\) means \(\sum a_n\) and \(\sum b_n\) converge or diverge together.

Where they lie: subtraction

Take \(c_n=\sqrt{n+2}\,\sqrt{n+6}\). It is asymptotic to \(n\). So it is tempting to say \(\lim(c_n-n)=\lim(n-n)=0\). The true limit is \(4.\)

Look at real numbers. At \(n=1{,}000{,}000\), \(c_n=1{,}000{,}003.999998\). The ratio \(c_n/n=1.000004\), which is \(1\) to five decimal places. That is exactly what \(\sim\) promises, and it delivers. But the gap \(c_n-n\) is about \(4\), and it stays near \(4\) forever. The relation \(\sim\) only controls the error relative to \(n\). When you subtract \(n\), the \(n\)'s cancel, and the error is all that is left: the exact part that \(\sim\) threw away.

\(a_n\sim b_n\quad\not\Longrightarrow\quad a_n-c_n\sim b_n-c_n\)Asymptotic is a statement about ratios. Subtraction can turn a small relative error into the entire answer.

Explore: the ratio goes to 1, the gap does not go to 0

the ratio \(c_n/n\)

the gap \(c_n-n\)

Left: the ratio comes down to \(1\), so \(c_n\sim n\) is true. Right: the gap settles at \(\frac{p+q}{2}\), not at the dashed orange line \(0\) that "\(n-n\)" predicts. Change \(p\) and \(q\) and watch the ratio picture barely change while the gap picture moves. The ratio cannot see the thing the gap measures.

The fix: the conjugate trick

\(A-B=\dfrac{(A-B)(A+B)}{A+B}=\dfrac{A^2-B^2}{A+B}\)Squaring removes the square roots, so the cancellation happens in plain algebra where you can see it. The denominator is now a sum, where nothing cancels, so asymptotics are safe there.

Step-by-step: a limit that asymptotics get wrong

Step 1 is the habit to build: before you simplify, ask whether the two pieces you are subtracting are asymptotic to each other. If they are, the naive answer is wrong and you need the conjugate.

How to see the trap coming. Two pieces that are asymptotic to each other, being subtracted. Whenever the simplest forms cancel to \(0\), the real answer lives in the next-order terms, and you need algebra (a conjugate, or a common denominator) to get at it.

When the integral test is the wrong tool

The integral test is a theorem with three hypotheses. When they fail, the integral and the series can disagree completely, and neither one tells you anything about the other.

If \(f\) is positive, continuous, and decreasing on \([m,\infty)\) and \(a_n=f(n)\), then \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}a_n\) and \(\displaystyle\int_m^{\infty}f(x)\,dx\) both converge or both diverge.The integral test. The hypotheses only need to hold eventually, from some \(m\) on. The test never says the sum equals the integral.

Each hypothesis has a job.

  • Decreasing makes the sandwich work. The rectangle of height \(f(n)\) over \([n,n+1]\) sits above the curve, and the one over \([n-1,n]\) sits below it. So the sum and the integral stay within one term of each other: finite together or infinite together.
  • Positive stops areas from cancelling. With sign changes, positive and negative area can cancel in the integral in a way the sum does not, or the other way round.
  • Continuous makes the integral exist in the first place.

The series only sees the dots

\(\sum a_n\) uses only the values \(f(1), f(2), f(3),\dots\). The integral uses every value in between. Infinitely many functions pass through the same dots, so pick one that wiggles between the integers and the integral picks up area the series never sees.

Explore: same dots, different integral

\(f(x)=\dfrac{1}{x^2}+A\sin^2(2\pi x)\). Since \(\sin(2\pi n)=0\), the purple dots are \(f(n)=\dfrac{1}{n^2}\) for every \(A\). The shaded region is \(\displaystyle\int_1^{b}f(x)\,dx\).

At \(A=0\) the function is decreasing, and the integral and the sum both level off: the test applies and they agree. Now raise \(A\). The dots do not move, so the series still converges to \(\pi^2/6\). But every bump adds area, the integral grows like \(\frac{A}{2}(b-1)\), and it diverges. The integral test has no opinion here, because this \(f\) is not decreasing.

"Not a counterexample" is a logic statement. A theorem promises its conclusion only when its hypotheses hold. A case where a hypothesis fails and the conclusion also fails does not contradict the theorem. It just lies outside it. To explain why something is not a counterexample, name the hypothesis that fails.

Simplify first: trig at integer multiples of \(\pi\)

Before choosing any test, plug in integers. Trig functions at multiples of \(\pi\) are just numbers, and a series that looks frightening often turns out to be geometric, alternating, or all zeros.

Integrals of squared trig functions use the half-angle identity: \(\sin^2\theta=\frac{1-\cos 2\theta}{2}\) and \(\cos^2\theta=\frac{1+\cos 2\theta}{2}\). Over a whole number of periods the cosine part integrates to \(0\), so the average value is exactly \(\frac12\), and an integral of length \(L\) over whole periods is \(\frac{L}{2}\).

Checking "decreasing" with a derivative

Show \(f'(x)<0\) from some point on. "Eventually" is enough, because dropping finitely many terms never changes convergence. For \(f(x)=\dfrac{x}{x^2+5}\), \(f'(x)=\dfrac{5-x^2}{(x^2+5)^2}\), which is negative for \(x>\sqrt5\approx 2.24\). So apply the test on \([3,\infty)\).
