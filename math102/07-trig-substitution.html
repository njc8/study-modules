Chapter 1 · Stewart 7.3

The three substitutions

Three radicals, three substitutions, three identities. Everything in this module comes from this table.

Ordinary substitution fails on \(\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\) because the derivative of the inside is not sitting there as a factor. The fix is to run substitution backwards: instead of setting a new variable equal to something in \(x\), set \(x\) equal to a function of a new variable \(\theta\). Choose that function so the Pythagorean identity collapses the radical.

\(\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\;:\qquad x=a\sin\theta,\qquad dx=a\cos\theta\,d\theta,\qquad \sqrt{a^2-x^2}=a\cos\theta\)Because \(a^2-a^2\sin^2\theta=a^2(1-\sin^2\theta)=a^2\cos^2\theta\). Take \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\le\theta\le\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), where \(\cos\theta\ge 0\), so the root is \(+a\cos\theta\).
\(\sqrt{a^2+x^2}\;:\qquad x=a\tan\theta,\qquad dx=a\sec^2\theta\,d\theta,\qquad \sqrt{a^2+x^2}=a\sec\theta\)Because \(a^2+a^2\tan^2\theta=a^2(1+\tan^2\theta)=a^2\sec^2\theta\). Take \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\lt\theta\lt\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), where \(\sec\theta>0\).
\(\sqrt{x^2-a^2}\;:\qquad x=a\sec\theta,\qquad dx=a\sec\theta\tan\theta\,d\theta,\qquad \sqrt{x^2-a^2}=a\tan\theta\)Because \(a^2\sec^2\theta-a^2=a^2(\sec^2\theta-1)=a^2\tan^2\theta\). Take \(0\le\theta\lt\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) for \(x\ge a\), where \(\tan\theta\ge 0\).

The three identities behind them are the same identity written three ways:

\(1-\sin^2\theta=\cos^2\theta,\qquad 1+\tan^2\theta=\sec^2\theta,\qquad \sec^2\theta-1=\tan^2\theta\)Match the sign pattern of your radical to one of these three. Minus a square goes with sine, plus a square goes with tangent, a square minus something goes with secant.
Read the shape, not the letters. \(\sqrt{9-4x^2}\) is \(\sqrt{a^2-u^2}\) with \(a=3\) and \(u=2x\), so \(2x=3\sin\theta\), that is \(x=\tfrac32\sin\theta\). And the radical does not have to be visible: \((4+x^2)^{3/2}\), \(\dfrac{1}{(x^2+9)^2}\), and \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}\) are all the same three cases in disguise.

The reference triangle

Once you integrate in \(\theta\) you must return to \(x\). Do not solve \(\theta=\arcsin(x/a)\) and substitute it into every trig function. Draw a right triangle in which the substitution is true, and read the other functions straight off it.

Place \(\theta\) at one acute vertex. For \(x=a\sin\theta\), \(\sin\theta=\dfrac{x}{a}\) means opposite \(=x\), hypotenuse \(=a\), so the third side is \(\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\). For \(x=a\tan\theta\), opposite \(=x\), adjacent \(=a\), hypotenuse \(\sqrt{x^2+a^2}\). For \(x=a\sec\theta\), hypotenuse \(=x\), adjacent \(=a\), opposite \(\sqrt{x^2-a^2}\). In every case the radical is the side that is neither \(x\) nor \(a\).

The angle \(\theta\) sits at the left vertex. The purple side is the radical, the one side that is neither \(x\) nor \(a\).

Slide \(x\) to the ends. In the sine case, \(x\to a\) squashes the adjacent side to zero and \(\theta\to 90^\circ\). In the secant case, \(x\to a\) squashes the opposite side and \(\theta\to 0\). Watch which expression each trig function reads off, and notice that only two of the three sides are ever given to you.

\(dx\) is not \(d\theta\). Every one of these substitutions changes the differential, and the new factor is what makes the integral collapse. Forgetting the \(a\cos\theta\) in \(dx\) leaves you with an integral that does not simplify, which is the signal that you dropped it.

Chapter 2 · Stewart 7.3

Working an integral end to end

Substitute, simplify, integrate in \(\theta\), come back through the triangle. Five moves, always the same five.

Here is the whole procedure on \(\displaystyle\int\frac{\sqrt{9-x^2}}{x^2}\,dx\), Stewart's first example.

  1. Choose. The radical is \(\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\) with \(a=3\), so \(x=3\sin\theta\) and \(dx=3\cos\theta\,d\theta\), with \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\le\theta\le\tfrac{\pi}{2}\).
  2. Rewrite. \(\sqrt{9-x^2}=\sqrt{9-9\sin^2\theta}=3|\cos\theta|=3\cos\theta\) on that interval, and \(x^2=9\sin^2\theta\), so \[\int\frac{\sqrt{9-x^2}}{x^2}\,dx=\int\frac{3\cos\theta}{9\sin^2\theta}\,3\cos\theta\,d\theta.\]
  3. Simplify to a trig integral. The 9s cancel: \[\int\frac{\cos^2\theta}{\sin^2\theta}\,d\theta=\int\cot^2\theta\,d\theta=\int(\csc^2\theta-1)\,d\theta.\] This is module 5 territory: every trig-substitution problem ends as a trig integral.
  4. Integrate. \(\displaystyle\int(\csc^2\theta-1)\,d\theta=-\cot\theta-\theta+C\).
  5. Back to \(x\) with the triangle. With opposite \(x\), hypotenuse 3, adjacent \(\sqrt{9-x^2}\): \(\cot\theta=\dfrac{\sqrt{9-x^2}}{x}\) and \(\theta=\arcsin\dfrac{x}{3}\). So \[\int\frac{\sqrt{9-x^2}}{x^2}\,dx=-\frac{\sqrt{9-x^2}}{x}-\arcsin\frac{x}{3}+C.\]

Sanity check the shape of an answer before you trust it: a term with the radical, and an inverse trig term for the leftover \(\theta\). If your answer still has a \(\theta\) in it, you skipped step 5.

Definite integrals: change the limits and never come back. For \(\displaystyle\int_0^{3/2}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{9-x^2}}\) with \(x=3\sin\theta\): at \(x=0\), \(\sin\theta=0\) so \(\theta=0\); at \(x=\tfrac32\), \(\sin\theta=\tfrac12\) so \(\theta=\tfrac{\pi}{6}\). The integral becomes \(\int_0^{\pi/6}d\theta=\tfrac{\pi}{6}\). No triangle needed, no back-substitution, and the answer is a number.

Explore: the limits become angles

Blue curve: the integrand. Shaded: the area counted so far. The orange line is the moving upper limit \(b\).

Push \(b\) to the right end of each preset and read the angle. In the sine case \(b=a\) gives exactly \(\theta=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), which is why quarter-circle areas come out as multiples of \(\pi\). The \(x\)-integral and the \(\theta\)-integral in the readout are always the same number, and one of them is elementary.

Step-by-step: one integral, all six moves

Cover the screen after step 2 and finish it yourself, then step forward to compare. The step people skip is the third one: cancel the constants before integrating, so you can see which trig integral you actually have.

The answer must be in \(x\). An indefinite trig substitution is only finished after the triangle. A definite one is finished at the number, and going back to \(x\) is wasted work that invites sign errors.

Chapter 3 · Stewart 7.3

Completing the square first

A middle term is not a new problem. Shift the variable and you are back to one of the three cases.

Any quadratic can be written with its middle term absorbed into a square:

\(x^2+bx+c=\left(x+\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2+\left(c-\tfrac{b^2}{4}\right)\)Half the middle coefficient goes inside the square, and its square comes back out. Then \(u=x+\tfrac{b}{2}\), \(du=dx\), and the three cases apply to \(u\).

Two examples. For \(\displaystyle\int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2+2x+5}}\), half of 2 is 1, so \(x^2+2x+5=(x+1)^2+4\). With \(u=x+1\) this is \(\int\frac{du}{\sqrt{u^2+4}}=\ln\left|u+\sqrt{u^2+4}\right|+C\), that is \(\ln\left|x+1+\sqrt{x^2+2x+5}\right|+C\).

For \(\displaystyle\int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{3-2x-x^2}}\), factor the sign out of the \(x\) terms first: \(3-2x-x^2=3-(x^2+2x)=3+1-(x+1)^2=4-(x+1)^2\). Now it is the sine case with \(a=2\), and the answer is \(\arcsin\dfrac{x+1}{2}+C\).

Which of the three you land in is decided by the completed square. \((x+h)^2+a^2\) is the tangent case, \(a^2-(x+h)^2\) is the sine case, \((x+h)^2-a^2\) is the secant case. Nothing about the original middle term matters once the square is complete.

Explore: completing the square is a shift

Purple: \(y=x^2+2hx+(h^2+k)\). Green dashed: the same parabola slid to the origin, \(y=u^2+k\).

Move \(h\) and watch the vertex slide to \(x=-h\) while its height \(k\) never changes. That height is the whole story: \(k>0\) leaves \(u^2+a^2\) (tangent case), \(k<0\) leaves \(u^2-a^2\) (secant case), and a leading minus sign in front of \(x^2\) leaves \(a^2-u^2\) (sine case).

Step-by-step: complete, shift, name the case

Say the case out loud at step 4 before you look. Getting \(u^2+a^2\) versus \(a^2-u^2\) right is the only decision in this chapter.

Not every quadratic denominator wants trig substitution. For \(\displaystyle\int\frac{dx}{x^2+bx+c}\) look at the discriminant \(b^2-4c\) first. Positive means the quadratic factors into distinct linear pieces, so it is partial fractions (module 3) and the answer is logs. Negative means the completed square is \((x+h)^2+a^2\), which is arctan. Zero means it is a perfect square and the power rule finishes it.
