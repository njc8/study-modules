Chapter 1 · Stewart 7.8

Infinite intervals

You cannot substitute \(\infty\) into an antiderivative. You substitute \(t\), then let \(t\) run.

An integral over an unbounded interval is a type 1 improper integral. It is defined as a limit of ordinary integrals:

\(\displaystyle\int_a^{\infty} f(x)\,dx=\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_a^{t} f(x)\,dx,\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{b} f(x)\,dx=\lim_{t\to-\infty}\int_{t}^{b} f(x)\,dx\)If the limit exists as a finite number, the integral converges and that number is its value. Otherwise it diverges. "Diverges" covers both \(\pm\infty\) and a limit that simply fails to exist.

For a doubly infinite interval you must split, and both halves must converge on their own:

\(\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx=\int_{-\infty}^{c} f(x)\,dx+\int_{c}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx\)Any convenient \(c\) works, usually \(c=0\). If either piece diverges, the whole integral diverges, no matter how nicely the two infinities look like they should cancel.
Symmetry is not convergence. \(\int_{-t}^{t} x\,dx=0\) for every \(t\), but \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\,dx\) diverges: the piece \(\int_0^{\infty}x\,dx\) is already infinite. You are not allowed to let the two ends run to infinity at the same rate. Split first, then take two independent limits.

The one fact this chapter is built on

\(\displaystyle\int_1^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x^{p}}\ \text{converges}\iff p>1,\qquad\text{and then}\ \int_1^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x^{p}}=\frac{1}{p-1}\)Proof sketch: for \(p\ne1\), \(\int_1^t x^{-p}dx=\dfrac{t^{1-p}-1}{1-p}\). If \(p>1\) the exponent \(1-p\) is negative, so \(t^{1-p}\to0\) and the limit is \(\frac{1}{p-1}\). If \(p<1\) then \(t^{1-p}\to\infty\). If \(p=1\) the antiderivative is \(\ln t\), which also goes to infinity.

More generally \(\displaystyle\int_a^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x^p}=\frac{a^{1-p}}{p-1}\) for \(a>0\) and \(p>1\). The other workhorse is the exponential:

\(\displaystyle\int_0^{\infty} e^{-ax}\,dx=\frac{1}{a}\quad(a>0),\qquad \int_c^{\infty} e^{-ax}\,dx=\frac{e^{-ac}}{a}\)Exponential decay always wins. Every \(\int x^n e^{-ax}dx\) over \([0,\infty)\) converges too, because \(e^{-ax}\) beats any power.
The five limits you actually need. \(\dfrac{1}{t^{k}}\to0\) for \(k>0\); \(e^{-t}\to0\); \(t\,e^{-t}\to0\) (and \(t^n e^{-t}\to 0\) for any \(n\)); \(\dfrac{\ln t}{t}\to0\); \(\arctan t\to\dfrac{\pi}{2}\). Every type 1 problem in this course ends by using one of these.

Step-by-step: replace the infinity with \(t\)

Write the limit symbol on every line until it is gone. Students who skip it end up writing \(e^{-\infty}=0\) inside a bracket, which is not a legal substitution, and they lose the habit exactly when the limit is the hard part.

Chapter 2 · Stewart 7.8

Discontinuous integrands

The interval can be short and the integral still improper, if the integrand runs off to infinity somewhere on it.

If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b)\) and blows up at \(b\), the integral is a type 2 improper integral:

\(\displaystyle\int_a^{b} f(x)\,dx=\lim_{t\to b^{-}}\int_a^{t} f(x)\,dx,\qquad\text{and if the blow-up is at }a:\ \int_a^{b} f\,dx=\lim_{t\to a^{+}}\int_t^{b} f\,dx\)Same move as chapter 1: pull the bad endpoint back to \(t\), integrate normally, then let \(t\) slide onto it. The one-sided arrow matters, because you approach from inside the interval.

If the blow-up is at an interior point \(c\) with \(a \lt c \lt b\), split there and require both pieces to converge:

\(\displaystyle\int_a^{b} f\,dx=\int_a^{c} f\,dx+\int_c^{b} f\,dx\)Both pieces are now type 2 with the bad point at an endpoint. If either one diverges, the whole thing diverges.
The classic wrong answer. \(\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x^{2}}\) is not \(\left[-\tfrac1x\right]_{-1}^{1}=-1-1=-2\). A negative answer for a positive integrand should stop you cold. The Fundamental Theorem needs \(f\) continuous on \([-1,1]\), and \(1/x^2\) is not. Split at \(0\): \(\int_0^1 x^{-2}dx\) already diverges, so the whole integral diverges.

The mirror image of the chapter 1 fact, and the reason the two are so easy to confuse:

\(\displaystyle\int_0^{1}\frac{dx}{x^{p}}\ \text{converges}\iff p<1,\qquad\text{and then}\ \int_0^{1}\frac{dx}{x^{p}}=\frac{1}{1-p}\)Near \(0\) a small exponent is harmless, because \(x^{-p}\) barely blows up. Out at \(\infty\) a large exponent is what you need, because \(x^{-p}\) must die fast. The exponent \(p=1\) fails on both ends, which is why \(\int_0^{\infty}dx/x\) diverges twice over.

One more standard value, because the integrand is unbounded but the area is not: \(\displaystyle\int_0^{1}\ln x\,dx=\Big[x\ln x-x\Big]_0^{1}=-1\), using \(x\ln x\to0\) as \(x\to0^{+}\).

Only \(p=1\) manages to be bad in both places.

Check the interval before you integrate. Look at the integrand and ask where the denominator vanishes, where a logarithm hits its argument zero, where a tangent or secant hits an odd multiple of \(\pi/2\). If any of those points lies in \([a,b]\), the integral is improper and the Fundamental Theorem does not apply directly.

Chapter 3 · Stewart 7.8

The comparison theorem

Most integrands have no elementary antiderivative. You can still decide convergence, by trapping the integrand between things you already know.

\(\text{If }0\le f(x)\le g(x)\text{ on }[a,\infty):\quad \int_a^{\infty}g\ \text{converges}\ \Longrightarrow\ \int_a^{\infty}f\ \text{converges};\qquad \int_a^{\infty}f\ \text{diverges}\ \Longrightarrow\ \int_a^{\infty}g\ \text{diverges}\)Read it as: the small one is dragged down by a convergent big one, and the big one is dragged up by a divergent small one. It gives you convergence, not the value. It also gives you a bound: \(\int f\le\int g\).

The two things you compare to are always the same:

  • Powers: \(\displaystyle\int_1^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x^p}\) converges exactly when \(p>1\).
  • Exponentials: \(\displaystyle\int_a^{\infty}e^{-x}dx=e^{-a}\) converges, and every \(e^{-kx}\) with \(k>0\) does too.

How to guess the answer first

Keep only the dominant term in the numerator and the denominator, and read off the power. For large \(x\):

\(\frac{x^2+3}{x^4+x}\sim\frac{x^2}{x^4}=\frac{1}{x^{2}},\qquad \frac{x+1}{x^2+2}\sim\frac1x,\qquad \frac{1}{\sqrt{x^3+1}}\sim\frac{1}{x^{3/2}}\)Subtract the degree of the bottom from the degree of the top; a square root halves a degree. If the resulting power \(p\) is bigger than \(1\), expect convergence.

Three size facts settle almost every comparison: \(e^{-x}\) beats every power (so \(x^{100}e^{-x}\to0\)), \(\ln x\) loses to every power (so \(\ln x\le x^{\varepsilon}\) eventually), and bounded factors do not matter (\(\sin x\), \(\cos x\), \(\arctan x\) are trapped between constants).

Choosing the direction

You need the inequality that points the right way, and you get it by adjusting the same expression you guessed with:

  • To prove convergence you need \(f\le g\) with \(\int g\) convergent, so make the integrand bigger: drop a positive term from a denominator, or replace a bounded factor by its maximum. \(\dfrac{2+\sin x}{x^{3}}\le\dfrac{3}{x^{3}}\).
  • To prove divergence you need \(f\ge g\) with \(\int g\) divergent, so make the integrand smaller: drop a positive term from a numerator, or replace a bounded factor by its minimum. \(\dfrac{1+e^{-x}}{x}\ge\dfrac1x\).
Only the tail matters. The inequality has to hold from some point on, not everywhere. \(\int_1^{\infty}e^{-x^2}dx\) converges because \(x^2\ge x\) for \(x\ge1\), so \(e^{-x^2}\le e^{-x}\) there. What happens on a bounded piece can never make an integral diverge, as long as the integrand is continuous on it.
Comparison needs \(f\ge0\). The theorem as stated is for nonnegative integrands. With a sign-changing \(f\) the right move is to compare \(|f|\) instead, which gives absolute convergence.
