Chapter 1 · Stewart 5.4

The antiderivative table

An antiderivative is a derivative read backwards. There is no new theory here, only a table you should be able to write from memory in two minutes.

A function \(F\) is an antiderivative of \(f\) if \(F'=f\). If \(F\) is one antiderivative, so is \(F+C\) for every constant \(C\), and on an interval those are the only ones: two functions with the same derivative differ by a constant. That whole family is what the indefinite integral names:

\(\displaystyle\int f(x)\,dx = F(x) + C \quad\text{means}\quad F'(x)=f(x)\)The \(+\,C\) is not decoration. It is the statement that a derivative forgets constants, so integrating cannot recover one.

Every entry in the table below is a derivative you already know, written from right to left.

Derivative factAntiderivative, read backwards
\(\frac{d}{dx}\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}=x^n\)\(\displaystyle\int x^n\,dx=\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C\quad (n\neq -1)\)
\(\frac{d}{dx}\ln|x|=\frac1x\)\(\displaystyle\int \frac{dx}{x}=\ln|x|+C\)
\(\frac{d}{dx}e^x=e^x\)\(\displaystyle\int e^x\,dx=e^x+C\)
\(\frac{d}{dx}\frac{a^x}{\ln a}=a^x\)\(\displaystyle\int a^x\,dx=\frac{a^x}{\ln a}+C\)
\(\frac{d}{dx}(-\cos x)=\sin x\)\(\displaystyle\int \sin x\,dx=-\cos x+C\)
\(\frac{d}{dx}\sin x=\cos x\)\(\displaystyle\int \cos x\,dx=\sin x+C\)
\(\frac{d}{dx}\tan x=\sec^2 x\)\(\displaystyle\int \sec^2 x\,dx=\tan x+C\)
\(\frac{d}{dx}(-\cot x)=\csc^2 x\)\(\displaystyle\int \csc^2 x\,dx=-\cot x+C\)
\(\frac{d}{dx}\sec x=\sec x\tan x\)\(\displaystyle\int \sec x\tan x\,dx=\sec x+C\)
\(\frac{d}{dx}(-\csc x)=\csc x\cot x\)\(\displaystyle\int \csc x\cot x\,dx=-\csc x+C\)
\(\frac{d}{dx}\arctan x=\frac{1}{1+x^2}\)\(\displaystyle\int \frac{dx}{1+x^2}=\arctan x+C\)
\(\frac{d}{dx}\arcsin x=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\)\(\displaystyle\int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}=\arcsin x+C\)
\(\displaystyle\int x^n\,dx=\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C\;\;(n\neq-1)\)Raise the power by one, divide by the new power. It works for every real \(n\) except \(-1\), including \(n=\tfrac12\) and \(n=-\tfrac34\).
\(\displaystyle\int \frac{1}{x}\,dx=\ln|x|+C\)The absolute value patches the hole at \(n=-1\) on both sides of the origin: for \(x<0\), \(\frac{d}{dx}\ln(-x)=\frac{-1}{-x}=\frac1x\) as well.
\(\displaystyle\int a^x\,dx=\frac{a^x}{\ln a}+C\quad (a>0,\;a\neq1)\)Differentiating \(a^x\) drops a factor of \(\ln a\), so integrating must divide by it. With \(a=e\) the factor is 1 and the formula collapses to \(\int e^x dx=e^x+C\).

Linearity. \(\int\big(c\,f+g\big)dx=c\int f\,dx+\int g\,dx\). Constants come out, sums split. There is no product rule and no quotient rule for integrals, which is exactly why the rest of this course exists.

Rewrite before you integrate

The power rule only sees powers. Most of the work in this chapter is algebra done before the integral sign:

  • \(\sqrt{x}=x^{1/2}\), \(\sqrt[4]{x}=x^{1/4}\), \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt[4]{x}}=x^{-1/4}\)
  • \(\dfrac{1}{x^3}=x^{-3}\), so \(\displaystyle\int\frac{dx}{x^3}=\frac{x^{-2}}{-2}+C=-\frac{1}{2x^2}+C\)
  • \(\dfrac{(x+1)^2}{x}=\dfrac{x^2+2x+1}{x}=x+2+\dfrac1x\)
  • \(x(x+2)(x-3)=x^3-x^2-6x\)

The WebAssign integration review opens with \(\displaystyle\int\left(\sqrt[4]{x}+\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{x}}\right)dx\). Written as powers it is \(\int\big(x^{1/4}+x^{-1/4}\big)dx=\tfrac45x^{5/4}+\tfrac43x^{3/4}+C\), and there is nothing left to think about.

Explore: the slope of \(F\) is the height of \(f\)

Blue is \(f\), orange is \(F\). The purple dashed line is the tangent to \(F\); the blue bar is the height \(f(x)\).

Slide \(x\) and watch two numbers stay equal: the height of the blue bar and the slope of the purple tangent. Then move the \(C\) slider. The orange curve slides up and down, the tangent slides with it, and its slope never changes. Every \(C\) gives the same slopes, and that is why one integral is a whole family.

Three ways this goes wrong. First, using the power rule at \(n=-1\): \(\int x^{-1}dx\) is not \(\frac{x^0}{0}\), it is \(\ln|x|+C\). Second, integrating a radical without rewriting it as a power. Third, misreading a coefficient in the denominator: \(\displaystyle\int\frac{dx}{8x}=\frac18\int\frac{dx}{x}=\frac18\ln|x|+C\), not \(\ln|8x|\) and not \(8\ln|x|\).

Chapter 2 · Stewart 5.3

The Fundamental Theorem

Two statements, one idea: accumulating a rate and measuring a change are inverse operations.

Fix a continuous \(f\) and a starting point \(a\), and define the accumulation function

\(\displaystyle g(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad g'(x)=f(x)\)FTC Part 1. The rate at which accumulated area grows is the current height. The letter \(t\) is a dummy: \(g\) is a function of the limit \(x\), not of \(t\).

Differentiating an integral therefore takes no work at all, as long as the variable sits alone in the upper limit. When it does not, use the chain rule. Write \(g(x)=H(u(x))\) where \(H(u)=\int_a^u f\):

\(\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^{u(x)} f(t)\,dt=f\big(u(x)\big)\,u'(x)\)Evaluate \(f\) at the upper limit, then multiply by the derivative of that limit. This is the version most exam problems test.

Two variations follow from it. If the variable is in the lower limit, flip the sign, because \(\int_x^b=-\int_b^x\):

\(\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\int_x^{b} f(t)\,dt=-f(x),\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\int_{v(x)}^{u(x)} f(t)\,dt=f\big(u(x)\big)u'(x)-f\big(v(x)\big)v'(x)\)With both limits moving, split at any constant \(c\): \(\int_v^u=\int_c^u-\int_c^v\), then differentiate each piece.

Part 2 is the one you compute with. If \(F\) is any antiderivative of a continuous \(f\) on \([a,b]\):

\(\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)=\Big[F(x)\Big]_a^b\)FTC Part 2. Any antiderivative works, because the \(+\,C\) cancels in the subtraction. This turns a limit of Riemann sums into two evaluations.

Read Part 2 with \(f=F'\) and it becomes the Net Change Theorem: the integral of a rate of change is the net change in the quantity.

\(\displaystyle\int_{t_1}^{t_2} Q'(t)\,dt=Q(t_2)-Q(t_1)\)Water flowing in at \(r(t)\) litres per minute puts \(\int_{t_1}^{t_2} r\,dt\) litres in the tank. A particle with velocity \(v(t)\) has displacement \(\int v\,dt\) and distance travelled \(\int|v|\,dt\).

Step-by-step: differentiating an integral with a moving limit

Say the steps out loud: name \(u\), differentiate \(u\), plug \(u\) into \(f\), multiply. No integration ever happens, which is the whole point of Part 1.

The dummy variable never survives. In \(g(x)=\int_1^{x^2}\sqrt{t}\,dt\) the answer \(g'(x)=\sqrt{x^2}\cdot 2x=2x^2\) (for \(x>0\)) contains no \(t\). If a \(t\) appears in your answer, you differentiated the wrong letter.
Displacement is not distance. \(\int_0^T v\,dt\) is where the particle ends up relative to where it started, and it can be zero for a long trip. Total distance is \(\int_0^T|v|\,dt\), which means finding where \(v\) changes sign and splitting the integral there.

Reading integrals as areas

Some integrals are faster to look at than to compute. Others are easier if you turn the picture sideways.

For \(f\ge0\), \(\int_a^b f\,dx\) is the area under the graph. For general \(f\) it is signed area: the parts above the axis count positive, the parts below count negative. So \(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin x\,dx=0\), not because nothing is there, but because the two halves cancel.

When the region is a shape you know, skip the antiderivative:

\(\displaystyle\int_{-r}^{r}\sqrt{r^2-x^2}\,dx=\frac{\pi r^2}{2},\qquad \int_0^{r}\sqrt{r^2-x^2}\,dx=\frac{\pi r^2}{4},\qquad \int_{-a}^{a}|x|\,dx=a^2\)\(y=\sqrt{r^2-x^2}\) is the upper half of the circle \(x^2+y^2=r^2\), so the first integral is half a disk. The last is two triangles of base \(a\) and height \(a\).

A constant gives a rectangle, \(\int_a^b c\,dx=c(b-a)\), and a line through the origin gives a triangle, \(\int_0^b mx\,dx=\tfrac12 mb^2\). None of these need the Fundamental Theorem.

Integrating with respect to \(y\)

Nothing says the strips have to be vertical. If the region is described more easily by its left and right edges than by its top and bottom, slice it with horizontal strips. A strip at height \(y\) has width \(x_{\text{right}}-x_{\text{left}}\) and thickness \(dy\):

\(\displaystyle A=\int_c^d \big(x_{\text{right}}(y)-x_{\text{left}}(y)\big)\,dy\)Solve the boundary curves for \(x\) as a function of \(y\) first. The limits \(c\) and \(d\) are the lowest and highest \(y\) the region reaches.

The region bounded by the \(y\)-axis, the line \(y=1\), and the curve \(y=\sqrt[3]{x}\) is the standard example. Solving for \(x\) gives \(x=y^3\), the strip runs from \(x=0\) to \(x=y^3\), and \(y\) runs from 0 to 1:

\(\displaystyle A=\int_0^1 y^3\,dy=\Big[\tfrac{y^4}{4}\Big]_0^1=\tfrac14\)In \(x\) the same region needs \(\int_0^1(1-\sqrt[3]{x})\,dx\), which is more work for the same number. Choosing the direction of the strips is a real decision.

Area between two curves

Between two graphs the height of a vertical strip is top minus bottom, so

\(\displaystyle A=\int_a^b\big(y_{\text{top}}-y_{\text{bottom}}\big)\,dx\)Find \(a\) and \(b\) by setting the two curves equal. Between consecutive intersections the same curve stays on top, so you never need absolute values inside one piece.

For \(y=x^2\) and \(y=2x\): solve \(x^2=2x\) to get \(x=0\) and \(x=2\). On \((0,2)\) the line is above the parabola, so \(A=\int_0^2(2x-x^2)dx=\big[x^2-\tfrac{x^3}{3}\big]_0^2=\tfrac43\).

Check which curve is on top by testing one point. Pick any \(x\) strictly between the intersections and compare the two heights. If you get a negative area, you subtracted in the wrong order, and the fix is a sign, not a redo.
