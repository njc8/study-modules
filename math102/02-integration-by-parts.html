Chapter 1 · Stewart 7.1

The formula and the choice of \(u\)

Substitution undoes the chain rule. Integration by parts undoes the product rule.

Start from the product rule for two differentiable functions \(u(x)\) and \(v(x)\):

\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\big[u v\big]=u'v+uv'\)The derivative of a product differentiates one factor at a time.

Integrate both sides. On the left the integral and the derivative cancel, leaving \(uv\):

\(uv=\displaystyle\int u'v\,dx+\int uv'\,dx\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \int uv'\,dx=uv-\int u'v\,dx\)Now rename: \(dv=v'\,dx\) and \(du=u'\,dx\). That is the formula.
\(\displaystyle\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du\)You do not get an answer. You get a different integral. The method pays off only when \(\int v\,du\) is easier than \(\int u\,dv\).

For a definite integral the same derivation gives the same formula with the boundary term evaluated:

\(\displaystyle\int_a^b u\,dv=\Big[\,uv\,\Big]_a^b-\int_a^b v\,du\)Evaluate \(uv\) at both limits and subtract, then subtract the remaining integral. The limits go on that one too.

Explore: why the formula is \(uv-\int v\,du\)

One increasing curve through the origin, drawn in the \(u\)-\(v\) plane. Blue is the area under it, orange is the area to the left of it.

The two shaded pieces tile the rectangle exactly, so \(uv=\int v\,du+\int u\,dv\). Rearranged, that is the formula. Slide the point along the curve and watch the two areas trade off while their sum stays equal to \(uv\). Press Play to sweep it.

Choosing \(u\): LIATE

You choose \(u\); everything left over, including \(dx\), is \(dv\). Two rules decide the choice.

  1. You must be able to integrate \(dv\). If you cannot find \(v\), the split is dead on arrival.
  2. \(du\) should be simpler than \(u\). Differentiating should make progress: \(x\to 1\), \(\ln x\to 1/x\), \(\arctan x\to 1/(1+x^2)\).

LIATE is the priority list that gets this right almost every time. Pick as \(u\) the first category on the list that appears in the integrand:

\(\underbrace{\textbf{L}}_{\ln x}\;\;\underbrace{\textbf{I}}_{\arctan x,\ \arcsin x}\;\;\underbrace{\textbf{A}}_{x,\ x^2,\ \text{polynomials}}\;\;\underbrace{\textbf{T}}_{\sin x,\ \cos x,\ \sec^2 x}\;\;\underbrace{\textbf{E}}_{e^{x},\ 2^{x}}\)Logs, Inverse trig, Algebraic, Trig, Exponential. Whatever is left, together with \(dx\), is \(dv\).

The list is ordered by how much differentiating helps and how easy the leftovers are to integrate. Logs and inverse trig turn into algebraic fractions, which is a huge win, and neither is pleasant to integrate anyway, so they go first. Exponentials integrate to themselves, so they make ideal \(dv\) and come last.

The sanity check. After you write \(uv-\int v\,du\), look at the new integral. Is it simpler than the one you started with? If it is worse, back up and swap the choice. One glance saves a page of algebra.

Look only at the new integral in each column. The good split lowers a power or kills a logarithm; the bad split raises a power or, worse, asks you to integrate the very thing you started with. Press the button a few times until you can call the winner before you read the tags.

Step-by-step: one round of parts

Write the four pieces \(u\), \(dv\), \(du\), \(v\) in a little block before you touch the formula. Nearly every wrong answer in this chapter is a sign error that came from assembling \(uv-\int v\,du\) out of memory instead of out of that block.

Two traps. First, the minus sign in front of the remaining integral distributes: \(-\int(-\cos x)\,dx=+\int\cos x\,dx\). Second, \(v\) is any one antiderivative of \(dv\), so take the simplest, with no \(+C\). Carrying a constant through just cancels itself and gives you two more chances to slip.

Chapter 2 · Stewart 7.1

Repeat, tabulate, and go around in a circle

One round is often not enough. Sometimes you run parts again, sometimes you run it until the original integral comes back and you solve for it.

Running parts twice

Take \(\int x^2e^x\,dx\). LIATE says \(u=x^2\), \(dv=e^x\,dx\), so \(du=2x\,dx\) and \(v=e^x\):

\(\displaystyle\int x^2e^x\,dx=x^2e^x-2\int xe^x\,dx\)The power dropped from 2 to 1. That is progress, not an answer.

The new integral is the one from chapter 1: \(\int xe^x\,dx=(x-1)e^x\). Substituting it back,

\(\displaystyle\int x^2e^x\,dx=x^2e^x-2(x-1)e^x+C=(x^2-2x+2)e^x+C\)Each round of parts knocks one off the power. A power of \(n\) against \(e^{ax}\) needs \(n\) rounds.
Keep the same direction. On the second round choose \(u\) the same way you did on the first. If you swap the roles halfway through, parts undoes itself and you land back on \(\int x^2e^x\,dx=\int x^2e^x\,dx\), which is true and useless.

The tabular shortcut

When \(u\) is a polynomial that eventually differentiates to zero and \(dv\) is something you can integrate over and over (\(e^{ax}\), \(\sin bx\), \(\cos bx\)), you can skip the bookkeeping. Make two columns: derivatives of \(u\) going down the left, antiderivatives of \(dv\) going down the right. Multiply along the diagonals, alternating the signs \(+,-,+,-\), and stop when the left column hits 0.

When the table does not apply. The left column has to reach 0, so \(u\) must be a polynomial. For \(\int x^2\ln x\,dx\) the left column would be \(\ln x,\ 1/x,\ -1/x^2,\dots\), which never terminates. Use one plain round of parts there instead.

When the integral comes back: cyclic integrals

Now take \(I=\int e^{ax}\sin bx\,dx\). Neither factor ever simplifies, so no number of rounds will finish it. Run parts twice anyway, keeping \(u\) exponential both times, and the original integral reappears on the right:

\(I=\text{(boundary terms)}-\dfrac{a^2}{b^2}\,I\)Keeping \(u=e^{ax}\) both rounds produces the factor \(a^2/b^2\). The exact constant depends on the split, but the shape is always this: \(I=\text{something}+kI\) with \(k\neq 1\).

Now it is algebra, not calculus. Move the \(I\) to the left, divide, and you are done:

\(\displaystyle\int e^{ax}\sin bx\,dx=\frac{e^{ax}\big(a\sin bx-b\cos bx\big)}{a^2+b^2}+C\qquad \int e^{ax}\cos bx\,dx=\frac{e^{ax}\big(a\cos bx+b\sin bx\big)}{a^2+b^2}+C\)Two formulas worth knowing cold. Check them by differentiating: the \(a^2+b^2\) in the denominator is exactly what the product rule regenerates.
Do not lose the constant. Solving \(I=F-kI\) gives \(I=\dfrac{F}{1+k}\), and only then do you add \(+C\). And if you ever get \(I=F+I\), you ran the second round in the opposite direction and undid your own work: go back and keep \(u\) the same kind of function both times.

The same trick handles \(\int\sec^3x\,dx\), which returns in the trigonometric integrals module: parts with \(u=\sec x\) and \(dv=\sec^2x\,dx\) brings \(\int\sec^3x\,dx\) back on the right, and you solve for it.

Chapter 3 · Stewart 7.1

Substitution first, then parts, and applications

If there is an inner function, clean it up with a substitution before you reach for parts.

Integration by parts expects a product of two plain functions. When one factor has something stuffed inside it, like \(e^{x^2}\) or \(\sin\sqrt{x}\), substitute first and do parts second.

\(\displaystyle\int x^3e^{x^2}dx \;\overset{w=x^2}{=}\; \tfrac12\int w\,e^{w}\,dw=\tfrac12(w-1)e^{w}+C=\tfrac12\big(x^2-1\big)e^{x^2}+C\)With \(w=x^2\), \(dw=2x\,dx\). One factor of \(x\) pairs with \(dx\) to make \(dw\); the remaining \(x^2\) becomes \(w\). Now it is the standard \(\int we^w\,dw\).

The same move handles a root inside a function. For \(\int\sin\sqrt{x}\,dx\) put \(w=\sqrt{x}\), so \(x=w^2\) and \(dx=2w\,dw\):

\(\displaystyle\int\sin\sqrt{x}\,dx=\int 2w\sin w\,dw=-2w\cos w+2\sin w+C=2\sin\sqrt{x}-2\sqrt{x}\cos\sqrt{x}+C\)The substitution creates the extra factor \(2w\) that makes parts possible. Without it there is nothing to split.

Two more worth recognizing: \(\int e^{\sqrt{x}}dx=2(\sqrt{x}-1)e^{\sqrt{x}}+C\) with the same \(w=\sqrt x\), and \(\int\cos(\ln x)\,dx=\tfrac{x}{2}\big(\cos(\ln x)+\sin(\ln x)\big)+C\), where \(w=\ln x\) turns the integral into the cyclic \(\int e^{w}\cos w\,dw\).

Which comes first. Ask what is inside what. An inner function (a square, a root, a log in an argument) is a substitution signal. A product of two unrelated functions is a parts signal. If you see both, substitute first: it almost never hurts, and it usually leaves a clean parts problem.

Reduction formulas

Doing one round in general, with \(u=x^n\) and \(dv=e^x\,dx\), gives a formula that steps the exponent down by one:

\(\displaystyle\int x^ne^x\,dx=x^ne^x-n\int x^{n-1}e^x\,dx\)A reduction formula: apply it \(n\) times and you reach \(\int e^x\,dx\). The tabular method is this formula run to the end in one picture.

Distance from a velocity

A particle moving with velocity \(v(t)\ge 0\) travels \(\int_0^T v(t)\,dt\) in the first \(T\) seconds. When the velocity is a product like \(te^{-t}\), that integral is a parts problem:

\(\displaystyle\int_0^T te^{-t}\,dt=\Big[-te^{-t}\Big]_0^T+\int_0^T e^{-t}dt=1-(T+1)e^{-T}\)Here \(u=t\), \(dv=e^{-t}dt\), \(v=-e^{-t}\). The boundary term is negative and the leftover integral flips sign, which is where the \(+1\) comes from.

Explore: velocity to distance

The curve is the speed \(v(t)\). The shaded area from \(0\) to \(T\) is the distance travelled.

Push \(T\) out to the right. The particle never quite stops, but the distance flattens against a ceiling: the area keeps growing and still stays finite. That is the whole idea of an improper integral, a few modules ahead.

Distance or displacement. \(\int_0^T v\,dt\) is displacement. It equals the distance travelled only while \(v\ge 0\). Every velocity in this chapter is non-negative on the interval given, so the two agree here, but check the sign before you assume it.
