Polar coordinates

A second address system for the plane. Circles and spirals that need two ugly Cartesian equations become one line, and area stops being a height times a width: it becomes half of an integral of \(r^2\).

How to use this

Work through the chapters in order. Each one teaches an idea, gives you something to drag or sweep, then drills you with fresh random problems until you hit the goal. Progress is saved in this browser.

Angles are radians, typed as pi/4, 7pi/6, -pi/3. Points are typed with parentheses: a polar point is (r, theta) and a Cartesian point is (x, y). Answers are exact unless a problem says otherwise, so write 3sqrt(3)/2, pi - 3sqrt(3)/2, or pi/4 + 3sqrt(3)/4 rather than a decimal. A Cartesian equation may be typed in any equivalent form, like x^2+y^2=2y. Lists are comma separated: 2pi/3, 4pi/3.

Answer boxes work like WebAssign's calcPad: sqrt makes a radical, / a fraction, ^ an exponent, pi and theta the Greek letters. Use the right arrow key to step out of a radical or fraction, and press Enter to check.

the moving piece: the sweeping ray, one sector, the tangent the second curve or the comparison the result: an area, a length, a value the polar curve and marked points

What you will be able to do at the end

  • Convert points and equations between Cartesian and polar, in both directions, with the quadrant handled correctly.
  • Recognize the standard curves on sight: circles through the origin, cardioids, limaçons with and without an inner loop, roses, lemniscates.
  • Say where a curve passes through the pole, and use those angles as the tangent directions there and as the limits of a loop.
  • Find \(dy/dx\) on a polar curve and locate the horizontal and vertical tangents.
  • Set up and evaluate areas: one petal, an inner loop, inside one curve and outside another, inside both. Also arc length.

Chapter 1 · Stewart 10.3

Points and conversions

A Cartesian address says how far right and how far up. A polar address says how far out and in which direction.

The polar point \((r,\theta)\) means: face in the direction \(\theta\) measured counterclockwise from the positive \(x\)-axis, then walk a signed distance \(r\). Signed is the whole subtlety. If \(r<0\) you walk backwards, so the point lands in the opposite direction.

\(x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta,\qquad r^2=x^2+y^2,\qquad \tan\theta=\dfrac{y}{x}\)Left to right: polar to Cartesian, which is always a plug-in. Right to left: Cartesian to polar, which needs care, because the last two equations do not determine \(r\) and \(\theta\) by themselves.

Going from polar to Cartesian is mechanical. Going the other way has two traps.

The arctangent does not know the quadrant. \(\arctan(y/x)\) always returns an angle in \((-\pi/2,\pi/2)\), so it is only right when the point is in quadrant I or IV. For a point in quadrant II or III, add \(\pi\). Check your answer against the picture every time: the point \((-1,1)\) has \(\tan\theta=-1\), but \(\theta=3\pi/4\), not \(-\pi/4\).

The second trap is that a point has infinitely many polar names. Adding \(2\pi\) to \(\theta\) returns to the same direction, and flipping the sign of \(r\) while adding \(\pi\) to \(\theta\) also lands in the same place:

\((r,\theta)=(r,\theta+2\pi k)=(-r,\theta+\pi)\)The origin is the extreme case: \((0,\theta)\) is the pole for every \(\theta\). That is why the pole needs separate handling in almost every problem in this module.

Curves as equations

The same substitutions convert equations. To go from polar to Cartesian, multiply by \(r\) or square when it helps, then replace \(r\cos\theta\) by \(x\), \(r\sin\theta\) by \(y\), and \(r^2\) by \(x^2+y^2\). For example \(r=4\cos\theta\) has no obvious Cartesian form until you multiply both sides by \(r\):

\(r=4\cos\theta \;\Longrightarrow\; r^2=4r\cos\theta \;\Longrightarrow\; x^2+y^2=4x\)Completing the square gives \((x-2)^2+y^2=4\), the circle of radius 2 centered at \((2,0)\). Multiplying by \(r\) adds the pole to the solution set, and the pole is already on the curve (at \(\theta=\pi/2\)), so nothing is lost.

Going the other way, substitute \(x=r\cos\theta\) and \(y=r\sin\theta\) and then solve for \(r\). A vertical line \(x=c\) becomes \(r\cos\theta=c\), that is \(r=c\sec\theta\). A line through the origin \(y=mx\) has no \(r\) in it at all: it is \(\theta=\arctan m\).

Chapter 2 · Stewart 10.3

The polar curve zoo

Six families cover almost every polar curve you will be asked about. Learn what each one looks like and where it touches the pole, and sketching stops being guesswork.

A polar graph is drawn by letting \(\theta\) run and plotting the point at distance \(r(\theta)\) in the direction \(\theta\). It helps to think of it in two stages: first graph \(r\) against \(\theta\) on ordinary axes, then wrap that graph around the origin. Where the ordinary graph dips below the axis, \(r\) is negative and the wrapped point jumps to the opposite side.

  • Circles. \(r=a\) is the circle of radius \(a\) about the origin. \(r=2a\cos\theta\) is the circle of radius \(a\) centered at \((a,0)\); \(r=2a\sin\theta\) is the circle of radius \(a\) centered at \((0,a)\). Both pass through the origin and are traced once as \(\theta\) moves through an interval of length \(\pi\).
  • Cardioids. \(r=a(1\pm\cos\theta)\) and \(r=a(1\pm\sin\theta)\): heart shapes with a cusp at the pole, maximum \(|r|=2a\).
  • Limaçons. \(r=a+b\cos\theta\). If \(a<b\) there is an inner loop; if \(b<a<2b\) there is a dimple; if \(a\ge 2b\) the curve is convex. The cardioid is the boundary case \(a=b\).
  • Roses. \(r=a\cos(n\theta)\) or \(a\sin(n\theta)\) has \(n\) petals when \(n\) is odd and \(2n\) petals when \(n\) is even, each of length \(|a|\).
  • Lemniscate. \(r^2=a^2\cos 2\theta\): a figure eight lying along the \(x\)-axis, only defined where \(\cos 2\theta\ge 0\).
  • Spiral. \(r=\theta\): the radius grows with the angle forever.
Why \(n\) even doubles the petal count. For \(r=\cos(n\theta)\) the tips sit at \(\theta=k\pi/n\), which is \(2n\) directions. When \(n\) is odd the negative-\(r\) tips land on top of the positive-\(r\) ones, so half of them are duplicates and you see \(n\) petals. When \(n\) is even nothing collides and you see all \(2n\).

Symmetry, and where the curve hits the pole

Three tests save half the work when sketching:

\(\theta\to-\theta \text{ leaves the equation unchanged}\;\Rightarrow\;\text{symmetric about the }x\text{-axis}\)Cosine survives \(\theta\to-\theta\), so every \(r=f(\cos\theta)\) curve is symmetric about the polar axis. Replacing \(\theta\) by \(\pi-\theta\) tests the \(y\)-axis, and replacing \(r\) by \(-r\) (or \(\theta\) by \(\theta+\pi\)) tests the origin.

The angles where \(r=0\) matter more than any other feature. They are where the curve passes through the pole, they are the endpoints of an inner loop or a petal, and they are the directions of the tangent lines at the origin. Solve \(f(\theta)=0\) first, every time.

Chapter 3 · Stewart 10.3

Tangents to polar curves

There is no new rule here. Write the curve parametrically in \(\theta\) and use the parametric slope formula.

With \(r=f(\theta)\), the curve is the parametrized path \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\). Differentiate each with the product rule:

\(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{dy/d\theta}{dx/d\theta}=\dfrac{\dfrac{dr}{d\theta}\sin\theta+r\cos\theta}{\dfrac{dr}{d\theta}\cos\theta-r\sin\theta}\)Numerator: the derivative of \(r\sin\theta\). Denominator: the derivative of \(r\cos\theta\), where the minus sign comes from differentiating \(\cos\theta\). Do not memorize it as a new formula; rebuild it from the product rule if you forget.
  • Horizontal tangent: \(dy/d\theta=0\) and \(dx/d\theta\ne 0\).
  • Vertical tangent: \(dx/d\theta=0\) and \(dy/d\theta\ne 0\).
  • At the pole: if \(f(\theta_0)=0\) and \(f'(\theta_0)\ne 0\), then \(dy/dx=\tan\theta_0\), so the tangent line at the origin is simply the line \(\theta=\theta_0\).
The pole shortcut. Setting \(r=0\) in the big fraction leaves \(\dfrac{f'\sin\theta_0}{f'\cos\theta_0}=\tan\theta_0\). So the angles you already found by solving \(r=0\) are exactly the tangent directions at the origin. One computation, two uses.
When both derivatives vanish. At the cusp of a cardioid, \(dy/d\theta\) and \(dx/d\theta\) are both zero and the fraction is \(0/0\). The slope there is a limit, not a substitution. Stewart handles those points separately, and so should you.

Chapter 4 · Stewart 10.4

Area and arc length in polar coordinates

The area element is a thin sector, not a thin rectangle. That single fact produces every formula in this chapter.

A circular sector of radius \(r\) and angle \(\Delta\theta\) is the fraction \(\Delta\theta/2\pi\) of a full disk, so its area is \(\tfrac{\Delta\theta}{2\pi}\pi r^2=\tfrac12 r^2\Delta\theta\). Slice the region into thin sectors, add, and take the limit:

\(\displaystyle A=\int_\alpha^\beta \tfrac12\,[f(\theta)]^2\,d\theta=\tfrac12\int_\alpha^\beta r^2\,d\theta\)The \(r\) is squared because a sector's area grows with the square of its radius. Doubling the distance from the origin quadruples the area swept by the same angle.
Half, and squared. The two most common slips are dropping the \(\tfrac12\) and integrating \(r\) instead of \(r^2\). Sanity check on the circle \(r=a\): \(\tfrac12\int_0^{2\pi}a^2\,d\theta=\pi a^2\). If your formula does not give \(\pi a^2\) there, it is wrong.

Choosing the limits

The integral is easy. Deciding \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) is the actual problem.

  • A whole cardioid or limaçon: \(0\) to \(2\pi\), which traces it exactly once.
  • One petal of \(r=a\cos(n\theta)\): from \(-\pi/2n\) to \(\pi/2n\), the interval on which \(r\ge 0\) around \(\theta=0\). For \(r=a\sin(n\theta)\) the first petal is \(0\) to \(\pi/n\).
  • An inner loop: between the two consecutive angles where \(r=0\), the ones on which \(r\) is negative.
  • Inside \(r=f\) and outside \(r=g\): solve \(f(\theta)=g(\theta)\) for the crossing angles, check which curve is farther out between them, and integrate \(\tfrac12(f^2-g^2)\).
  • Inside both: split at the crossing angle and use whichever curve is closer in on each piece.
Half-angle identities, coming up in module 5. Every one of these integrals ends in \(\int\cos^2\) or \(\int\sin^2\), so keep these two on the tip of your tongue: \(\cos^2\theta=\dfrac{1+\cos 2\theta}{2}\) and \(\sin^2\theta=\dfrac{1-\cos 2\theta}{2}\). Over a full period both average to \(\tfrac12\), so \(\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta=\pi\). Module 5 drills these properly.

Step-by-step: inside one curve, outside another

Step 2 is the one people skip. Two curves that cross divide the plane into pieces, and only a test value tells you which curve is on the outside between the crossings. Plug in the midpoint angle and compare the two radii.

Arc length

The same parametrization \(x=r\cos\theta\), \(y=r\sin\theta\) gives \(\left(\frac{dx}{d\theta}\right)^2+\left(\frac{dy}{d\theta}\right)^2=r^2+\left(\frac{dr}{d\theta}\right)^2\) after the cross terms cancel, so:

\(\displaystyle L=\int_\alpha^\beta\sqrt{r^2+\left(\dfrac{dr}{d\theta}\right)^2}\;d\theta\)Compare with area, which uses \(\tfrac12 r^2\). Length has no \(\tfrac12\) and no square: it has a square root, and it needs \(dr/d\theta\).

Simplify inside the root before integrating. For the cardioid \(r=a(1+\cos\theta)\) the root collapses to \(a\sqrt{2+2\cos\theta}=2a\left|\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right|\), and the total length comes out to the clean \(8a\).
