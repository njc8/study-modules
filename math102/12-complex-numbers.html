Complex numbers

Add one new number, \(i\), with \(i^2=-1\), and three things happen at once: every polynomial gets all of its roots, multiplication becomes rotation, and \(e^{i\theta}\) walks around the unit circle.

Chapter 1 · Stewart Appendix H

Arithmetic and the complex plane

A complex number is a point in the plane that you can also multiply. That one extra ability is what the whole appendix is about.

A complex number is \(z=a+bi\) where \(a\) and \(b\) are real and \(i^2=-1\). Call \(a=\operatorname{Re}z\) the real part and \(b=\operatorname{Im}z\) the imaginary part. Note that the imaginary part is a real number: the imaginary part of \(3-4i\) is \(-4\), not \(-4i\).

Plot \(a+bi\) as the point \((a,b)\). The horizontal axis is the real axis, the vertical axis is the imaginary axis, and the picture is the complex plane. Addition is then exactly vector addition, tip to tail:

\((a+bi)+(c+di)=(a+c)+(b+d)i\)Add the real parts, add the imaginary parts. Subtraction works the same way. Nothing new yet: this is \(\mathbb{R}^2\).

Multiplication is where complex numbers stop being vectors. Multiply out with FOIL and use \(i^2=-1\) on the last term:

\((a+bi)(c+di)=ac+adi+bci+bdi^2=(ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i\)The only rule you add to ordinary algebra is \(i^2=-1\). Everything else, commutativity, distributivity, the binomial theorem, still holds.

Conjugate and modulus

The conjugate of \(z=a+bi\) is \(\bar z=a-bi\). Geometrically it is the reflection of \(z\) across the real axis. The modulus \(|z|\) is the distance from \(z\) to the origin:

\(|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\qquad\qquad z\bar z=(a+bi)(a-bi)=a^2+b^2=|z|^2\)Multiplying by the conjugate kills the \(i\): the cross terms \(+abi\) and \(-abi\) cancel and \(-b^2i^2=+b^2\). So \(z\bar z\) is always a nonnegative real number.

That identity is the whole trick for division. To divide, multiply the top and the bottom by the conjugate of the bottom, which turns the denominator into a real number:

\(\dfrac{z}{w}=\dfrac{z}{w}\cdot\dfrac{\bar w}{\bar w}=\dfrac{z\bar w}{|w|^2}\qquad\text{so}\qquad \dfrac{1}{z}=\dfrac{\bar z}{|z|^2}\)The same move as rationalizing \(\frac{1}{2+\sqrt3}\) in algebra. You are clearing the \(i\) out of the denominator.

Explore: drag z and watch its relatives

Drag anywhere to move \(z\); it snaps to half integers and is held inside the dashed circle \(|z|=2\) so that \(z^2\) stays on screen. The two arcs are \(\arg z\) and \(\arg z^2\).

Squaring doubles the angle and squares the length. Drag \(z\) around the unit circle and watch \(z^2\) go around twice as fast, always staying on the circle. Push \(z\) out past the unit circle and \(1/z\) dives inside it. That doubling is the seed of the whole next chapter.

Step-by-step: dividing by the conjugate

Check yourself at the third step: the denominator must be a plain real number with no \(i\) left in it. If an \(i\) survives down there, you multiplied by the wrong thing.

Quadratics that used to have no roots

The quadratic formula never fails now. For \(x^2+bx+c=0\) with \(b^2-4c<0\), write \(\sqrt{b^2-4c}=i\sqrt{4c-b^2}\):

\(x=\dfrac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4c}}{2}=-\dfrac{b}{2}\pm\dfrac{\sqrt{4c-b^2}}{2}\,i\)The two roots are a conjugate pair. Whenever a polynomial has real coefficients, complex roots come in conjugate pairs, because conjugating the whole equation leaves it unchanged.

This is the point of the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: every polynomial of degree \(n\) with complex coefficients has exactly \(n\) complex roots counted with multiplicity. Nothing is missing any more.

Powers of i

\(i^1=i\), \(i^2=-1\), \(i^3=-i\), \(i^4=1\), and then it repeats. The powers of \(i\) cycle with period 4, so \(i^n\) depends only on \(n \bmod 4\): \(i^{27}=i^{24}\cdot i^3=-i\).

Divide the exponent by 4 and keep the remainder. Remainder 0 gives 1, remainder 1 gives \(i\), remainder 2 gives \(-1\), remainder 3 gives \(-i\). For \((1+i)^n\) use \((1+i)^2=2i\) first, which turns a big power into a power of \(2i\).
\(\sqrt{ab}=\sqrt a\sqrt b\) is a real-number rule. It breaks here: \(\sqrt{-4}\sqrt{-9}=(2i)(3i)=-6\), not \(\sqrt{36}=6\). Convert every negative radicand to \(i\sqrt{\;}\) form first, then multiply.

Polar form, products, powers, and roots

Write a complex number as a length and a direction, and multiplication turns into one multiplication and one addition.

Any \(z=a+bi\) other than 0 can be written with polar coordinates. Let \(r=|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\) and let \(\theta\) be an angle with \(a=r\cos\theta\), \(b=r\sin\theta\). Then

\(z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\)\(r\) is the modulus, \(\theta\) is an argument of \(z\). Any two arguments differ by a multiple of \(2\pi\); the one with \(-\pi\lt\theta\le\pi\) is the principal argument, written \(\arg z\).

To find \(\theta\), do not just type \(\arctan(b/a)\) into a calculator. Arctangent always returns an angle in the right half plane, so it gets the second and third quadrants wrong by \(\pi\). Sketch the point first, then pick the angle that matches the quadrant. For \(-1+i\) the reference angle is \(\pi/4\) and the point is in quadrant II, so \(\theta=3\pi/4\).

Multiplication adds the angles

Take \(z_1=r_1(\cos\theta_1+i\sin\theta_1)\) and \(z_2=r_2(\cos\theta_2+i\sin\theta_2)\), multiply out, and collect with the addition formulas \(\cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2-\sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2=\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\) and \(\sin\theta_1\cos\theta_2+\cos\theta_1\sin\theta_2=\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\):

\(z_1z_2=r_1r_2\big(\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)+i\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\big)\qquad \dfrac{z_1}{z_2}=\dfrac{r_1}{r_2}\big(\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)+i\sin(\theta_1-\theta_2)\big)\)Multiply the moduli, add the arguments. Divide the moduli, subtract the arguments. Multiplying by a number of modulus 1 is a pure rotation.

Explore: multiplying is rotating and stretching

Drag near \(z\) or \(w\) to move it; both are held inside \(|\,\cdot\,|\le 1.7\) so the product stays on screen. The green point is \(zw\).

Press Spin and watch: \(w\) travels around a circle, and \(zw\) travels around a circle of radius \(|z||w|\) at exactly the same angular speed. Drag \(w\) onto the unit circle first, and then \(zw\) is just \(z\) rotated. Turn on the roots and step \(n\) up: the \(n\) roots always sit on one circle, equally spaced, starting at angle \((\arg z)/n\).

De Moivre's theorem

Multiply \(z\) by itself \(n\) times and the rule above says the modulus gets raised to the \(n\) and the argument gets multiplied by \(n\):

\(\big[r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\big]^n=r^n\big(\cos n\theta+i\sin n\theta\big)\)This is De Moivre's theorem. Computing \((1+i)^{10}\) by expanding the binomial takes ten minutes; in polar form it is \((\sqrt2)^{10}\) at angle \(10\cdot\frac{\pi}{4}\), so \(32\) at angle \(\frac{5\pi}{2}\), which is \(32i\).

Every number has exactly n nth roots

To solve \(\zeta^n=z\), write \(z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\) and remember that \(\theta+2\pi k\) is also an argument of \(z\) for every integer \(k\). De Moivre run backwards gives

\(\zeta_k=r^{1/n}\left[\cos\left(\dfrac{\theta+2\pi k}{n}\right)+i\sin\left(\dfrac{\theta+2\pi k}{n}\right)\right],\qquad k=0,1,\dots,n-1\)All \(n\) roots have the same modulus \(r^{1/n}\), so they sit on one circle. The first is at angle \(\theta/n\) and each next one is \(2\pi/n\) further around. After \(k=n-1\) you start repeating.

The \(n\)th roots of 1, the roots of unity, are the cleanest case: \(1, e^{2\pi i/n}, e^{4\pi i/n},\dots\), the vertices of a regular \(n\)-gon inscribed in the unit circle with one vertex at 1. The cube roots of unity are \(1\) and \(-\tfrac12\pm\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i\).

Step-by-step: all n of the nth roots

The count is the check. If you wrote down fewer than \(n\) roots you stopped early, and if you wrote more than \(n\) you kept going past \(k=n-1\) and repeated one.

Principal argument, every time. When a problem asks for \(\arg\), the answer must land in \(-\pi\lt\theta\le\pi\). After adding or multiplying angles, add or subtract \(2\pi\) until you are back in range: \(6\cdot\frac{3\pi}{4}=\frac{9\pi}{2}\) reduces to \(\frac{\pi}{2}\).
Why the roots are equally spaced. Any two \(n\)th roots differ by a factor whose \(n\)th power is 1, so they differ by an \(n\)th root of unity, which is a rotation by a multiple of \(2\pi/n\). Find one root and rotating it by \(2\pi/n\) generates all the rest.

Euler's formula

The exponential and the two trigonometric functions turn out to be the same function looked at from different directions. The bridge is a power series.

\(e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\)Euler's formula. The right side is the point at angle \(\theta\) on the unit circle, so \(e^{i\theta}\) is exactly "walk \(\theta\) radians counterclockwise around the unit circle starting at 1".

Where it comes from

Module 11 gave you three Maclaurin series that converge for every \(x\). Put \(x=i\theta\) into the first one and use the cycle \(i^2=-1\), \(i^3=-i\), \(i^4=1\):

Step-by-step: plug \(i\theta\) into the series for \(e^x\)

The whole derivation is one regrouping. The even powers of \(i\theta\) are real and reassemble the cosine series; the odd powers each carry exactly one factor of \(i\) and reassemble \(i\) times the sine series.

Set \(\theta=\pi\). Then \(\cos\pi=-1\) and \(\sin\pi=0\), so \(e^{i\pi}=-1\), which is usually written

\(e^{i\pi}+1=0\)One equation containing \(e\), \(i\), \(\pi\), \(1\), and \(0\). It says nothing mysterious: walking \(\pi\) radians counterclockwise from 1 around the unit circle lands you at \(-1\).

Other immediate consequences: \(e^{2\pi i}=1\), \(e^{i\pi/2}=i\), and \(|e^{i\theta}|=\sqrt{\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta}=1\) for every real \(\theta\). The exponential of a purely imaginary number always has modulus 1.

Explore: the series spirals into the circle

Each segment is one term \(\frac{(i\theta)^n}{n!}\) added to the running total. Orange segments are the even terms (real, horizontal), blue segments are the odd terms (imaginary, vertical). The green point is \(e^{i\theta}\) on the unit circle.

Set \(\theta=\pi\) and push \(N\) up: the path staggers out to a distance of about 5 and then spirals back in to \(-1\), which is Euler's identity being computed one term at a time. Watch the colors alternate: the orange steps only ever move horizontally and the blue steps only ever move vertically, which is the split into \(\cos\theta\) and \(i\sin\theta\).

The exponential form and what it buys you

\(z=re^{i\theta}\qquad z_1z_2=r_1r_2e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}\qquad \dfrac{z_1}{z_2}=\dfrac{r_1}{r_2}e^{i(\theta_1-\theta_2)}\qquad z^n=r^ne^{in\theta}\)Every rule from chapter 2 is now just the exponent law \(e^{A}e^{B}=e^{A+B}\). De Moivre's theorem becomes one line: \((e^{i\theta})^n=e^{in\theta}\).

For a general complex exponent, split it into real and imaginary parts:

\(e^{a+bi}=e^ae^{bi}=e^a(\cos b+i\sin b)\)The real part of the exponent sets the modulus \(e^a\); the imaginary part sets the angle \(b\). So \(e^{\ln 2+i\pi/3}=2\left(\tfrac12+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i\right)=1+\sqrt3\,i\).

Adding and subtracting \(e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\) and \(e^{-i\theta}=\cos\theta-i\sin\theta\) solves for the two trigonometric functions:

\(\cos\theta=\dfrac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}{2}\qquad\qquad \sin\theta=\dfrac{e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}}{2i}\)Sine and cosine are combinations of two exponentials. This is how you turn a trigonometric identity into an algebra problem about exponents.

That is the practical payoff. Squaring \(e^{i\theta}\) two different ways gives the double angle formulas in one line:

\(\cos 2\theta+i\sin 2\theta=e^{2i\theta}=\big(e^{i\theta}\big)^2=(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^2=(\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta)+i(2\sin\theta\cos\theta)\)Match real parts: \(\cos2\theta=\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta\). Match imaginary parts: \(\sin2\theta=2\sin\theta\cos\theta\). Cubing gives the triple angle formulas the same way.

Products of exponentials give the product to sum formulas, and \(\ln\) can be extended too: since \(-1=e^{i\pi}\), the principal logarithm is \(\ln(-1)=i\pi\), and in general \(\ln z=\ln|z|+i\arg z\).

\(e^{i\theta}\) is never zero and never large. It sits on the unit circle for every real \(\theta\), so \(|e^{i\theta}|=1\) always. If a computation hands you \(|e^{i\theta}|\ne1\), you multiplied a modulus somewhere you should have added an angle.
