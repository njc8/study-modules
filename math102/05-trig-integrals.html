Trigonometric integrals

Four identities and two "save one factor" moves handle every product of sines, cosines, tangents, and secants you will meet.

How to use this

Work through the chapters in order. Each one teaches an idea, gives you something to slide or step through, then drills you with fresh random problems until you hit the goal. Progress is saved in this browser.

Antiderivatives can be written with or without + C. Powers are typed either way: sin(x)^3 or sin^3(x), and the ^ key builds the exponent for you. Absolute values come from the | key, so ln|sec x| types straight through. Numbers can be exact: pi/6, 3/8, 2/15, 1 - pi/4, 3/2 - ln(2), sqrt(3).

The checker compares your answer to the reference numerically, so any equivalent form is accepted, including forms that differ by a constant. If you get \(-\tfrac12\cos^2x\) and the book prints \(\tfrac12\sin^2x\), both are marked correct: they differ by the constant \(\tfrac12\). Press Enter to check, and Enter again for a new question.

What you will be able to do at the end

  • Rewrite any even power of sine or cosine with the half-angle identities, so it can be integrated on sight.
  • Integrate \(\sin^m x\cos^n x\) for any \(m\) and \(n\), by saving one factor when a power is odd and by half-angle identities when both are even.
  • Integrate \(\tan^m x\sec^n x\), and produce the four standard antiderivatives \(\int\tan x\,dx\), \(\int\cot x\,dx\), \(\int\sec x\,dx\), \(\int\csc x\,dx\) from memory.
  • Turn a product of two different frequencies into a sum with the product-to-sum identities, and use it to integrate.
  • Evaluate the definite versions that show up in polar area and in Fourier series, including the orthogonality integrals.

Chapter 1 · Stewart 7.2

The identities that do the work

Every trigonometric integral in this module is one of two moves: trade an even power for a lower frequency, or save one factor and substitute.

Three Pythagorean identities convert one squared function into another. They are the reason an even power can always be traded away:

\(\sin^2x+\cos^2x=1 \qquad 1+\tan^2x=\sec^2x \qquad 1+\cot^2x=\csc^2x\)Divide the first by \(\cos^2x\) to get the second, by \(\sin^2x\) to get the third. You never need to memorize all three separately.

The half-angle identities are the workhorses. They come straight from the double angle formula \(\cos 2x = 2\cos^2x-1 = 1-2\sin^2x\). Solve that for \(\cos^2 x\), then for \(\sin^2x\):

\(\cos^2x=\dfrac{1+\cos 2x}{2}\qquad\qquad \sin^2x=\dfrac{1-\cos 2x}{2}\)A squared function of \(x\) becomes a first power of a function of \(2x\). Squares are hard to integrate; first powers are not.

Read the right side as a constant plus a wiggle. The constant is \(\tfrac12\), and the wiggle \(\pm\tfrac12\cos 2x\) averages to zero over a full period. That single sentence is the whole reason these identities exist: the average value of \(\sin^2x\) or \(\cos^2x\) over a period is exactly \(\tfrac12\).

Explore: the average of a square

Blue is the square. Orange dashed is its half-angle rewrite \(\tfrac12\mp\tfrac12\cos 2x\), drawn on top so you can see the two curves are the same curve. The gray dashed line is \(y=\tfrac12\).

Slide \(b\) to a full period, \(b=2\pi\). The shaded area is exactly \(\pi\), which is half of the width \(2\pi\): the wiggle above the line \(y=\tfrac12\) cancels the dip below it. Now stop at \(b=\pi/2\) and watch the running average sit above \(\tfrac12\) for \(\sin^2\) and below it for \(\cos^2\). Over any whole number of half periods the two agree.

Two more rewrites fall out of the same two identities and get used constantly:

\(\sin^4x=\dfrac{3-4\cos 2x+\cos 4x}{8}\qquad \sin^2x\cos^2x=\dfrac{1-\cos 4x}{8}\)Square \(\sin^2x=\tfrac{1-\cos 2x}{2}\) and apply the identity again to the \(\cos^2 2x\) it produces. For the second one, use \(\sin x\cos x=\tfrac12\sin 2x\) first, then square.
The shortcut for \(\sin^2x\cos^2x\). Do not expand it. Recognize \(\sin x\cos x=\tfrac12\sin 2x\), square to get \(\tfrac14\sin^2 2x\), then half-angle that: \(\tfrac14\cdot\tfrac{1-\cos 4x}{2}=\tfrac{1-\cos 4x}{8}\). Two lines instead of six.

Integrating the rewritten form is immediate, because \(\int\cos(kx)\,dx=\tfrac1k\sin(kx)\):

\(\displaystyle\int\sin^2(kx)\,dx=\frac{x}{2}-\frac{\sin(2kx)}{4k}+C\qquad \int\cos^2(kx)\,dx=\frac{x}{2}+\frac{\sin(2kx)}{4k}+C\)Only the sign in front of the sine changes. The \(x/2\) is the average value \(\tfrac12\) times the width, and the sine term is the wiggle.
The inner constant divides twice. In \(\int\sin^2(3x)\,dx\) the rewrite gives \(\tfrac12-\tfrac12\cos 6x\), and integrating \(\cos 6x\) divides by 6, not by 3. The answer is \(\tfrac{x}{2}-\tfrac{\sin 6x}{12}\). Differentiate to check whenever you are unsure.

Chapter 2 · Stewart 7.2

Powers of sine and cosine

One question decides the whole problem: is a power odd? If it is, save one factor and substitute. If not, use the half-angle identities.

The reason an odd power is easy is that \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) are each other's derivative, up to a sign. Saving one factor turns it into the \(du\):

\(u=\sin x\;\Rightarrow\;du=\cos x\,dx\qquad\qquad u=\cos x\;\Rightarrow\;du=-\sin x\,dx\)The saved factor is exactly what \(du\) needs. Everything left over must be turned into the new variable, and only an even power can be, because \(\cos^2x=1-\sin^2x\).

The decision, for \(\displaystyle\int\sin^m x\cos^n x\,dx\)

  1. \(n\) odd (the cosine power). Save one \(\cos x\). The remaining \(\cos^{n-1}x\) has an even power, so write it as \((1-\sin^2x)^{(n-1)/2}\). Substitute \(u=\sin x\).
  2. \(m\) odd (the sine power). Save one \(\sin x\). Write \(\sin^{m-1}x=(1-\cos^2x)^{(m-1)/2}\). Substitute \(u=\cos x\), and carry the minus sign from \(du=-\sin x\,dx\).
  3. Both even. There is nothing to save. Use \(\sin^2x=\tfrac{1-\cos 2x}{2}\) and \(\cos^2x=\tfrac{1+\cos 2x}{2}\) to halve every power, and repeat if a square survives.
  4. Both odd. Either choice works. Save from the smaller power: it leaves less to expand.

Three worked examples, in the order you will meet them.

\(\int\sin^3x\,dx\). The sine power is odd. Save one: \(\int\sin^2x\,\sin x\,dx=\int(1-\cos^2x)\sin x\,dx\). With \(u=\cos x\), \(du=-\sin x\,dx\), this is \(-\int(1-u^2)\,du=-u+\tfrac{u^3}{3}\), so the answer is \(-\cos x+\tfrac13\cos^3x+C\).

\(\int\sin^2x\cos^3x\,dx\). The cosine power is odd. Save one cosine: \(\int\sin^2x(1-\sin^2x)\cos x\,dx\). With \(u=\sin x\) this is \(\int(u^2-u^4)\,du=\tfrac{u^3}{3}-\tfrac{u^5}{5}\), so \(\tfrac13\sin^3x-\tfrac15\sin^5x+C\).

\(\int\cos^4x\,dx\). Both even, so no substitution. \(\cos^4x=\left(\tfrac{1+\cos 2x}{2}\right)^2=\tfrac14(1+2\cos 2x+\cos^2 2x)\), and the surviving square needs the identity again: \(\cos^2 2x=\tfrac{1+\cos 4x}{2}\). The result is \(\tfrac38 x+\tfrac14\sin 2x+\tfrac1{32}\sin 4x+C\).

Step-by-step: save a factor and substitute

Check yourself at the substitution step: after you replace \(\sin x\) or \(\cos x\) by \(u\), not a single trigonometric function may remain. If one does, you saved the wrong factor.

Two ways to get this wrong. In \(\int\sin^3x\cos^3x\,dx\), save one cosine, not both: the point is to leave an even power behind, not to strip the factor bare. And never try to save a factor from \(\int\sin^2x\cos^2x\,dx\): both powers are even, so whichever factor you pull out leaves an odd power that the Pythagorean identity cannot convert. That one is a half-angle problem.

Set \(n\) odd and watch the curve stay lopsided about \(x=\pi/2\): the cosine factor changes sign there, and the whole integral over \([0,\pi]\) collapses to zero. Set both powers even and the curve is a nonnegative hump with a genuinely positive area, which is exactly the case that needs the half-angle identities.

Chapter 3 · Stewart 7.2

Powers of tangent and secant

Same game, different pair. Two derivative facts and one Pythagorean identity decide everything.

\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\tan x=\sec^2x\qquad \dfrac{d}{dx}\sec x=\sec x\tan x\qquad 1+\tan^2x=\sec^2x\)The two derivatives tell you what a usable \(du\) looks like: either a lone \(\sec^2x\), or the pair \(\sec x\tan x\) together. The identity converts whatever is left.

The decision, for \(\displaystyle\int\tan^m x\sec^n x\,dx\)

  1. \(n\) even (the secant power), \(n\ge 2\). Save \(\sec^2x\) for \(du\). The remaining \(\sec^{n-2}x\) has an even power, so write it as \((1+\tan^2x)^{(n-2)/2}\). Substitute \(u=\tan x\).
  2. \(m\) odd (the tangent power). Save the pair \(\sec x\tan x\) for \(du\). The remaining \(\tan^{m-1}x\) is an even power, so write it as \((\sec^2x-1)^{(m-1)/2}\). Substitute \(u=\sec x\).
  3. Neither. Even tangent power with no secant, or an odd secant power with no tangent. These reduce to the standard list below, usually with \(\tan^2x=\sec^2x-1\) or with integration by parts.

The four basic antiderivatives are pure memory. Two of them are substitutions you could do; two are tricks you would not invent under time pressure.

\(\displaystyle\int\tan x\,dx=\ln|\sec x|+C \qquad \int\cot x\,dx=\ln|\sin x|+C\)\(\int\tan x\,dx=\int\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}dx\) with \(u=\cos x\) gives \(-\ln|\cos x|\), which is the same as \(\ln|\sec x|\).
\(\displaystyle\int\sec x\,dx=\ln|\sec x+\tan x|+C \qquad \int\csc x\,dx=\ln|\csc x-\cot x|+C\)Multiply and divide by \(\sec x+\tan x\): the numerator becomes exactly the derivative of the denominator. Memorize the result instead of rediscovering it.

Two more that show up constantly. The first is a one-line identity, the second is the by-parts loop from Module 2:

\(\displaystyle\int\tan^2x\,dx=\tan x-x+C \qquad \int\sec^3x\,dx=\tfrac12\big(\sec x\tan x+\ln|\sec x+\tan x|\big)+C\)For the first, \(\tan^2x=\sec^2x-1\). For the second, take \(u=\sec x\), \(dv=\sec^2x\,dx\); the integral reappears on the right and you solve for it.
Cotangent and cosecant are the mirror image. \(\frac{d}{dx}\cot x=-\csc^2x\) and \(\frac{d}{dx}\csc x=-\csc x\cot x\). Every rule above carries over with a sign flip, so nothing new has to be learned. Stewart's problems are written in tangent and secant almost every time.

Step-by-step: tangent and secant

Notice which identity each branch uses. Saving \(\sec^2x\) leaves secants, which become \(1+\tan^2x\). Saving \(\sec x\tan x\) leaves tangents, which become \(\sec^2x-1\). Reaching for the wrong one is the usual slip.

An odd secant power with an even tangent power is the hard case. \(\int\sec^3x\,dx\) and \(\int\tan^2x\sec x\,dx\) have no substitution: there is no lone \(\sec^2x\) and no \(\sec x\tan x\) pair to save. Convert to pure secants and use the \(\int\sec^3 x\,dx\) formula.

Chapter 4 · Stewart 7.2

Products with different frequencies

When the two factors have different inner multipliers, no substitution helps. Turn the product into a sum instead.

Add and subtract the angle addition formulas and three identities drop out. They convert a product of two waves into a sum of two waves, and a sum is something you can integrate one term at a time:

\(\sin A\cos B=\tfrac12\big[\sin(A-B)+\sin(A+B)\big]\)From \(\sin(A+B)+\sin(A-B)=2\sin A\cos B\).
\(\sin A\sin B=\tfrac12\big[\cos(A-B)-\cos(A+B)\big] \qquad \cos A\cos B=\tfrac12\big[\cos(A-B)+\cos(A+B)\big]\)Both come from \(\cos(A\mp B)=\cos A\cos B\pm\sin A\sin B\). Note the minus sign in the sine-sine one: it is the only asymmetry to remember.

Every product of two different frequencies becomes a beat: a slow wave at the difference frequency \(m-n\) and a fast one at the sum frequency \(m+n\), each with amplitude \(\tfrac12\). This is the first step of a Fourier series, where a signal is broken into pure frequencies by integrating it against \(\sin(nx)\) and \(\cos(nx)\).

\(n\)

Set \(m=n\) and watch the difference frequency collapse to a constant: the slow wave becomes the flat line \(\tfrac12\), the signed areas stop cancelling, and the integral jumps from \(0\) to \(\pi/2\). That jump is orthogonality, and it is the reason a Fourier coefficient picks out exactly one frequency.

\(\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sin(mx)\cos(nx)\,dx=0 \quad\text{always}\qquad \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos(mx)\cos(nx)\,dx=\begin{cases}0,& m\ne n\\ \pi,& m=n\end{cases}\)The same holds for \(\sin(mx)\sin(nx)\). Distinct frequencies are orthogonal over a full period: their product has as much area above the axis as below.
Check the difference frequency first. If \(m=n\) the term \(\cos((m-n)x)\) is \(\cos 0=1\), a constant, and it integrates to \(x\), not to a sine. Forgetting that is how a nonzero orthogonality integral turns into a wrong zero.
