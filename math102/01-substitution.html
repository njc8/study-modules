Chapter 1 · Stewart 5.5

Spotting \(u\)

The chain rule says \(\frac{d}{dx}F(g(x))=F'(g(x))\,g'(x)\). Read that right to left and you have the whole technique.

If an integrand looks like a function of something, times the derivative of that something, then it is a chain rule derivative in disguise. Undo it by naming the something \(u\).

\(\displaystyle\int f(g(x))\,g'(x)\,dx=\int f(u)\,du \qquad\text{where } u=g(x),\; du=g'(x)\,dx\)The whole content of the rule: replace \(g(x)\) by \(u\) and replace \(g'(x)\,dx\) by \(du\). What is left must be an integral you already know.

The mechanical version is three lines. Write \(u=g(x)\). Differentiate to get \(du=g'(x)\,dx\). Solve that for whatever piece of the integrand you actually have, then swap.

The checklist

  1. Is there an inside function? Something under a root, inside a power, inside \(\sin\), inside \(e^{(\;)}\), inside \(\ln\), or in a denominator. Call it \(u\).
  2. Is its derivative outside, up to a constant? If \(u=x^3+5\) then \(u'=3x^2\), and an \(x^2\) sitting outside is close enough: constants are free.
  3. Do the constant adjustment. From \(du=3x^2\,dx\) you get \(x^2\,dx=\tfrac{du}{3}\). Pull the \(\tfrac13\) out front.
  4. Rewrite completely in \(u\). No \(x\) may survive.
  5. Integrate, then substitute back \(u=g(x)\), and add \(+\,C\).

Explore: the u-spotter, one step at a time

Watch the two colors. Orange is everything that becomes \(u\). Blue is everything that becomes \(du\). If a piece of the integrand is neither color by step 4, the substitution is not going to close.

\(du\) must absorb everything that is left. After you substitute, no \(x\) may survive. If one does, either the substitution is wrong, or you need the back-substitution trick from Chapter 3 (solve \(u=g(x)\) for \(x\) and replace the leftover too).

Check by differentiating

An antiderivative is right exactly when its derivative is the integrand. That is a check you can always run, and it costs ten seconds.

Explore: the slope of \(F\) is the integrand

Purple: the antiderivative \(F\). Orange: the integrand \(f\). Green: the tangent line to \(F\) at the marked point.

Slide \(x_0\) and watch two numbers stay locked together: the slope of the purple curve and the height of the orange curve. Where the orange curve crosses zero the purple curve is flat. Where orange is negative, purple falls. That correspondence is the only thing "antiderivative" means.

Constants are free, variables are not. You may pull \(\tfrac13\) out of an integral. You may never pull an \(x\) out. If your \(du\) leaves you needing to divide by \(x\), the substitution failed.

Chapter 2 · Stewart 5.5

Definite integrals: change the limits

Once you substitute, the old limits are limits for the old variable. Convert them and you never have to go back.

\(\displaystyle\int_a^b f(g(x))\,g'(x)\,dx=\int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u)\,du\)The limits travel through \(g\) exactly like the variable does. The left side sweeps \(x\) from \(a\) to \(b\); the right side sweeps \(u\) from \(g(a)\) to \(g(b)\).

You have two legal options and one of them is better.

  • Substitute back. Find the antiderivative in \(u\), rewrite it in \(x\), then use the original limits \(a\) and \(b\).
  • Change the limits. Convert \(a\mapsto g(a)\) and \(b\mapsto g(b)\) right when you write \(du\), then finish entirely in \(u\). Recommended: it is shorter, and it removes the most common error, which is plugging \(x\)-limits into a \(u\)-antiderivative.
Reversed limits are fine. If \(g\) is decreasing you can get something like \(\int_2^{0}\). Do not flip it and do not take absolute values. Keep the order the substitution gives you, or flip it and change the sign: \(\int_2^0=-\int_0^2\).

Explore: two pictures, one number

in \(x\): the integrand on \([a,\,b]\)

in \(u\): the integrand on \([g(a),\,g(b)]\)

Sweep the upper limit and watch the two shaded areas. The shapes are completely different, the widths are different, the heights are different, and the two numbers are equal at every instant. Substitution is not an approximation, it is a re-labelling of the same accumulated area.

Step-by-step: a definite integral by substitution

Step 2 is the one people skip. Write \(u(a)\) and \(u(b)\) out as numbers before you touch the integrand, and the rest of the problem has no \(x\) in it anywhere.

The new limits are numbers, not expressions. \(g(a)\) and \(g(b)\) are evaluated immediately. If a limit still has an \(x\) in it you substituted into the wrong slot.

Chapter 3 · Stewart 5.5

Symmetry, splitting, and back-substitution

Three moves that turn an integral you cannot do into one you can, or into nothing at all.

Symmetry on \([-a,\,a]\)

A function is odd when \(f(-x)=-f(x)\) and even when \(f(-x)=f(x)\). Over an interval centered at the origin this decides a lot.

\(\displaystyle\int_{-a}^{a} f(x)\,dx = 0 \;\;(f \text{ odd}) \qquad\qquad \int_{-a}^{a} f(x)\,dx = 2\int_0^a f(x)\,dx \;\;(f \text{ even})\)Odd: the area to the left of 0 is the exact negative of the area to the right, so they cancel. Even: the two halves are mirror images, so double one of them.

This is worth real points, because it works on integrands with no elementary antiderivative at all. Nobody can write \(\int x e^{x^2}\cos x\,dx\) in closed form, but \(\int_{-3}^{3} x e^{x^2}\cos x\,dx=0\) is immediate: odd times even is odd.

Quick parity arithmetic: odd \(\times\) odd \(=\) even, odd \(\times\) even \(=\) odd, even \(\times\) even \(=\) even. Powers \(x^{\text{odd}}\), \(\sin x\), \(\tan x\) are odd; \(x^{\text{even}}\), \(\cos x\), \(|x|\), and any function of \(x^2\) are even.

Splitting off a known area

An integral does not have to be done in one piece. Split the integrand into a sum, and handle each piece with whichever tool fits. Stewart 5.5.83 is the model problem:

\(\displaystyle\int_{-4}^{4}(x+2)\sqrt{16-x^2}\,dx=\underbrace{\int_{-4}^{4}x\sqrt{16-x^2}\,dx}_{\text{odd, so }0}+\underbrace{2\int_{-4}^{4}\sqrt{16-x^2}\,dx}_{2\,\cdot\,\text{half disk of radius }4}=0+2\cdot 8\pi=16\pi\)The first piece is odd and dies. The second is twice the area of a semicircle of radius 4, which is \(\tfrac12\pi(4)^2=8\pi\). No antiderivative is needed anywhere.

The area fact behind it: \(y=\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\) is the upper half of the circle \(x^2+y^2=a^2\), so \(\displaystyle\int_{-a}^{a}\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\,dx=\tfrac12\pi a^2\) and \(\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\,dx=\tfrac14\pi a^2\).

Explore: odd plus even

\(x\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\) : odd

\(c\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\) : even

\((x+c)\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\) : the sum

The odd picture has two lobes that are mirror images with opposite signs, so its signed area is exactly zero no matter what \(a\) is. The even picture is a scaled half-disk whose area you know without integrating. The third picture is lopsided and looks hard, and it is just the sum of a zero and a half-disk.

Back-substitution: when an \(x\) survives

Sometimes \(u=g(x)\) clears the hard part but leaves a stray \(x\) behind. Do not give up: solve \(u=g(x)\) for \(x\) and substitute that too.

\(\displaystyle\int x\sqrt{x+c}\,dx \;\xrightarrow{\;u=x+c,\;x=u-c,\;dx=du\;}\; \int (u-c)\sqrt{u}\,du=\int\left(u^{3/2}-c\,u^{1/2}\right)du=\tfrac25 u^{5/2}-\tfrac{2c}{3}u^{3/2}+C\)Then put \(u=x+c\) back. The trick is that \(x\) is a simple function of \(u\), so replacing it costs nothing and the integrand becomes a sum of powers.

The same move handles \(\int x(ax+b)^n\,dx\) with \(x=\frac{u-b}{a}\), and \(\int \frac{x}{(x+c)^2}\,dx\), which becomes \(\int\frac{u-c}{u^2}\,du=\ln|u|+\frac{c}{u}+C\).

The alternative: let \(u\) be the whole radical. For \(\int x\sqrt{2x+1}\,dx\) you may set \(u=\sqrt{2x+1}\), so \(u^2=2x+1\), \(x=\frac{u^2-1}{2}\), and \(dx=u\,du\). The integrand becomes a polynomial in \(u\). Both routes are correct; pick whichever leaves you with powers instead of roots.
