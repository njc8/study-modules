Chapter 1 · Stewart 11.4

Comparison and limit comparison

You already know two families cold: \(\sum 1/n^p\) and \(\sum r^n\). Every comparison test says the same thing: your series behaves like the family member it sits next to.

Both tests in this chapter require positive terms. That is what makes partial sums increasing, and an increasing sequence either climbs to a finite limit or runs off to infinity, with nothing in between.

\(\text{If } 0\le a_n\le b_n \text{ and } \sum b_n \text{ converges, then } \sum a_n \text{ converges.}\)Trapped under something finite. The partial sums of \(\sum a_n\) are increasing and bounded above by \(\sum b_n\), so they have a limit.
\(\text{If } 0\le b_n\le a_n \text{ and } \sum b_n \text{ diverges, then } \sum a_n \text{ diverges.}\)Pushed up by something infinite. The partial sums of \(\sum a_n\) are at least those of \(\sum b_n\), which already run to infinity.
The two useless combinations. Bigger than a convergent series tells you nothing. Smaller than a divergent series tells you nothing. Before you write anything down, decide which way your inequality points and whether that direction is the useful one.

The inequality only has to hold eventually, that is for all \(n\ge N\) for some \(N\). Changing or dropping finitely many terms cannot change convergence, only the value of the sum.

Choosing \(b_n\): keep only the dominant terms

To pick the comparison series, throw away everything that does not matter as \(n\to\infty\), then simplify:

\(\dfrac{3n^2+2}{n^4+n}\;\longrightarrow\;\dfrac{3n^2}{n^4}=\dfrac{3}{n^2}\;\longrightarrow\; b_n=\dfrac{1}{n^2}\qquad\qquad \dfrac{1}{2^n+n}\;\longrightarrow\;b_n=\left(\tfrac12\right)^{\!n}\)Highest power on top over highest power on the bottom for a rational function. For a sum of an exponential and a polynomial, the exponential wins. Constant multiples never matter, so drop the 3.

Radicals count fractional powers: \(\sqrt{n^3+1}\) behaves like \(n^{3/2}\), so \(1/\sqrt{n^3+1}\) is compared with \(1/n^{3/2}\), a convergent \(p\)-series. Then check the direction: \(\sqrt{n^3+1}>\sqrt{n^3}\), so \(a_n<1/n^{3/2}\), and the comparison test finishes it.

When the inequality points the wrong way

Take \(\sum \dfrac{1}{n-1}\) (for \(n\ge 2\)). The obvious comparison is \(b_n=1/n\), but \(\dfrac{1}{n-1}>\dfrac{1}{n}\), and \(\sum 1/n\) diverges, so this time the direction is useful. Now take \(\sum \dfrac{1}{n+1}\): here \(a_n<1/n\) with \(\sum 1/n\) divergent, which is the useless combination. The fix is the limit comparison test, which does not care about the direction at all.

\(\text{If } a_n,b_n>0 \text{ and } \displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n}{b_n}=c \text{ with } 0\lt c\lt\infty, \text{ then } \sum a_n \text{ and } \sum b_n \text{ both converge or both diverge.}\)A finite positive \(c\) means the two sequences are eventually within a constant factor of each other in both directions. Constant factors cannot change convergence.

In practice: pick \(b_n\) from the dominant terms, compute \(c=\lim a_n/b_n\) (usually by dividing top and bottom by the highest power), check that \(c\) is a positive number, and then quote what you know about \(\sum b_n\).

Explore: the ratio settles at \(c\)

Orange dots are \(a_n\), blue dots are \(b_n\). Switch to partial sums to see the two totals move together or apart.

Watch the last column of the readout. The ratio \(a_n/b_n\) settling at a positive number is the whole test: after that point the two series are within a constant factor of each other forever, so they live or die together. Try the last preset, where the ratio runs off to infinity: limit comparison does not apply, but the one-sided comparison \(a_n\ge b_n\) still works.

Bounded factors are free. \(2+\cos n\) is stuck between 1 and 3, so \(\dfrac{2+\cos n}{n^2}\le\dfrac{3}{n^2}\) and the series converges. Any factor that is trapped between two positive constants can be replaced by a constant when you choose \(b_n\).
Logs lose to every power. For any \(\varepsilon>0\), \(\ln n\lt n^{\varepsilon}\) eventually. So \(\sum \dfrac{\ln n}{n^p}\) converges exactly when \(p>1\), and so does \(\sum\dfrac{(\ln n)^k}{n^p}\) for any fixed \(k\). A logarithm never changes the threshold, it only slows the approach to it.

Chapter 2 · Stewart 11.5

Alternating series

Signs that flip make the partial sums close in on the answer from both sides. That is the one test where you get an error bound for free.

An alternating series is \(\sum(-1)^{n-1}b_n=b_1-b_2+b_3-b_4+\cdots\) with every \(b_n>0\). Write the series that way first: pull the sign out, and let \(b_n=|a_n|\) be the positive size of the term.

\(\text{Alternating series test: if } b_{n+1}\le b_n \text{ eventually and } \lim_{n\to\infty}b_n=0, \text{ then } \sum(-1)^{n-1}b_n \text{ converges.}\)Two conditions: the sizes decrease, and they go to zero. Nothing about how fast.

Why it works: \(s_2\le s_4\le s_6\le\cdots\) climbs and \(s_1\ge s_3\ge s_5\ge\cdots\) falls, and every even partial sum sits below every odd one. The two sequences squeeze together because the gap between consecutive partial sums is \(b_{n+1}\to 0\). The sum is caught between them.

That squeeze gives the estimate you actually use:

\(|R_N|=|s-s_N|\le b_{N+1}\)Stop after \(N\) terms and the error is no bigger than the first term you left out, and it has that term's sign. No other elementary test hands you an error bound this cheaply.

Explore: bracketing the sum

Purple dots are the partial sums \(s_N\). The dashed line is the sum \(s\). The orange band is \(s_N\pm b_{N+1}\).

Press Play and watch the dots hop over the dashed line, never twice on the same side. The true sum is always between two consecutive partial sums, which is exactly why the leftover term bounds the error. Now try the preset that fails: the terms do not shrink to zero, the dots never settle, and the series diverges by the test for divergence.

Checking "decreasing" when it is not obvious

For \(b_n=\dfrac{n^2}{n^3+1}\) you cannot see it at a glance. Treat \(n\) as a real variable and differentiate \(f(x)=\dfrac{x^2}{x^3+1}\):

\(f'(x)=\dfrac{2x(x^3+1)-x^2(3x^2)}{(x^3+1)^2}=\dfrac{x(2-x^3)}{(x^3+1)^2}<0 \text{ for } x>2^{1/3}\)Negative derivative from some point on is exactly "eventually decreasing", which is all the test needs.

The other honest option is to check \(b_{n+1}/b_n\le 1\) or \(b_{n+1}-b_n\le 0\) directly.

The limit condition is not optional, and it is the first thing to check. If \(b_n\not\to 0\) then \(a_n\not\to 0\), so the series diverges by the test for divergence. Do not write "the alternating series test fails, so it diverges": the test never proves divergence. The divergence test does. For \(\sum(-1)^n\dfrac{n}{n+1}\) the terms approach \(\pm 1\), so it diverges.
The alternating harmonic series. \(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n}=1-\tfrac12+\tfrac13-\tfrac14+\cdots=\ln 2\) converges, while \(\sum 1/n\) diverges. Flipping signs can rescue a divergent series. That gap is what the next chapter is about.

Chapter 3 · Stewart 11.6

Absolute convergence, the ratio test, and the root test

Take absolute values and the series becomes a positive-term series, where every test you own applies. If that stronger series converges, the original one had no chance to diverge.

\(\sum a_n\) is absolutely convergent when \(\sum|a_n|\) converges, and conditionally convergent when \(\sum a_n\) converges but \(\sum|a_n|\) does not.

\(\sum|a_n| \text{ converges}\;\Longrightarrow\;\sum a_n \text{ converges}\)Absolute convergence implies convergence. The converse is false: \(\sum(-1)^{n-1}/n\) converges, \(\sum 1/n\) does not.

So the practical order is: take absolute values first and test \(\sum|a_n|\). If that converges you are finished, with the stronger conclusion. Only if \(\sum|a_n|\) diverges do you go back to the alternating series test to see whether the original series is conditionally convergent.

The ratio test

\(L=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\dfrac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right|:\qquad L<1\Rightarrow \sum a_n \text{ converges absolutely};\qquad L>1 \text{ or } L=\infty\Rightarrow \text{diverges};\qquad L=1\Rightarrow \text{no information.}\)The series is eventually compared with a geometric series of ratio \(L\). When \(L>1\) the terms grow, so they cannot go to zero, which is why divergence follows.

The cancellations you need are always the same three:

\(\dfrac{(n+1)!}{n!}=n+1,\qquad \dfrac{c^{\,n+1}}{c^{\,n}}=c,\qquad \dfrac{(n+1)^k}{n^k}=\left(1+\tfrac1n\right)^{k}\to 1\)Factorials leave one factor, powers leave the base, and polynomial pieces leave 1. Write \(a_{n+1}\) by replacing every \(n\) with \(n+1\), then divide and cancel before taking any limit.
\(L=1\) is not a verdict. Every \(p\)-series gives \(L=1\): \(\sum 1/n\) diverges and \(\sum 1/n^2\) converges, and the ratio test cannot tell them apart. If you land on \(L=1\), the ratio test is over and you go back to comparison, the integral test, or the alternating series test.

The root test

\(L=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}:\qquad L<1\Rightarrow \text{converges absolutely};\qquad L>1 \text{ or } L=\infty\Rightarrow \text{diverges};\qquad L=1\Rightarrow \text{no information.}\)Same three cases. Use it when the whole term is an \(n\)th power, because the root just peels the exponent off.

Two limits do most of the work here: \(\sqrt[n]{n}\to 1\) and \(\left(1+\tfrac{x}{n}\right)^n\to e^{x}\). So \(\left(\dfrac{2n+3}{3n+2}\right)^{n}\) has \(\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}=\dfrac{2n+3}{3n+2}\to\tfrac23<1\), and \(\left(\dfrac{n}{n+1}\right)^{n^2}\) has \(\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}=\left(1-\tfrac{1}{n+1}\right)^{n}\to e^{-1}<1\).

Explore: which limit is settling down

Orange dots: \(|a_{n+1}/a_n|\). Blue dots: \(\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}\). The gray line is the deciding level \(L=1\).

Both sequences chase the same limit, but at very different speeds. On the factorial presets the ratio locks on immediately while the root crawls, which is exactly why factorials mean ratio test. On the \(n\)th-power presets it is the other way round. On \(\sum 1/n^2\) both crawl toward 1 and neither test can ever conclude.

Step-by-step: running the ratio test

Check yourself at step 3: after cancelling, no factorial should be left anywhere, and every surviving \(n\) should sit in a ratio of polynomials or a single constant. If a factorial survives, you did not write out \((n+1)!=(n+1)\cdot n!\).

Which test for which shape. Factorials, \(n^n\), or a constant to the \(n\): ratio. The whole term raised to the \(n\)th power: root. Neither, and the terms are a rational function of \(n\): comparison or limit comparison, because ratio and root both give \(L=1\) there.

Chapter 4 · Stewart 11.7

Strategy: reading the shape

There is no algorithm, but there is an order. Run down this list and stop at the first line that fits the series in front of you.

  1. Do the terms go to zero? If \(a_n\not\to 0\), the series diverges by the test for divergence. This costs one limit and settles a surprising number of exam problems.
  2. Is it a \(p\)-series or a geometric series? \(\sum 1/n^p\) converges exactly when \(p>1\). \(\sum ar^{n}\) converges exactly when \(|r|<1\), to first term over \(1-r\).
  3. Is it a rational or algebraic function of \(n\)? Keep the dominant powers, compare or limit compare with a \(p\)-series.
  4. Does it alternate? Check \(b_n\to 0\) and \(b_n\) decreasing, and use the alternating series test. Take absolute values first if you want the stronger conclusion.
  5. Does it contain \(n!\), \(n^n\), or \(c^{\,n}\)? Ratio test. The cancellation is the whole computation.
  6. Is the whole term an \(n\)th power? Root test.
  7. Is \(a_n=f(n)\) for a positive decreasing \(f\) you can integrate? Integral test, and the integral's value even gives you a remainder bound.
