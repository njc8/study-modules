Power series and Taylor series

A polynomial that never stops. Written correctly it does not just approximate \(e^x\), \(\sin x\), \(\ln(1+x)\) or \(\arctan x\), it equals them, and an error bound tells you exactly how many terms you need.

How to use this

Work through the chapters in order. Each one teaches an idea, gives you something to slide or play, then drills you with fresh random problems until you hit the goal. Progress is saved in this browser.

Answer formats here: a general term is a formula in \(n\) and \(x\), typed like (-1)^n x^(2n+1)/(2n+1)! or x^n/3^(n+1). An interval of convergence is typed like [-1, 1), (-inf, inf), or {2} for a single point. A Taylor polynomial is typed like 1 - x^2/2 + x^4/24. Exact numbers are expected unless the problem asks for decimals: pi/4, ln(3/2), sqrt(3)/2, e^3, 1/384. Press Enter to check, and Enter again for a new question.

The answer boxes work like WebAssign's calcPad: / makes a fraction, ^ an exponent, n! a factorial, sqrt a radical, pi the symbol. Use the right arrow key to step out of a fraction or an exponent before typing what comes next.

What you will be able to do at the end

  • Find the radius and the interval of convergence of a power series, endpoints tested separately.
  • Build new power series out of the geometric series by substitution, multiplication, differentiation, and integration.
  • Write a Taylor or Maclaurin series from the definition \(c_n=f^{(n)}(a)/n!\), and much faster from the table of seven.
  • Write Taylor polynomials \(T_n\) and bound the error with Taylor's inequality or the alternating series estimate.
  • Use series to evaluate limits, to integrate functions with no elementary antiderivative, and to recognize a number hiding inside a series.

Chapter 1 · Stewart 11.8

Power series and where they converge

A power series is a series whose terms contain a variable. For some \(x\) it converges, for others it blows up, and the ratio test draws the line between them.

A power series centered at \(a\) is

\(\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n (x-a)^n = c_0 + c_1(x-a) + c_2(x-a)^2 + \cdots\)The \(c_n\) are constants, the coefficients. The number \(a\) is the center. Plug in a number for \(x\) and you have an ordinary series, which either converges or diverges.

At \(x=a\) every term after the first is zero, so the series always converges there. That is the only thing that is true for every power series. Beyond it there are exactly three possibilities:

  1. It converges only at \(x=a\). Radius \(R=0\). Example: \(\sum n!\,x^n\).
  2. It converges for every real \(x\). Radius \(R=\infty\). Example: \(\sum x^n/n!\).
  3. There is a number \(R>0\) with convergence for \(|x-a|<R\) and divergence for \(|x-a|>R\). At the two endpoints \(x=a\pm R\) anything can happen and you must check them one at a time.

The radius of convergence is that \(R\). The interval of convergence is the set of all \(x\) where the series converges: the open interval \((a-R,\,a+R)\) plus whichever endpoints survive their own test.

The ratio test gives the radius

Apply the ratio test to the terms \(a_n = c_n (x-a)^n\), treating \(x\) as a constant:

\(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right| = \lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{c_{n+1}}{c_n}\right|\,|x-a| = L\,|x-a| < 1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad |x-a| < \frac{1}{L}=R\)Solve the inequality for \(|x-a|\) and read the radius off the right-hand side. If \(L=0\) the inequality holds for every \(x\) and \(R=\infty\); if \(L=\infty\) it holds only at \(x=a\) and \(R=0\).

The ratio test says nothing when the limit equals 1, which is exactly what happens at the two endpoints. That is why endpoints always need a separate test: alternating series test, \(p\)-series, comparison, or the test for divergence.

Step-by-step: radius and interval by the ratio test

The two endpoint lines are the whole game. Substitute the endpoint into the original series, simplify until you recognize a series from Module 10, then name the test that settles it.

Endpoints are two separate problems. \(\sum (x-3)^n/n\) converges at \(x=2\) (alternating harmonic) and diverges at \(x=4\) (harmonic), so the interval is \([2,4)\). Writing \((2,4)\) or \([2,4]\) throws away half the work. Test each end on its own and record the bracket you earned.

Chapter 2 · Stewart 11.9

Functions as power series

One series does most of the work. Everything in this chapter is the geometric series with something substituted into it, multiplied by something, differentiated, or integrated.

\(\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-x}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n = 1+x+x^2+x^3+\cdots,\qquad |x|<1\)The engine. Read it right to left: a geometric series with first term 1 and ratio \(x\) sums to \(1/(1-x)\). Read left to right: the function is that series on \((-1,1)\).

Substitution. Put anything you like in place of \(x\), then impose the condition \(|\text{that thing}|<1\):

\(\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+x^2}=\frac{1}{1-(-x^2)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-x^2)^n=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^n x^{2n},\qquad |x|<1\)\(|-x^2|<1\) means \(x^2<1\), that is \(|x|<1\), so \(R=1\).

A denominator that does not start with 1. Factor the constant out first, so the ratio is visible:

\(\displaystyle\frac{1}{a-x}=\frac{1}{a}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\frac{x}{a}}=\frac{1}{a}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right)^n=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^n}{a^{n+1}},\qquad |x|<a\)The ratio is \(x/a\), so the radius is \(a\), and the extra \(1/a\) out front is what turns \(a^n\) into \(a^{n+1}\).

Multiplying by a power of \(x\) shifts every exponent and changes nothing else: \(\dfrac{x^2}{1-x^3}=x^2\sum x^{3n}=\sum x^{3n+2}\), still with \(R=1\). If the denominator factors, do partial fractions first and expand each piece.

Differentiating and integrating term by term

Inside the interval of convergence a power series behaves like a polynomial. You may differentiate it and integrate it one term at a time, and the radius does not change (the endpoints can change, and often do).

\(\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-x)^2}=\frac{d}{dx}\frac{1}{1-x}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n x^{n-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+1)x^n\)Differentiating kills the constant term, so the sum starts at \(n=1\); re-indexing to start at \(n=0\) turns \(n x^{n-1}\) into \((n+1)x^n\). Both forms are the same series.
\(\displaystyle\ln(1+x)=\int\frac{dx}{1+x}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n x^{n+1}}{n+1}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}x^n}{n},\qquad -1<x\le 1\)Integrate \(\sum(-1)^nx^n\) term by term and fix the constant by plugging in \(x=0\): \(\ln 1 = 0\), so \(C=0\). The radius is still 1, but now the right endpoint \(x=1\) is included: that is the alternating harmonic series, and its sum is \(\ln 2\).
\(\displaystyle\arctan x=\int\frac{dx}{1+x^2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n x^{2n+1}}{2n+1},\qquad |x|\le 1\)Same move on \(1/(1+x^2)\). At \(x=1\) it gives \(1-\tfrac13+\tfrac15-\cdots=\tfrac{\pi}{4}\), one of the oldest formulas for \(\pi\) and a famously slow one.
Always find the constant. Term-by-term integration produces \(f(x)=C+\sum\cdots\). Plug in \(x=0\), where every term of the series vanishes, and \(C=f(0)\) falls out. For \(\ln(1+x)\) and \(\arctan x\) that constant is 0, which is why it is easy to forget it exists.

Every one of these has radius 1, and they behave completely differently at the ends. For \(\ln(1+x)\), slide \(x_0\) to 1 and push \(N\): the partial sums crawl toward \(\ln 2\) from both sides, alternating. At \(x_0=-1\) they fall off a cliff. Same radius, different endpoints.

What the series are for

Two payoffs, both on the final. First, integrals with no elementary antiderivative: expand, integrate term by term, and stop when the next term is smaller than the accuracy you want. Second, summing a numerical series: recognize it as a known series evaluated at a specific \(x\).

\(\displaystyle\int_0^{1/2}\frac{dx}{1+x^4}=\int_0^{1/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^nx^{4n}\,dx=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{(4n+1)2^{4n+1}}=\tfrac12-\tfrac{1}{160}+\tfrac{1}{2304}-\cdots\)The result alternates with decreasing terms, so the alternating series estimate applies: the error from stopping is smaller than the first term you dropped.

And in the other direction, \(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{n2^n}\) is the series \(-\ln(1-x)=\sum x^n/n\) evaluated at \(x=\tfrac12\), so it equals \(-\ln\tfrac12=\ln 2\). Recognizing the pattern is the entire technique.

Chapter 3 · Stewart 11.10

Taylor and Maclaurin series

Chapter 2 went from a series to a function. This chapter goes the other way: given a function, the coefficients are forced, and there is only one power series it can have.

Suppose \(f(x)=\sum c_n(x-a)^n\) on some interval around \(a\). Differentiate \(k\) times and set \(x=a\): every term dies except one, and it leaves \(f^{(k)}(a)=k!\,c_k\). So the coefficients are not a choice.

\(\displaystyle c_n=\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^n\)The Taylor series of \(f\) at \(a\). When \(a=0\) it is called the Maclaurin series. The partial sum through degree \(n\) is the Taylor polynomial \(T_n(x)\).
\(\displaystyle T_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k = f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)+\frac{f''(a)}{2}(x-a)^2+\cdots+\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^n\)\(T_1\) is the tangent line. \(T_2\) adds the curvature. Each new term matches one more derivative at the center, which is why the fit is so good near \(a\) and can be so bad far away.

The table you should know cold

\(\dfrac{1}{1-x}=\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n\)\(R=1\)
\(e^x=\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^n}{n!}\)\(R=\infty\)
\(\sin x=\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n x^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}\)\(R=\infty\)
\(\cos x=\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n x^{2n}}{(2n)!}\)\(R=\infty\)
\(\arctan x=\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n x^{2n+1}}{2n+1}\)\(R=1\)
\(\ln(1+x)=\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}x^{n}}{n}\)\(R=1\)
\((1+x)^k=\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\binom{k}{n}x^n\)\(R=1\)

Notice that \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) are each other's derivative series: differentiate \(\cos x=\sum(-1)^nx^{2n}/(2n)!\) term by term and you get \(-\sin x\). Notice also that \(\sin\) has only odd powers and \(\cos\) only even ones, which is the series saying that one is odd and the other is even.

Almost every Maclaurin series you are asked for is one of these seven with a substitution and a multiplication:

\(\displaystyle e^{-x^2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-x^2)^n}{n!}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^nx^{2n}}{n!}\qquad x\cos x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^nx^{2n+1}}{(2n)!}\qquad \sin(x^2)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^nx^{4n+2}}{(2n+1)!}\)Substitute into the table entry, then push the outside factor into the exponent. Never differentiate \(e^{-x^2}\) nine times to get its ninth coefficient.

The binomial series

For any real \(k\), with the binomial coefficients \(\displaystyle\binom{k}{n}=\frac{k(k-1)(k-2)\cdots(k-n+1)}{n!}\) and \(\binom{k}{0}=1\):

\((1+x)^k=1+kx+\dfrac{k(k-1)}{2!}x^2+\dfrac{k(k-1)(k-2)}{3!}x^3+\cdots,\qquad |x|<1\)The numerator has exactly \(n\) factors, each one less than the last, starting at \(k\). When \(k\) is a positive integer the factors eventually hit zero and the series stops: that is the ordinary binomial theorem.

The two you will actually use are \(\sqrt{1+x}=1+\tfrac12x-\tfrac18x^2+\tfrac{1}{16}x^3-\cdots\) and \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+x}}=1-\tfrac12x+\tfrac38x^2-\tfrac{5}{16}x^3+\cdots\)

Centers other than zero

When the problem says "at \(a=1\)", the answer must be written in powers of \((x-1)\). Two ways: compute \(f^{(n)}(1)/n!\) from the definition, or rewrite \(f\) so the table applies. For \(e^x\) at \(a=1\), write \(e^x=e\cdot e^{x-1}\) and use the table on \(e^{x-1}\):

\(\displaystyle e^x=e\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(x-1)^n}{n!}\qquad\qquad \frac1x=\frac{1}{1+(x-1)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^n(x-1)^n\)A Taylor series at \(a\) is not allowed to contain a bare \(x\) anywhere. Every \(x\) lives inside \((x-a)\).

Explore: Taylor polynomials closing in

Blue: \(f\). Orange: \(T_N\) centered at the purple point. The dashed line marks \(x_0\).

Press Play on \(e^x\) and watch the polynomial swallow the whole window. Then switch to \(\ln(1+x)\) or \(1/(1-x)\) and play again: however high the degree goes, the polynomials never get past \(x=1\), because the radius of convergence is 1 and there is a genuine wall there. Now drag the center \(a\) to the right and watch the good region move with it.

The series can converge and still not be \(f\). Taylor's theorem does not say \(f\) equals its series; it says \(f=T_n+R_n\). You get equality only where the remainder \(R_n(x)\to 0\), which is the subject of the next chapter. For every function in this course it does go to zero inside the radius, but the logic matters: convergence of the series is not the same statement as convergence to \(f\).

Chapter 4 · Stewart 11.10 to 11.11

Error bounds and applications

An approximation with no error bound is a guess. This chapter is how you know that \(\int_0^1 e^{-x^2}dx=0.746824\ldots\) is right to six places.

Define the remainder \(R_n(x)=f(x)-T_n(x)\). It is the exact error made by stopping at degree \(n\). Taylor's inequality bounds it:

\(\displaystyle |R_n(x)|\le \frac{M}{(n+1)!}\,|x-a|^{n+1}\qquad\text{whenever } |f^{(n+1)}(t)|\le M \text{ for all } t \text{ between } a \text{ and } x\)Three ingredients: a bound \(M\) on the next derivative over the whole interval, the distance \(|x-a|\) raised to the next power, and the factorial that eventually crushes everything.

Finding \(M\) is the only creative step. For \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) every derivative is \(\pm\sin\) or \(\pm\cos\), so \(M=1\) always works. For \(e^x\) on \([0,b]\) the derivatives are all \(e^x\), increasing, so \(M=e^b\) (and Stewart often writes \(e<3\) to keep the arithmetic clean).

\(\displaystyle T_3 \text{ for } \sin x \text{ on } |x|\le 0.5:\quad |R_3(x)|\le\frac{1\cdot(0.5)^4}{4!}=\frac{0.0625}{24}\approx 0.0026\)Three decimal places from two nonzero terms. The true error at \(x=0.5\) is about \(0.00026\), ten times smaller: the bound is a guarantee, not a prediction.

When the series alternates, use the alternating series estimate instead. If the terms alternate and decrease in size to zero, the error from stopping is smaller than the first term you dropped. It is easier to compute than Taylor's inequality and usually much sharper.

Explore: the error and the bound that traps it

The orange curve stays inside the green envelope everywhere, usually by a wide margin near the center, and comes close only at the far end of the interval. Raise \(N\) by one and read the vertical scale: the bound drops by a factor of about \(c/(N+2)\) each time, which is why a few extra terms buy so many decimal places.

Three things series are for

Limits. Replace each function by its series and cancel. No L'Hospital, no algebra:

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin x-x}{x^3}=\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\left(x-\frac{x^3}{6}+\frac{x^5}{120}-\cdots\right)-x}{x^3}=\lim_{x\to 0}\left(-\frac16+\frac{x^2}{120}-\cdots\right)=-\frac16\)Keep terms up to the power in the denominator; everything above it goes to zero. The lowest surviving power gives you the answer immediately.

Integrals with no elementary antiderivative. The motivating question of this course:

\(\displaystyle\int_0^1 e^{-x^2}dx=\int_0^1\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^nx^{2n}}{n!}dx=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{n!(2n+1)}=1-\frac13+\frac{1}{10}-\frac{1}{42}+\frac{1}{216}-\cdots\)Alternating with decreasing terms, so the alternating estimate applies: stopping after \(1/216\) guarantees an error below the next term, \(1/1320\approx 0.00076\). Carry it to \(n=9\) and the guarantee is under \(10^{-6}\), giving \(0.746824\).

Physics one-liners. Einstein's kinetic energy \(K=mc^2\big[(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}-1\big]\) has binomial series \(K=\tfrac12mv^2+\tfrac38 mv^4/c^2+\cdots\), so Newton's \(\tfrac12mv^2\) is simply the first term of the relativistic one, and at everyday speeds the rest is invisible.

Which bound do I use? If the series at your \(x\) alternates with terms decreasing to zero, use the alternating estimate: error is less than the first omitted term. If it does not alternate (\(e^x\) at \(x>0\), for instance), use Taylor's inequality with an honest \(M\).
