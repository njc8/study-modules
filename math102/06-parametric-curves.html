Parametric curves

A curve as a motion: time goes in, a position comes out. Loops, self-crossings, and vertical tangents stop being a problem, because nothing has to be a function of \(x\) any more.

How to use this

Work through the chapters in order. Each one teaches an idea, gives you something to slide, play, and watch, then drills you with fresh random problems until you hit the goal. Progress is saved in this browser.

Answer formats: a parametrization is typed as <x(t), y(t)>, for example <3cos(t), 3sin(t)>. A point is (a, b), a list of points is (1,2), (3,-1), and DNE means there are none. Cartesian equations are typed as equations: y = x^2 - 1 or x^2/4 + y^2/9 = 1. Numbers are exact unless the problem says otherwise: 24/5, 2(2sqrt(2)-1), pi/3, 6pi.

Answer boxes work like WebAssign's calcPad: sqrt makes a radical, / a fraction, ^ an exponent, pi the symbol, < and > the vector brackets. Use the right arrow key to step out of a radical, fraction, or exponent, and press Enter to check, then Enter again for a new question.

What you will be able to do at the end

  • Sketch a parametric curve with its orientation, and eliminate the parameter to get a Cartesian equation.

  • Compute \(dy/dx\) and \(d^2y/dx^2\), find horizontal and vertical tangents, and say where the curve is concave up.
  • Compute the area between a parametric curve and an axis, the area enclosed by a closed curve, arc length, and surface area of revolution.

Chapter 1 · Stewart 10.1

Curves that are not graphs

Give \(x\) and \(y\) each their own formula in a third variable and you can draw anything: circles, loops, spirals, the track of a point on a rolling wheel.

A parametric curve is a pair of equations

\(x=f(t),\qquad y=g(t),\qquad t\in[\alpha,\beta]\)As \(t\) runs through the interval, the point \((f(t),g(t))\) traces the curve. Think of \(t\) as time and the point as a particle: the pair of formulas is a motion, and the curve is its track.

Nothing forces \(y\) to be a function of \(x\). The curve may loop, cross itself, or double back, and it may be traced more than once. That freedom is the whole point.

A parametrization carries more information than the curve does. It also carries the orientation (which way the point moves), the speed, the starting point, and how much of the curve is covered. Two different parametrizations can trace exactly the same picture in completely different ways, and WebAssign's "as described" problems test precisely that difference.

The faint line is the whole curve. The purple part is what has been traced so far, the orange dot is the particle, the blue arrow is the velocity \(\langle x'(t),y'(t)\rangle\).

Play the cycloid and watch the velocity arrow: it shrinks to nothing at each cusp, because the point of the wheel touching the ground is momentarily at rest. On the loop \(x=t^3-3t,\ y=t^2\), stop at the crossing point and notice that the particle passes through it at two different times, with two different velocity arrows.

Eliminating the parameter

To get a Cartesian equation, remove \(t\). There are two moves and you pick whichever is easier.

Solve and substitute. If one equation can be solved for \(t\), do that and substitute into the other. From \(x=t+3\) you get \(t=x-3\), so \(y=t^2\) becomes \(y=(x-3)^2\).

Use an identity. When \(t\) sits inside trig or exponential functions, combine the two equations so that a Pythagorean identity kills the parameter:

\(\cos^2t+\sin^2t=1,\qquad \sec^2t-\tan^2t=1,\qquad \cosh^2t-\sinh^2t=1,\qquad e^t\cdot e^{-t}=1\)So \(x=a\cos t,\ y=b\sin t\) gives \(\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}=1\), an ellipse, and \(x=a\sec t,\ y=b\tan t\) gives \(\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}-\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}=1\), a hyperbola.
The Cartesian equation usually says too much. For \(x=t^2,\ y=t^4\) the equation is \(y=x^2\), but the curve is only the half with \(x\ge 0\). For \(x=\sqrt t\) only \(x\ge0\) occurs, and for \(x=\sin t\) only \(-1\le x\le 1\). Always state the piece of the graph the parametrization actually traces.

The same path, traced differently

All four of these trace the unit circle, and no two of them are the same motion. This is the whole content of the "particle moving on a circle as described" problems.

Explore: four motions, one circle

Watch where each dot starts (the ring) and which way it goes. Reading a description backwards is the reliable method: the starting point tells you which of \(\cos\) and \(\sin\) goes where, the direction tells you the sign, and the number of trips tells you the coefficient in front of \(t\).

\(\text{radius }a\text{ about }(h,k):\quad \langle h+a\cos t,\ k+a\sin t\rangle \ \text{counterclockwise from } (h+a,k),\qquad \langle h+a\cos t,\ k-a\sin t\rangle\ \text{clockwise}\)To go around \(n\) times while \(t\) runs over \([0,2\pi]\), replace \(t\) by \(nt\). To start somewhere else, start the angle there: from the top \((h,k+a)\) counterclockwise is \(\langle h-a\sin t,\ k+a\cos t\rangle\).

Line segments

The straight path from \(P\) to \(Q\) that takes one unit of time is a weighted average of the endpoints:

\(\langle x,y\rangle = P + t\,(Q-P),\qquad 0\le t\le 1\)At \(t=0\) you are at \(P\), at \(t=1\) at \(Q\). In coordinates, \(x=p_1+(q_1-p_1)t\) and \(y=p_2+(q_2-p_2)t\). Because the interval is fixed at \([0,1]\), the answer is unique.

The cycloid

Mark a point on the rim of a wheel of radius \(r\) and roll the wheel along the \(x\)-axis. The marked point traces a cycloid:

\(x=r(\theta-\sin\theta),\qquad y=r(1-\cos\theta)\)\(\theta\) is the angle the wheel has turned. The \(r\theta\) is how far the center has moved, the \(-r\sin\theta,\ -r\cos\theta\) is where the point sits on the rim. One arch takes \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\). This curve has no reasonable Cartesian equation, which is exactly why parametric equations exist.
Two particles meet only if they are there at the same time. Paths crossing is a statement about two sets of points. A collision is a statement about one value of \(t\) that works in all four coordinate equations at once. Solve \(x_1(t)=x_2(t)\) and \(y_1(t)=y_2(t)\) as a system in the single unknown \(t\).

Chapter 2 · Stewart 10.2

Tangents and concavity

The slope of the curve is a ratio of two ordinary derivatives. Take that ratio twice, carefully, and you get concavity.

The chain rule says \(\dfrac{dy}{dt}=\dfrac{dy}{dx}\cdot\dfrac{dx}{dt}\). Divide, and the parameter drops out of the slope:

\(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}\qquad\text{whenever }\dfrac{dx}{dt}\neq 0\)This is the slope of the curve at the point, not the slope of anything in \(t\). Both derivatives are functions of \(t\), so the slope comes out as a function of \(t\) as well.
  • Horizontal tangent where \(\dfrac{dy}{dt}=0\) and \(\dfrac{dx}{dt}\neq0\): the slope is 0.
  • Vertical tangent where \(\dfrac{dx}{dt}=0\) and \(\dfrac{dy}{dt}\neq0\): the slope is undefined.
  • If both are 0, look closer. The curve usually has a cusp or a corner there, and you need the limit of \(dy/dx\) to see what happens (the cycloid at \(\theta=0\) is the standard example).

The tangent line at the point with parameter \(t_0\) is then the ordinary point-slope line through \(\big(x(t_0),y(t_0)\big)\):

\(y-y(t_0)=m\,\big(x-x(t_0)\big),\qquad m=\dfrac{y'(t_0)}{x'(t_0)}\)Find the point first, then the slope, then write the line. A curve that crosses itself has two tangent lines at the crossing point, one for each \(t\) that lands there.

The second derivative

\(d^2y/dx^2\) is the derivative of \(dy/dx\) with respect to \(x\). So apply the same rule again, this time to the function \(dy/dx\):

\(\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}=\dfrac{\dfrac{d}{dt}\!\left(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right)}{\dfrac{dx}{dt}}\)Differentiate the slope with respect to \(t\), then divide by \(dx/dt\) one more time. The curve is concave up where this is positive and concave down where it is negative.
It is not \(\dfrac{d^2y/dt^2}{d^2x/dt^2}\). That expression is wrong, and it is the most common mistake in Stewart 10.2. The first derivative is a ratio of first derivatives, but the second derivative is not a ratio of second derivatives: differentiate the quotient \(dy/dx\) with the quotient rule, then divide by \(dx/dt\).

Slide through a vertical tangent and watch \(dy/dx\) blow up and change sign. On the loop, park the point at the self-crossing, note the slope, then find the other \(t\) that reaches the same point and note the other slope. Step-by-step: from a parametrization to \(d^2y/dx^2\)

Check yourself at the third step: \(dy/dx\) must be a function of \(t\) alone, and it is the thing you differentiate next. Dividing by \(dx/dt\) at the end is the step everyone forgets.

Reading the sign. Once you have \(d^2y/dx^2\) as a single fraction in \(t\), concavity is a sign question, not a calculation. Factor the numerator and the denominator, mark the values of \(t\) where each is zero, and read off the intervals.

Chapter 3 · Stewart 10.2

Areas, arc length, and surface area

Every formula in this chapter is a first-year formula with \(dx\), \(dy\), and \(ds\) rewritten in terms of \(t\).

The area under a curve is \(\int y\,dx\). Substituting \(y=g(t)\) and \(dx=f'(t)\,dt\) turns it into an ordinary integral in \(t\):

\(A=\int_{a}^{b} y\,dx=\int_{\alpha}^{\beta} g(t)\,f'(t)\,dt\)The limits are the \(t\)-values that produce the \(x\)-limits. If the curve is traced left to right, take \(\alpha\) to \(\beta\) in that order and the answer is positive; traced right to left the integral comes out negative, and you take the absolute value.

The same substitution with the roles swapped gives the area between the curve and the \(y\)-axis:

\(A=\int_{c}^{d} x\,dy=\int_{\alpha}^{\beta} f(t)\,g'(t)\,dt\)Use this one when the region is bounded by the \(y\)-axis instead: the strips are horizontal, of length \(x\) and thickness \(dy\).

For a closed curve traced once, either formula gives the enclosed area up to sign. The ellipse \(x=a\cos t,\ y=b\sin t\) gives \(\left|\int_0^{2\pi} (b\sin t)(-a\sin t)\,dt\right|=\pi ab\), the formula you already know, and the astroid \(x=a\cos^3t,\ y=a\sin^3t\) gives \(\tfrac38\pi a^2\).

\(L=\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{dx}{dt}\right)^{2}+\left(\dfrac{dy}{dt}\right)^{2}}\;dt\)The integrand is the speed. Distance is speed times time, so arc length is the integral of speed. Simplify what is under the radical before integrating: these problems are built so that it collapses to something like \(t+1\) or \(6t\sqrt{1+t^2}\).
Traced once. The arc length formula measures distance travelled, not the length of the picture. If the parametrization goes around twice, the integral is twice the length of the curve. Check the interval before you integrate.

Rotating the curve gives a surface, and the band at parameter \(t\) has circumference \(2\pi\times(\text{distance to the axis})\) and width \(ds\):

\(S=\int 2\pi y\,ds\ \ (\text{about the }x\text{-axis}),\qquad S=\int 2\pi x\,ds\ \ (\text{about the }y\text{-axis}),\qquad ds=\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{dx}{dt}\right)^{2}+\left(\dfrac{dy}{dt}\right)^{2}}\,dt\)The same \(ds\) as arc length, with one radius factor in front. Rotating a half circle of radius \(a\) about the \(x\)-axis gives \(4\pi a^{2}\), the surface of a sphere.

Explore: polygons approximate length, strips approximate area

Length mode: the inscribed polygon with \(n\) sides. Area mode: the region swept between the curve and the \(x\)-axis up to the current \(t\).

In length mode, push \(n\) up and watch the polygon length climb toward the exact value from below: a straight chord is always shorter than the arc it cuts. In area mode, sweep \(t\) across the astroid quarter and watch the running integral come out negative, because that piece is traced right to left.

Step-by-step: area between a parametric curve and the \(x\)-axis

The first step is always the same: find the \(t\)-values where the curve meets the axis. Those are the limits of integration, and forgetting to convert the \(x\)-limits into \(t\)-limits is the mistake that makes these problems go wrong.

Do not integrate blind. Every one of these integrals is built so that the algebra collapses. If you are looking at \(\sqrt{9t^4+4t^2}\), pull \(t^2\) out of the radical before you do anything else and it becomes \(t\sqrt{9t^2+4}\), which is a routine substitution.
