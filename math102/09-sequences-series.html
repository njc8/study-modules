Sequences and series

An infinite list either settles down or it does not. An infinite sum is nothing more exotic than the limit of its partial sums. This module gives you three families you can add up exactly, one test that catches most divergence, and one bridge back to integrals.

Chapter 1 · Stewart 11.1

Sequences

A sequence is a list of numbers with an address for each one. The only question that matters is where the list is heading.

A sequence \(\{a_n\}\) assigns a number \(a_n\) to each integer \(n=1,2,3,\dots\). You can give it by a formula (\(a_n=n/(n+1)\)), by a list (\(\{1,\tfrac12,\tfrac13,\dots\}\)), or by a recursion (\(a_1=1\), \(a_{n+1}=\sqrt{2+a_n}\)).

\(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=L\)Read it as: eventually the terms stay as close to \(L\) as you like. Precisely, for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there is an \(N\) with \(|a_n-L|<\varepsilon\) for all \(n>N\). If such an \(L\) exists the sequence converges; otherwise it diverges.

You already know how to find these limits. A sequence is a function sampled at the integers, so every limit rule for \(x\to\infty\) carries over.

  • Divide by the highest power. \(\dfrac{3n+5}{7n-2}=\dfrac{3+5/n}{7-2/n}\to\dfrac37\).
  • L'Hospital on the function. If \(f(x)\to L\) and \(a_n=f(n)\), then \(a_n\to L\). That is how you get \(\dfrac{\ln n}{n}\to 0\): differentiate \(\ln x\) and \(x\).
  • Squeeze. If \(b_n\le a_n\le c_n\) and both outer sequences go to \(L\), so does \(a_n\). And \(|a_n|\to 0\) forces \(a_n\to 0\), which is how \(\dfrac{\sin n}{n}\to 0\).
  • Continuous functions pass through. If \(a_n\to L\) and \(f\) is continuous at \(L\), then \(f(a_n)\to f(L)\).
\(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} r^{\,n}=\begin{cases}0 & -1<r<1\\ 1 & r=1\\ \text{does not exist} & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}\)The geometric sequence. It converges exactly when \(-1<r\le 1\). At \(r=-1\) it flips between \(1\) and \(-1\) forever; for \(|r|>1\) it blows up.

Explore: the dot plot and the \(\varepsilon\) band

Each dot is one term \(a_n\), plotted against its index \(n\). The dashed line is the limit \(L\); the shaded strip is the band \(|a_n-L|<\varepsilon\).

Shrink \(\varepsilon\) and watch \(N\) grow. Convergence means every band eventually captures all the terms, no matter how thin you make it. Now pick \((-1)^n\): the band around any candidate \(L\) always leaves half the terms outside, so no \(L\) works. Then push the ratio slider past \(1\) and past \(-1\) and see the two ways a geometric sequence can fail.

Monotone and bounded

A sequence is increasing if \(a_n<a_{n+1}\) for all \(n\), decreasing if \(a_n>a_{n+1}\) for all \(n\), and monotonic if it is one or the other. It is bounded above if some \(M\) has \(a_n\le M\) for all \(n\), bounded below if some \(m\) has \(m\le a_n\), and bounded if both.

Monotonic + bounded \(\Longrightarrow\) convergentThe Monotonic Sequence Theorem. An increasing sequence that never passes a ceiling has to pile up against a least ceiling, and that number is the limit. This is the one convergence result you can use without knowing the limit.

To test monotonicity, compare \(a_{n+1}\) with \(a_n\) (subtract, or divide when everything is positive), or differentiate the matching function \(f(x)\) and look at the sign of \(f'\).

Where the bounds come from. A monotone sequence is trapped between its first term and its limit. For \(a_n=\dfrac{n}{n+1}\), the terms climb from \(a_1=\tfrac12\) up toward \(1\), so \(\tfrac12\le a_n<1\) and \(1\) is the least upper bound. That is the pair of numbers the practice below asks for.

Recursively defined sequences

When \(a_{n+1}=g(a_n)\) and you already know the sequence converges, the limit is easy: both \(a_n\) and \(a_{n+1}\) go to the same \(L\), so \(L=g(L)\). Solve that equation and pick the root the sequence can actually reach.

\(a_{n+1}=\sqrt{2+a_n}\;\Longrightarrow\; L=\sqrt{2+L}\;\Longrightarrow\; L^2-L-2=0\;\Longrightarrow\; L=2\)The other root \(L=-1\) is thrown out: every term after the first is a square root, so \(L\ge 0\).
Solve for the limit only after you know it exists. Applying \(L=g(L)\) to a divergent recursion produces a number that means nothing. For \(a_{n+1}=2a_n\) with \(a_1=1\) the equation \(L=2L\) gives \(L=0\), and the sequence marches off to infinity.

The growth hierarchy

For large \(n\), each of these grows faster than everything to its left:

\(\ln n \;\ll\; n^{p}\;(p>0) \;\ll\; b^{\,n}\;(b>1) \;\ll\; n! \;\ll\; n^{n}\)Read a ratio right to left: the faster one wins. So \(\dfrac{2^n}{n!}\to 0\), \(\dfrac{n!}{2^n}\to\infty\), \(\dfrac{\ln n}{n}\to 0\), and \(\dfrac{n^{10}}{1.01^{\,n}}\to 0\) even though it takes a very long time.

Series and partial sums

You cannot add infinitely many numbers. You add finitely many and take a limit, and that limit is what the infinite sum means.

Given a sequence \(\{a_n\}\), build its partial sums \(s_n=a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n\). The partial sums form a new sequence. The series is the limit of that sequence:

\(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n=\lim_{n\to\infty}s_n,\qquad s_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k\)If the limit exists and equals \(s\), the series converges and its sum is \(s\). If not, the series diverges. Everything in this chapter and the next is a way of deciding which.

Geometric series

A geometric series has a constant ratio between consecutive terms. Write the first term as \(a\) and the ratio as \(r\):

\(a+ar+ar^2+\cdots=\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}ar^{\,n-1}\)

Here is the trick that gives the closed form. Write \(s_n\), multiply by \(r\), and subtract. Everything in the middle cancels:

\(s_n-rs_n=a-ar^{\,n}\;\Longrightarrow\; s_n=\dfrac{a(1-r^{\,n})}{1-r}\quad (r\ne 1)\)Now let \(n\to\infty\). If \(|r|<1\) then \(r^{\,n}\to 0\) and the whole thing settles.
\(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}ar^{\,n-1}=\frac{a}{1-r}\quad\text{for }|r|<1,\qquad\text{diverges for }|r|\ge 1\)The single most useful formula in the chapter. \(a\) is whatever the first term actually is, not the coefficient you happen to see in front.

Step-by-step: summing a geometric series

Step 1 is the only place you can go wrong. Plug \(n\) equal to the starting index into the general term and simplify: that number, and nothing else, is \(a\).

The starting index changes \(a\), never \(r\). \(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}3\left(\tfrac12\right)^{n}\) has \(a=3\) and sums to \(6\). \(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}3\left(\tfrac12\right)^{n}\) has \(a=\tfrac32\) and sums to \(3\). \(\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}3\left(\tfrac12\right)^{n}\) has \(a=\tfrac34\) and sums to \(\tfrac32\). Same ratio every time.

Mixed powers hide a geometric series. Force every exponent to be plain \(n\): \(\dfrac{3^{\,n+1}}{4^{\,n-1}}=\dfrac{3\cdot 3^{\,n}}{4^{\,n}/4}=12\left(\dfrac34\right)^{n}\), so \(r=\tfrac34\) and the first term (at \(n=1\)) is \(9\).

Repeating decimals

Every repeating decimal is a geometric series in disguise, which is why every one of them is a fraction:

\(0.\overline{27}=\dfrac{27}{100}+\dfrac{27}{100^2}+\cdots=\dfrac{27/100}{1-1/100}=\dfrac{27}{99}=\dfrac{3}{11}\)The ratio is \(1/10^{d}\) where \(d\) is the number of repeating digits. Any non-repeating head is added on separately.

Telescoping series

The other family you can sum exactly is the one where the partial sum collapses. Split the term with partial fractions so that consecutive pieces cancel:

\(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n(n+1)}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac1n-\frac1{n+1}\right),\qquad s_n=1-\frac{1}{n+1}\;\longrightarrow\; 1\)Write out four or five terms and cross off pairs. What survives is a fixed head and a tail that goes to zero. The head is the sum.

Always find \(s_n\) first, then take its limit. That is the definition, and for telescoping series it is also the fastest route.

The harmonic series

\(\sum 1/n\) diverges, even though its terms go to zero. Group the terms in blocks of \(1,1,2,4,8,\dots\):

\(1+\tfrac12+\underbrace{\left(\tfrac13+\tfrac14\right)}_{>\,1/2}+\underbrace{\left(\tfrac15+\cdots+\tfrac18\right)}_{>\,1/2}+\cdots\)Each block beats \(\tfrac12\), and there are infinitely many blocks, so the partial sums pass every bound. The growth is like \(\ln n\), which is slow but unstoppable.

The test for divergence

If \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n\ne 0\) or does not exist, then \(\displaystyle\sum a_n\) diverges.Contrapositive of "convergent \(\Rightarrow a_n\to 0\)". It is the first thing to check on any series, and it costs one limit.
The converse is false. \(a_n\to 0\) proves nothing. The harmonic series is the standing counterexample. When the limit is \(0\), the test is inconclusive and you move on to another test.

Properties

If \(\sum a_n\) and \(\sum b_n\) both converge, then so do \(\sum ca_n\) and \(\sum (a_n\pm b_n)\), with \(\sum ca_n=c\sum a_n\) and \(\sum(a_n\pm b_n)=\sum a_n\pm\sum b_n\). That is what lets you split \(\sum\frac{2^n+3^n}{6^n}\) into two geometric series. Also, changing or dropping finitely many terms never changes whether a series converges, though it does change the sum.

The integral test and \(p\)-series

A sum of rectangle areas next to the area under a curve. If one is finite so is the other, and the gap between them tells you how much of the sum you are still missing.

Suppose \(a_n=f(n)\) where \(f\) is positive, continuous, and decreasing on \([m,\infty)\). Draw rectangles of width \(1\) over \([1,2],[2,3],\dots\). Using the left endpoint for the height gives rectangles that sit above the curve; using the right endpoint gives rectangles under it. Comparing the two pictures with the integral gives the test.

\(\displaystyle\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}a_n \text{ converges} \iff \int_m^{\infty} f(x)\,dx \text{ converges}\)The integral test. The integral and the series are not equal, and the test never tells you the sum. It only tells you finite or infinite, and both answers come together.

Explore: rectangles against the curve

Blue curve \(y=1/x^{p}\). Orange rectangles use the left endpoint (heights \(a_1,\dots,a_n\), total \(s_n\)); green rectangles use the right endpoint (heights \(a_2,\dots,a_{n+1}\)).

Set \(p=2\) and push \(n\): the orange total and the integral both level off, and the sum is pinned between two finite numbers. Now set \(p=1\) and push \(n\) again: the integral is \(\ln n\), which never levels off, and it drags the sum up with it. Somewhere between the two, at \(p=1\), the behaviour switches.

\(\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{p}}\ \text{converges}\iff p>1\)The \(p\)-series test, and it is just the integral test applied to \(\int_1^\infty x^{-p}dx\). That integral is \(\left[\frac{x^{1-p}}{1-p}\right]_1^\infty\), finite exactly when \(1-p<0\). At \(p=1\) the antiderivative is \(\ln x\), which diverges.

So \(\sum 1/n^2\) converges, \(\sum 1/\sqrt n\) diverges, and \(\sum 1/n\) is the borderline case that diverges. The first job on any \(p\)-series is to rewrite it as a single power: \(\dfrac{1}{n\sqrt n}=n^{-3/2}\), so \(p=\tfrac32\).

How much is left over

Once you know a series converges, the same rectangles bound the remainder \(R_n=s-s_n=a_{n+1}+a_{n+2}+\cdots\), the part you throw away when you stop after \(n\) terms:

\(\displaystyle\int_{n+1}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx\;\le\; R_n\;\le\;\int_{n}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx\)The remainder is a sum of rectangles of width \(1\) starting at \(x=n\). Overshoot them with the integral from \(n\), undershoot them with the integral from \(n+1\).

The right-hand bound answers "how accurate am I?" and, solved for \(n\), answers "how many terms do I need?". Adding the bounds to \(s_n\) sharpens the estimate of the sum itself:

\(\displaystyle s_n+\int_{n+1}^{\infty}f\;\le\; s\;\le\; s_n+\int_{n}^{\infty}f\)Two numbers you can actually compute, with the true sum trapped between them. The midpoint of the two is a far better estimate than \(s_n\) alone.
Check the hypotheses before you integrate. \(f\) must be positive, continuous, and decreasing. \(f(x)=\dfrac{\ln x}{x}\) increases until \(x=e\), so start the test at \(n=3\). Dropping the first two terms cannot change convergence, only the sum.
