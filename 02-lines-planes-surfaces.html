Lines in space

A line needs one point to pin it down and one vector to tell it which way to go. Everything else is bookkeeping.

In the plane you describe a line with a slope. In space that fails: there is no single number that says "which way" in three dimensions. You need a direction vector \(\mathbf{v}\). Start at a known point \(P_0(x_0,y_0,z_0)\) with position vector \(\mathbf{r}_0\), then walk along \(\mathbf{v}\) for any amount of time \(t\):

\(\mathbf{r}(t)=\mathbf{r}_0+t\,\mathbf{v}\)The vector equation of a line. As \(t\) runs over all real numbers, the tip of \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) traces the whole line. Negative \(t\) walks backward.

Write \(\mathbf{v}=\langle a,b,c\rangle\) and read off one coordinate at a time. The numbers \(a,b,c\) are called the direction numbers of the line:

\(x=x_0+at,\qquad y=y_0+bt,\qquad z=z_0+ct\)Parametric equations. One equation per coordinate, all sharing the same parameter \(t\).

If no component of \(\mathbf{v}\) is zero, solve each equation for \(t\) and set the results equal. The parameter disappears:

\(\dfrac{x-x_0}{a}=\dfrac{y-y_0}{b}=\dfrac{z-z_0}{c}\)Symmetric equations. The denominators are the direction numbers. The numbers subtracted in the numerators are the coordinates of a point on the line.

If one direction number is zero, that coordinate never changes, so you cannot divide by it. Say \(b=0\). Then drop that fraction and carry the coordinate as its own equation:

\(\dfrac{x-x_0}{a}=\dfrac{z-z_0}{c},\qquad y=y_0\)A line with \(b=0\) lives inside the horizontal plane \(y=y_0\). Never write a zero in a denominator.

A line through two points

Given \(P\) and \(Q\), you already have a point. For the direction, use the vector that joins them: \(\mathbf{v}=\overrightarrow{PQ}=Q-P\). Either point works as the base, and \(\overrightarrow{QP}\) works just as well as \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\), so the same line has infinitely many correct parametrizations.

Why any answer that checks out is right. Changing the base point shifts which \(t\) hits which point. Scaling \(\mathbf{v}\) changes how fast you travel. Neither changes the set of points, and the set of points is the line. The drills accept any parametrization of the correct line.

Is a point on the line?

Substitute the coordinates and solve for \(t\) in every equation. The point is on the line only if you get the same \(t\) from all three. One disagreement is enough to rule it out. If a direction number is zero, that equation has no \(t\) in it, so instead check that the coordinate matches exactly.

The classic mistake. Getting \(t=2\) from the \(x\)-equation and stopping there. Two of the three equations agreeing is the usual near miss on an exam. Check all three, every time.

Line segments

The full line uses all real \(t\). Restrict the parameter and you get a piece of it. The segment from \(P\) to \(Q\) is the tidy version:

\(\mathbf{r}(t)=(1-t)\,\mathbf{r}_0+t\,\mathbf{r}_1,\qquad 0\le t\le 1\)At \(t=0\) you are at \(P\), at \(t=1\) you are at \(Q\), and in between you slide along at constant speed. \(t=\tfrac12\) is the midpoint.

It is the same line as \(\mathbf{r}_0+t(\mathbf{r}_1-\mathbf{r}_0)\), just regrouped. Expand it and you will see: \((1-t)\mathbf{r}_0+t\mathbf{r}_1=\mathbf{r}_0+t(\mathbf{r}_1-\mathbf{r}_0)\).

Planes

A plane is not described by a direction. It is described by the one direction it refuses: its normal vector.

Giving a direction vector would not pin a plane down, since a plane holds vectors pointing every which way inside it. Instead give a vector perpendicular to all of them. A plane is fixed by a point \(P_0\) on it and a normal vector \(\mathbf{n}=\langle a,b,c\rangle\) orthogonal to it. A point \(P\) lies on the plane exactly when the vector from \(P_0\) to \(P\) lies in the plane, which means it is orthogonal to \(\mathbf{n}\):

\(\mathbf{n}\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_0)=0\)The vector equation of a plane. It says: the displacement from the known point to the unknown point has no component along the normal.

Write out the dot product in components and you get the two forms you will actually use:

\(a(x-x_0)+b(y-y_0)+c(z-z_0)=0 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad ax+by+cz=d\)Scalar and linear form, where \(d=ax_0+by_0+cz_0=\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{r}_0\). Read the normal vector straight off the coefficients.
The one thing to remember. In \(ax+by+cz=d\), the coefficients are the normal vector: \(\mathbf{n}=\langle a,b,c\rangle\). Every plane question becomes "find a normal, find a point, assemble".

Three points determine a plane

Given \(P\), \(Q\), \(R\) (not all on one line), build two vectors that lie in the plane and cross them. The cross product is orthogonal to both, so it is orthogonal to the plane:

\(\mathbf{n}=\overrightarrow{PQ}\times\overrightarrow{PR}\)Then use any one of the three points with \(\mathbf{n}\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_0)=0\). Using \(Q\) or \(R\) instead of \(P\) gives the same equation.

Step-by-step: the plane through three points

Check your own normal the same way the last step does: dot it with \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) and with \(\overrightarrow{PR}\). Two zeros means your cross product is right.

Three more standard setups

  • Through a point, parallel to a given plane. Parallel planes share a normal. Copy the coefficients, then recompute \(d\) from the new point.
  • Containing a line and a point not on it. The line's direction \(\mathbf{v}\) lies in the plane, and so does the vector from a point of the line to the extra point. Cross those two for \(\mathbf{n}\).
  • Containing two intersecting or parallel lines. Same idea: get two non-parallel vectors that lie in the plane, then cross them.
  • Through two points, perpendicular to a given plane. The trick is to see that the given plane's normal \(\mathbf{n}_0\) is a direction inside the plane you want: perpendicular planes have perpendicular normals, and "the new normal is orthogonal to \(\mathbf{n}_0\)" is the same statement as "\(\mathbf{n}_0\) lies along the new plane". So cross \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) with \(\mathbf{n}_0\).
\(\mathbf{n}=\overrightarrow{PQ}\times\mathbf{n}_0\)Plane through \(P\) and \(Q\) perpendicular to the plane with normal \(\mathbf{n}_0\). Example: through \((1,0,2)\) and \((3,1,1)\), perpendicular to \(x-y+2z=4\). Here \(\overrightarrow{PQ}=\langle 2,1,-1\rangle\), \(\mathbf{n}_0=\langle 1,-1,2\rangle\), so \(\mathbf{n}=\langle 1,-5,-3\rangle\) and the plane is \(x-5y-3z=-5\). Check: both points satisfy it, and \(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{n}_0=1+5-6=0\).
Two points are not enough on their own. Infinitely many planes contain a given pair of points (they pivot around the line \(PQ\)). The perpendicularity condition is what picks one out, by handing you the second in-plane direction.

Two planes: parallel, perpendicular, or at an angle

Compare the normals, never the constants:

  • Parallel planes have parallel normals: \(\mathbf{n}_2=k\,\mathbf{n}_1\). If the constants also match after scaling, they are the same plane.
  • Perpendicular planes have orthogonal normals: \(\mathbf{n}_1\cdot\mathbf{n}_2=0\).
  • Otherwise the angle between the planes is the angle between the normals, taken acute:
\(\cos\theta=\dfrac{|\mathbf{n}_1\cdot\mathbf{n}_2|}{|\mathbf{n}_1|\,|\mathbf{n}_2|}\)The absolute value is what makes \(\theta\) acute. Without it, flipping a normal would change the answer to its supplement.
Do not test the constants. \(x+y+z=1\) and \(x+y+z=97\) are parallel, not unrelated. And \(2x-4y+6z=3\) is parallel to \(x-2y+3z=8\), because \(\langle 2,-4,6\rangle=2\langle 1,-2,3\rangle\). Scale before you judge.

Chapter 3 · Stewart 12.5

Intersections and distances

Now put lines and planes in the same room. Two objects either meet, or they miss, and if they miss you can measure by how much.

Where a line meets a plane

Before any algebra, notice that the line and the plane are two different kinds of object.

  • The line is a generator. Every point on it has the form \(\mathbf{r}_0+t\,\mathbf{v}\), where \(\mathbf{r}_0\) is a base point and \(\mathbf{v}=\langle v_1,v_2,v_3\rangle\) is the direction. The parameter \(t\) is a dial. Turn it and you slide along the line. The line produces points; it does not check them.
  • The plane is a test. A point \(\mathbf{r}\) is on the plane exactly when \(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{r}=d\), with \(\mathbf{n}=\langle a,b,c\rangle\) the normal. The plane does not produce points. It takes a point you hand it and answers yes or no.

Finding the intersection means feeding the generator into the test. Take the general point the line produces and ask the plane whether it passes. That turns three unknown coordinates into one unknown dial setting:

\(\mathbf{n}\cdot(\mathbf{r}_0+t\,\mathbf{v})=d\)Written out in coordinates: \(a(x_0+v_1t)+b(y_0+v_2t)+c(z_0+v_3t)=d\). The plane's normal is \(\langle a,b,c\rangle\) and the line's direction is \(\langle v_1,v_2,v_3\rangle\). They are different vectors and in general point different ways.

The dot product distributes, so the left side splits into a part that does not depend on \(t\) and a part that does. Collect and solve:

\(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{r}_0+t\,(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v})=d \;\Longrightarrow\; t=\dfrac{d-\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{r}_0}{\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v}}\)Both pieces have a meaning. The numerator \(d-\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{r}_0\) is how far the base point falls short of passing the plane test: it is zero exactly when \(\mathbf{r}_0\) is already on the plane. The denominator \(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v}\) is how much one unit step along the line changes the test value, since each step adds \(\mathbf{v}\) to the point and therefore adds \(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v}\) to \(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{r}\). So \(t\) is shortfall divided by rate: the number of steps needed to close the gap.

Solving for \(t\) gives a dial setting, not a point. The question asked for a point, so turn the dial to that value and read off where the generator lands: compute \(\mathbf{r}_0+t\,\mathbf{v}\). Stopping at \(t\) is the most common way to lose the marks on this problem.

When \(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v}=0\). Then the direction runs flat along the plane. Walking along the line never changes the test value, so there is no rate to divide by and no single crossing point. Two things can happen. If the base point passes the test, every point on the line passes it too, and the whole line lies in the plane. If the base point fails, every point fails by the same amount, and the line misses the plane entirely.

The special case where the line is perpendicular to the plane. That is when \(\mathbf{v}\) is parallel to \(\mathbf{n}\), so you can take \(\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{n}=\langle a,b,c\rangle\). The setup above still applies with \(v_1=a\), \(v_2=b\), \(v_3=c\), and the denominator becomes \(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{n}=a^2+b^2+c^2\). This is the line you walk to find the foot of the perpendicular from a point to a plane, and it is the only situation in which the line's direction and the plane's normal are the same vector.

Where two planes meet

Two non-parallel planes meet in a line. The line lies in both planes, so its direction is perpendicular to both normals, and there is exactly one vector that does that:

\(\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{n}_1\times\mathbf{n}_2\)The direction of the line of intersection. Now you only need one point on both planes.

To find a point, set one variable to a convenient value, usually \(z=0\), and solve the remaining \(2\times2\) system. If that system has no solution or is degenerate, the line never crosses \(z=0\), so set \(x=0\) instead and try again.

Two lines: parallel, intersecting, or skew

In space, two lines that do not meet need not be parallel. Lines that are neither parallel nor intersecting are called skew. They live on different levels, like a highway overpass. The test is a short procedure. Use a different letter for the second parameter, say \(s\), because the two lines need not reach the meeting point at the same time.

  1. Are the directions parallel, \(\mathbf{v}_2=k\mathbf{v}_1\)? If yes the lines are parallel (the same line if a point of one satisfies the other).
  2. If not, set the two parametrizations equal and solve two of the three coordinate equations for \(t\) and \(s\).
  3. Put that \(t\) and \(s\) into the third equation. If it holds, the lines intersect, and the intersection point comes from plugging \(t\) into line 1. If it fails, the lines are skew.

Distances

Every distance formula below is one idea in disguise: project the vector that joins the two objects onto the direction that measures the gap.

\(D=\dfrac{|ax_1+by_1+cz_1-d|}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}\)Point \((x_1,y_1,z_1)\) to the plane \(ax+by+cz=d\). Plug the point into the plane equation, subtract the constant, take the absolute value, divide by \(|\mathbf{n}|\). It is the scalar projection of \(\overrightarrow{P_0P_1}\) onto \(\mathbf{n}\).

Between two parallel planes: first rescale one equation so the two normals are identical, then pick any point on one plane and use the formula above. With matched normals it collapses to \(D=|d_1-d_2|/|\mathbf{n}|\).

\(D=\dfrac{|\overrightarrow{PQ}\times\mathbf{v}|}{|\mathbf{v}|}\)Point \(Q\) to the line through \(P\) with direction \(\mathbf{v}\). The cross product's length is the area of the parallelogram with sides \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\); dividing by the base \(|\mathbf{v}|\) leaves the height, which is the distance.
\(D=\dfrac{|\overrightarrow{P_1P_2}\cdot(\mathbf{v}_1\times\mathbf{v}_2)|}{|\mathbf{v}_1\times\mathbf{v}_2|}\)Between two skew lines through \(P_1\), \(P_2\) with directions \(\mathbf{v}_1\), \(\mathbf{v}_2\). Skew lines sit in two parallel planes whose shared normal is \(\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{v}_1\times\mathbf{v}_2\); the distance between the lines is the gap between those planes, which is the scalar projection of \(\overrightarrow{P_1P_2}\) onto \(\mathbf{n}\). For parallel lines this cross product is \(\mathbf{0}\), so use the point-to-line formula instead with a point of one line and the other line.
Which distance formula. Point to plane: divide by \(|\mathbf{n}|\). Point to line: cross with \(\mathbf{v}\), divide by \(|\mathbf{v}|\). Two parallel planes: match the normals, then the constants. Two skew lines: cross the directions to get \(\mathbf{n}\), then it is a point-to-plane problem. Two parallel lines: point-to-line. Every one of them is "project the connecting vector onto the direction that measures the gap".
Sanity checks that cost nothing. A distance is never negative, so if you forgot the absolute value you will see it. And the distance from a point to a plane is zero exactly when the point satisfies the plane equation, so a zero answer is a claim you can verify in one line.
Matching normals comes first. For the parallel planes \(2x-y+2z=4\) and \(4x-2y+4z=6\), the difference of the constants is not \(6-4\). Divide the second by 2 to get \(2x-y+2z=3\), so the gap is \(|4-3|/3=\tfrac13\).

Cylinders and quadric surfaces

Linear equations give planes. Let the variables be squared and you get the whole zoo: bowls, saddles, cones, and shells. You identify them by slicing.

Cylinders: a missing variable

A cylinder is a surface made of parallel lines (the rulings) passing through a fixed plane curve. The tell is simple: a variable is missing from the equation. If \(z\) never appears, then \(z\) is free, so whenever \((x,y,z)\) is on the surface so is \((x,y,z+7)\). The curve in the \(xy\)-plane is dragged straight along the \(z\)-axis.

  • \(x^2+y^2=9\): a circular cylinder of radius 3, rulings parallel to the \(z\)-axis.
  • \(z=x^2\): a parabolic cylinder, rulings parallel to the \(y\)-axis.
  • \(y^2-z^2=1\): a hyperbolic cylinder, rulings parallel to the \(x\)-axis.
Context decides. In two dimensions \(x^2+y^2=9\) is a circle. In three dimensions the same equation is a cylinder, because the absent \(z\) is unrestricted. Always ask how many variables the problem lives in.

Traces: the way to see a surface

A trace is the curve you get by intersecting the surface with a plane parallel to a coordinate plane. Set \(z=k\) and you get the horizontal trace at height \(k\); set \(x=k\) or \(y=k\) for the vertical traces. Stack the traces mentally and the surface appears. Only three questions matter for each family: what shape is it, how does it change as \(k\) grows, and for which \(k\) does it vanish.

The six quadric surfaces

A quadric surface is the graph of a second-degree equation in \(x,y,z\). After shifting and rotating, every one of them is in this list. Read the signs and count the squares:

Ellipsoid\(\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}+\dfrac{z^2}{c^2}=1\)ellipses; a point at \(|k|=c\); empty for \(|k|>c\)ellipses
Elliptic paraboloid\(\dfrac{z}{c}=\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}\)ellipses (one for each \(k\) on the open side, a point at the vertex, empty on the other side)parabolas
Hyperbolic paraboloid\(\dfrac{z}{c}=\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}-\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}\)hyperbolas; a pair of lines at \(k=0\)parabolas (opening opposite ways for \(x=k\) and \(y=k\))

Hyperboloid of two sheets\(-\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}-\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}+\dfrac{z^2}{c^2}=1\)empty for \(|k|\lt c\); a point at \(|k|=c\); ellipses beyondhyperbolas

Three signs and one right-hand side sort the whole table:

  • One variable appears to the first power (the others squared): a paraboloid. Squares with the same sign make it elliptic (a bowl), opposite signs make it hyperbolic (a saddle).
  • Three squares, all the same sign, equal to 1: an ellipsoid. All three coefficients equal gives a sphere.
  • Three squares, one sign different, equal to 0: a cone.
  • Three squares, one sign different, equal to 1: a hyperboloid of one sheet (one minus sign).
  • Three squares, two signs different, equal to 1: a hyperboloid of two sheets (two minus signs).
The odd one out names the axis. For a paraboloid it is the variable that is not squared. For a cone or a hyperboloid it is the variable whose sign differs from the other two. \(x^2-y^2+z^2=1\) has its axis along the \(y\)-axis, and it is a hyperboloid of one sheet.
One sheet or two? Count minus signs, not sheets. With \(=1\) on the right, one minus sign gives one connected piece and two minus signs give two. Checking the trace at \(z=0\) settles it instantly: if that trace is empty, the surface has to be in two pieces.

Shifted surfaces: complete the square

Real problems rarely sit at the origin. Linear terms mean the surface has been translated. Group by variable, complete the square, and read off the center. The classic case is the sphere:

\(x^2+y^2+z^2-6x+4z=3\;\Longrightarrow\;(x-3)^2+(y)^2+(z+2)^2=3+9+4=16\)Center \((3,0,-2)\), radius 4. Half the coefficient, squared, added to both sides, once per variable.

If the squared terms carry a common factor, divide it out first. \(2x^2+2y^2+2z^2-8x=0\) is the same surface as \(x^2+y^2+z^2-4x=0\), which is the sphere centered at \((2,0,0)\) with radius 2. The same completion works on any quadric: it tells you the center, and the surface type is unchanged by the shift.
