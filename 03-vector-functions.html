Chapter 1 · Stewart 13.1

Vector functions and space curves

A vector function takes a number in and hands you a vector back. Follow the tip of that vector and you trace a curve.

A vector function is written

\(\mathbf{r}(t)=\langle f(t),\,g(t),\,h(t)\rangle = f(t)\,\mathbf{i}+g(t)\,\mathbf{j}+h(t)\,\mathbf{k}\)\(f,g,h\) are the component functions. They are ordinary single-variable functions, so everything you know about them still applies.

As \(t\) runs over its interval, the tip of \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) sweeps out a space curve. The variable \(t\) is the parameter. Think of \(t\) as time and the curve as the path of a moving particle: the vector function carries more information than the curve alone, because it also says when the particle is where.

The domain of \(\mathbf{r}\) is the set of \(t\) for which every component is defined, so it is the intersection of the three component domains. The three usual restrictions:

  • \(\sqrt{t-a}\) needs \(t\ge a\) (a closed end).
  • \(\ln(b-t)\) needs \(t<b\) (an open end).
  • \(\dfrac{1}{t-c}\) needs \(t\ne c\) (a puncture).

Limits are also component-wise: \(\lim_{t\to a}\mathbf{r}(t)=\big\langle \lim f,\ \lim g,\ \lim h\big\rangle\), provided each limit exists. \(\mathbf{r}\) is continuous at \(a\) exactly when all three components are.

Explore: trace a space curve

Drag to rotate, scroll to zoom. The dashed blue vector is the position vector \(\mathbf{r}(t)\); the purple dot is its tip.

Slide \(t\) on the helix and watch \(x\) and \(y\) go around a circle while \(z\) climbs steadily: that is exactly what \(\langle\cos t,\sin t,t\rangle\) says. On the twisted cubic, notice the curve is not in any plane. On \(\langle\cos t,\sin t,\sin 3t\rangle\), the shadow on the \(xy\)-plane is still the unit circle.

The curves worth memorizing

  • Line through \(P_0\) with direction \(\mathbf{v}\): \(\mathbf{r}(t)=\mathbf{r}_0+t\,\mathbf{v}\), so every component is linear in \(t\).
  • Circle of radius \(a\) about the \(z\)-axis in the plane \(z=c\): \(\langle a\cos t,\,a\sin t,\,c\rangle\). Swap which slot holds the constant to wrap the circle around a different axis.
  • Helix: \(\langle a\cos t,\,a\sin t,\,bt\rangle\). Two circular components plus one linear component. The axis is the one that grows linearly.
  • Twisted cubic: \(\langle t,\,t^2,\,t^3\rangle\). The graph of \(y=x^2,\ z=x^3\) read as a curve.
  • Graph of a function \(y=f(x)\) in the plane \(z=0\): \(\langle t,\,f(t),\,0\rangle\).

Two surfaces meeting in a curve is the other common source of parametrizations: parametrize what one surface forces (usually the cylinder or the shadow), then use the other equation to solve for the remaining coordinate. For \(x^2+y^2=4\) and \(z=x\), take \(x=2\cos t\), \(y=2\sin t\), then \(z=x=2\cos t\).

Where a curve meets a surface

This is a substitution problem, not a geometry problem. Put the components of \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) into the equation of the surface, solve the resulting equation for \(t\), then feed each \(t\) back into \(\mathbf{r}\) to get a point.

\(\text{surface }F(x,y,z)=0\ \Longrightarrow\ F\big(f(t),g(t),h(t)\big)=0\)One equation in one unknown. Solve for \(t\), then report the points \(\mathbf{r}(t)\).
Domain first. Every question about a vector function starts with "for which \(t\) does this make sense". Take the intersection, and keep track of which ends are closed (from a square root) and which are open (from a logarithm or a division).
Values of \(t\) are not points. When a problem asks at what points a curve meets a surface, solving for \(t\) is only half the work. Substitute each \(t\) back into \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) and report \((x,y,z)\).

Chapter 2 · Stewart 13.2

Derivatives, tangents, and integrals

Differentiate each component and you get a vector that is tangent to the curve and points the way you are going.

\(\mathbf{r}'(t)=\lim_{h\to 0}\dfrac{\mathbf{r}(t+h)-\mathbf{r}(t)}{h}=\langle f'(t),\,g'(t),\,h'(t)\rangle\)The difference quotient is a secant vector; in the limit it becomes the tangent vector. In practice: differentiate each slot and leave the brackets alone.

The direction of \(\mathbf{r}'(t)\) is the direction of increasing \(t\), so it is the direction of motion. Its length \(|\mathbf{r}'(t)|\) is the speed. Stripping the length off leaves the unit tangent vector:

\(\mathbf{T}(t)=\dfrac{\mathbf{r}'(t)}{|\mathbf{r}'(t)|}\)Same direction as \(\mathbf{r}'\), length exactly 1. Compute \(\mathbf{r}'\), compute \(|\mathbf{r}'|\), divide.

A curve is smooth on an interval when \(\mathbf{r}'\) is continuous and \(\mathbf{r}'(t)\ne\mathbf{0}\) there. The nonzero part matters: where \(\mathbf{r}'=\mathbf{0}\) the particle stops and the curve is allowed to have a corner or a cusp, and \(\mathbf{T}\) is undefined.

The tangent line

A line needs a point and a direction. At \(t=t_0\), the point is \(\mathbf{r}(t_0)\) and the direction is \(\mathbf{r}'(t_0)\):

\(\mathbf{L}(s)=\mathbf{r}(t_0)+s\,\mathbf{r}'(t_0)\)Componentwise, \(x=x_0+s\,x'_0\), \(y=y_0+s\,y'_0\), \(z=z_0+s\,z'_0\). Any nonzero multiple of \(\mathbf{r}'(t_0)\) is an equally good direction.

Step-by-step: the tangent line at \(t_0\)

Four moves, always the same four: differentiate, plug in for the point, plug in for the direction, assemble. The only place students slip is step 3, where it is tempting to reuse the point instead of the derivative.

Differentiation rules

They look exactly like the single-variable rules, with one warning about order.

\(\begin{aligned}&\frac{d}{dt}\big[\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\big]=\mathbf{u}'+\mathbf{v}' \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\big[c\,\mathbf{u}\big]=c\,\mathbf{u}' \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\big[f(t)\,\mathbf{u}(t)\big]=f'\mathbf{u}+f\,\mathbf{u}'\\[4pt] &\frac{d}{dt}\big[\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}\big]=\mathbf{u}'\cdot\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}' \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\big[\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}\big]=\mathbf{u}'\times\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}' \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\big[\mathbf{u}(f(t))\big]=f'(t)\,\mathbf{u}'(f(t))\end{aligned}\)Two product rules: one gives a number, one gives a vector. In the cross product rule the factors must stay in their original order.

A useful consequence: if \(|\mathbf{r}(t)|\) is constant then \(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{r}\) is constant, so differentiating gives \(2\,\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{r}'=0\). A vector of constant length is always perpendicular to its own derivative. That is why \(\mathbf{T}\cdot\mathbf{T}'=0\), which the next chapter needs.

Integrals

\(\displaystyle\int_a^b \mathbf{r}(t)\,dt=\left\langle \int_a^b f\,dt,\ \int_a^b g\,dt,\ \int_a^b h\,dt\right\rangle\)Three ordinary integrals in a bracket. For indefinite integrals the constant of integration is a constant vector \(\mathbf{C}\).
Same direction, different length. \(\mathbf{r}'(t_0)\), \(2\,\mathbf{r}'(t_0)\), and \(\mathbf{T}(t_0)\) all point the same way, so all three are legal direction vectors for the tangent line. Only \(\mathbf{T}\) has length 1, and only \(\mathbf{r}'\) has the physical speed built in.
The direction is \(\mathbf{r}'(t_0)\), not \(\mathbf{r}(t_0)\). The point comes from \(\mathbf{r}\), the direction comes from \(\mathbf{r}'\). Writing \(\mathbf{r}(t_0)+s\,\mathbf{r}(t_0)\) gives a line through the origin, which is almost never tangent to anything.

Chapter 3 · Stewart 13.1 and 13.3

One curve, many parametrizations

A curve is a set of points. A parametrization is a way of running along it. Change the way you run and three things can change: which part you visit, which direction you go, and how fast.

Suppose \(\mathbf{p}(t)\) traces a curve \(C\) as \(t\) runs over all real numbers. Substitute \(t=g(u)\) for some function \(g\) and you get a new vector function \(\mathbf{q}(u)=\mathbf{p}(g(u))\). Every point of \(\mathbf{q}\) is a point of \(\mathbf{p}\), so \(\mathbf{q}\) also lies on \(C\). The chain rule tells you everything else:

\(\mathbf{q}(u)=\mathbf{p}(g(u)) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbf{q}'(u)=g'(u)\,\mathbf{p}'(g(u))\)The velocity of the new parametrization is the old velocity at the corresponding point, scaled by \(g'(u)\). The sign of \(g'\) sets the direction, the size of \(g'\) sets the speed, and the range of \(g\) sets how much of the curve you cover.
  • Coverage. \(\mathbf{q}\) reaches exactly the points \(\mathbf{p}(t)\) with \(t\) in the range of \(g\). If \(g(u)=u^2\), only \(t\ge 0\) is reached: half the curve. If \(g(u)=\sin u\), only \(-1\le t\le 1\).
  • Direction. If \(g'>0\) everywhere, \(\mathbf{q}\) runs along \(C\) the same way as \(\mathbf{p}\). If \(g'<0\) everywhere, the opposite way. If \(g'\) changes sign, \(\mathbf{q}\) turns around and retraces part of the curve.
  • Speed. At a shared point, \(|\mathbf{q}'(u)|=|g'(u)|\,|\mathbf{p}'(t)|\). With \(g(u)=2u\) the new runner is twice as fast everywhere. With \(g(u)=u^3\) it is slower near \(u=0\) (it pauses there, since \(g'(0)=0\)) and faster far away.

Parametrization

Try this. Run \(\mathbf{q}(u)=\langle u^2,u^4\rangle\) from left to right: it comes in along the right half, stops at the origin, and goes back out. It never sees \(x<0\). Then run \(\mathbf{m}(w)=\langle w^3,w^6\rangle\): it covers everything, in the same direction as \(\mathbf{p}\), but watch the orange arrow shrink to nothing at the origin.

Proving that a parametrization traces exactly a curve

When a curve is given as the intersection of two surfaces and you are asked to show that \(\mathbf{p}(t)\) "traces out all of \(C\)", there are two separate things to check, and an exam answer needs both:

  1. Every \(\mathbf{p}(t)\) lies on \(C\). Substitute the components into each surface equation and confirm both hold for every \(t\).
  2. Every point of \(C\) is some \(\mathbf{p}(t)\). Take an arbitrary point \((x,y,z)\) on \(C\). Use one coordinate to solve for \(t\) (usually \(t=x\)), then use the surface equations to show the other coordinates are forced to equal the remaining components of \(\mathbf{p}(t)\).

Worked example

Let \(C\) be the intersection of \(z=x^2\) and \(y=2x\). Claim: \(\mathbf{p}(t)=\langle t,\,2t,\,t^2\rangle\) traces all of \(C\).

Step 1. With \(x=t\), \(y=2t\), \(z=t^2\): the first surface needs \(z=x^2\), and \(t^2=t^2\). The second needs \(y=2x\), and \(2t=2t\). So every \(\mathbf{p}(t)\) is on \(C\).

Step 2. Let \((x,y,z)\) be any point of \(C\). Set \(t=x\). Because the point is on \(C\), \(y=2x=2t\) and \(z=x^2=t^2\). So \((x,y,z)=\mathbf{p}(t)\). Every point of \(C\) is hit.

Now compare \(\mathbf{q}(u)=\langle u^2,\,2u^2,\,u^4\rangle=\mathbf{p}(u^2)\): Step 1 still works, but Step 2 fails for points with \(x<0\), since \(u^2=x\) has no solution. It covers only half of \(C\), and it retraces that half. And \(\mathbf{r}(s)=\langle -s,\,-2s,\,s^2\rangle=\mathbf{p}(-s)\) covers all of \(C\) in the opposite direction at the same speed, while \(\mathbf{m}(w)=\mathbf{p}(w^3)\) covers all of it in the same direction but not at the same speed.

Lying on the curve is the easy half. Students check Step 1, see it works, and stop. Coverage is the half that separates \(\langle t,t^2,t^3\rangle\) from \(\langle t^2,t^4,t^6\rangle\), and it is the half that gets asked about.

Parametrizing an intersection curve

To parametrize the curve where two surfaces meet, let the more restrictive surface pick the parametrization and let the other one supply the missing coordinate. A cylinder \(x^2+z^2=r^2\) forces \(x=r\cos t\), \(z=r\sin t\); a plane \(ax+by+cz=d\) then hands you \(y=(d-ax-cz)/b\). A parabolic cylinder \(y=x^2\) forces \(x=t\), \(y=t^2\); a second surface \(z=xy\) then gives \(z=t^3\).

Chapter 4 · Stewart 13.3

Arc length and curvature

Add up the speed to get the length. Measure how fast the direction turns to get the curvature.

Over a short time \(dt\) the particle covers a distance \(|\mathbf{r}'(t)|\,dt\). Adding those up gives the length of the curve:

\(L=\displaystyle\int_a^b |\mathbf{r}'(t)|\,dt=\int_a^b\sqrt{\left(\tfrac{dx}{dt}\right)^2+\left(\tfrac{dy}{dt}\right)^2+\left(\tfrac{dz}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt\)The arc length differential is \(ds=|\mathbf{r}'(t)|\,dt\). The formula is just \(\int ds\).

These integrals are only doable when the expression under the square root turns into a perfect square, so textbook problems are built backward from that. When you square and add and get something ugly, check your derivatives before you fight the integral.

Measuring from a starting point gives the arc length function \(s(t)=\int_a^t|\mathbf{r}'(u)|\,du\), so \(ds/dt=|\mathbf{r}'(t)|\). Solving for \(t\) in terms of \(s\) and substituting reparametrizes the curve by arc length: the same curve traced at speed exactly 1, where the parameter is distance travelled. You rarely have to carry this out, but it is the reason the next definition is a statement about shape and not about speed.

Curvature

Curvature \(\kappa\) is how fast the unit tangent turns per unit of distance along the curve.

\(\kappa=\left|\dfrac{d\mathbf{T}}{ds}\right|=\dfrac{|\mathbf{T}'(t)|}{|\mathbf{r}'(t)|}=\dfrac{|\mathbf{r}'(t)\times\mathbf{r}''(t)|}{|\mathbf{r}'(t)|^{3}}\)Dividing by \(|\mathbf{r}'|\) converts "per unit time" into "per unit distance", which is what makes \(\kappa\) depend on the curve and not on how fast you drive along it. The cross product form is the one to use in practice.

For a plane curve given as a graph \(y=f(x)\), take \(\mathbf{r}(x)=\langle x, f(x)\rangle\) and the formula collapses to

\(\kappa(x)=\dfrac{|f''(x)|}{\big[1+\big(f'(x)\big)^2\big]^{3/2}}\)At a point where the tangent is horizontal, \(f'=0\) and this is just \(|f''|\). At an inflection point \(f''=0\), so \(\kappa=0\).
  • A line has \(\kappa=0\): the direction never turns.
  • A circle of radius \(a\) has \(\kappa=1/a\) at every point: small circles bend hard.
  • A helix \(\langle a\cos t,a\sin t,bt\rangle\) has the constant curvature \(\kappa=\dfrac{a}{a^2+b^2}\).

The reciprocal \(\rho=1/\kappa\) is the radius of curvature. The circle of that radius, sitting on the concave side of the curve and touching it at the point, is the osculating circle: the circle that matches the curve's position, tangent direction, and curvature all at once.

Step-by-step: curvature from \(\mathbf{r}'\times\mathbf{r}''\)

Differentiate twice, evaluate both at \(t_0\), cross them, then divide the length of the cross product by the cube of \(|\mathbf{r}'|\). The cube is the part people forget.

Explore: the osculating circle

Drag anywhere on the plot to move the point. Orange is \(\mathbf{T}\), green is the unit normal \(\mathbf{N}\), and the dashed blue circle is the osculating circle of radius \(1/\kappa\).

On \(y=x^2\) the circle is smallest at the vertex, where the curve bends hardest, and balloons as you move out the arms. On \(y=x^3-x\) drag to the inflection point at the origin: \(\kappa\) drops to 0 and the circle blows up to a straight line. On the circle preset the osculating circle is the curve itself, and \(\kappa\) stays at \(1/2\).

Curvature ignores speed. \(\langle\cos t,\sin t\rangle\) and \(\langle\cos 5t,\sin 5t\rangle\) trace the same unit circle at wildly different speeds, and both have \(\kappa=1\). Every curvature formula divides by a power of \(|\mathbf{r}'|\) precisely to cancel the parametrization out.
Watch the exponents. It is \(\kappa=|\mathbf{r}'\times\mathbf{r}''|/|\mathbf{r}'|^{3}\), with a cube, and \(\kappa=|f''|/(1+f'^2)^{3/2}\), with a \(3/2\) power on the whole bracket. Using \(|\mathbf{r}'|\) or \((1+f'^2)\) to the first power is the single most common arithmetic error in this section.

Chapter 5 · Stewart 13.4

Velocity and acceleration

Read the same three derivatives as physics: position, velocity, acceleration.

\(\mathbf{v}(t)=\mathbf{r}'(t)\qquad \text{speed}=|\mathbf{v}(t)|=\dfrac{ds}{dt}\qquad \mathbf{a}(t)=\mathbf{v}'(t)=\mathbf{r}''(t)\)Velocity is a vector and is tangent to the path. Speed is the single number you get by taking its length.

Running the derivatives backward is just as important. If you know the acceleration and the state at one instant, you integrate twice and the initial conditions pin down the two constant vectors:

\(\mathbf{v}(t)=\mathbf{v}(0)+\displaystyle\int_0^t\mathbf{a}(u)\,du \qquad\quad \mathbf{r}(t)=\mathbf{r}(0)+\displaystyle\int_0^t\mathbf{v}(u)\,du\)Integrate component by component. The constant of integration in the first step is \(\mathbf{v}(0)\); in the second it is \(\mathbf{r}(0)\).

Explore: velocity and acceleration in motion

Orange is \(\mathbf{v}\), blue is \(\mathbf{a}\), both drawn from the moving point and scaled by \(0.6\) so they fit.

On the circle the speed never changes, yet the acceleration is never zero: it points straight at the center the whole way around, because all it does is turn the velocity. On the parabola, \(\mathbf{a}\) is a constant downward vector, and the speed is smallest exactly at the top, where \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\mathbf{a}\) are perpendicular.

Projectile motion

Near the ground the only force is gravity, so \(\mathbf{a}=-g\,\mathbf{j}\) with \(g=9.8\) m/s\(^2\). Launching from the origin at speed \(v_0\) and angle \(\theta\) above the horizontal gives \(\mathbf{v}(0)=\langle v_0\cos\theta,\ v_0\sin\theta\rangle\), and integrating twice gives

\(\mathbf{r}(t)=\big\langle (v_0\cos\theta)\,t,\ (v_0\sin\theta)\,t-\tfrac12 g t^2\big\rangle\)

Setting \(y=0\) gives the flight time \(t=\dfrac{2v_0\sin\theta}{g}\), and the top of the arc is halfway through it. That produces the two formulas worth remembering:

\(\text{range } R=\dfrac{v_0^{2}\sin 2\theta}{g}\qquad\qquad \text{maximum height } H=\dfrac{v_0^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{2g}\)The range is largest at \(\theta=45^\circ\), where \(\sin 2\theta=1\). Angles \(\theta\) and \(90^\circ-\theta\) give the same range but different heights.

Minimizing speed

Speed is \(|\mathbf{v}|=\sqrt{v_1^2+v_2^2+v_3^2}\), and square roots are unpleasant to differentiate. Since \(\sqrt{\ }\) is increasing, \(|\mathbf{v}|\) and \(|\mathbf{v}|^2\) are smallest at the same \(t\). So minimize

\(|\mathbf{v}(t)|^{2}=\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}=v_1^2+v_2^2+v_3^2\)For a curve whose components are quadratic in \(t\), this is an ordinary quadratic in \(t\): set the derivative to zero, or use the vertex at \(t=-B/(2A)\).

Tangential and normal components of acceleration

Acceleration splits into a part that changes the speed and a part that turns the velocity:

\(\mathbf{a}=a_T\,\mathbf{T}+a_N\,\mathbf{N}, \qquad a_T=\dfrac{\mathbf{r}'\cdot\mathbf{r}''}{|\mathbf{r}'|},\qquad a_N=\kappa\,|\mathbf{v}|^2=\dfrac{|\mathbf{r}'\times\mathbf{r}''|}{|\mathbf{r}'|}\)There is no \(\mathbf{B}\) component: \(\mathbf{a}\) always lies in the plane of \(\mathbf{T}\) and \(\mathbf{N}\). Both formulas share the denominator \(|\mathbf{r}'|\); the numerators are the dot product and the cross product of the same two vectors.
Speed is a scalar, velocity is a vector. "How fast" is \(|\mathbf{v}|\), one number. "Which way and how fast" is \(\mathbf{v}\), three numbers. Constant speed does not mean constant velocity, and it certainly does not mean zero acceleration.
Minimize \(|\mathbf{v}|^2\), but answer the question asked. Working with \(|\mathbf{v}|^2\) finds the right \(t\). If the problem asks for the minimum speed, take the square root at the end. If it asks when, report the \(t\).

Chapter 6 · Stewart 16.2

Scalar line integrals

Ordinary integrals add up \(f\) along a straight interval. Line integrals add up \(f\) along a curve, and the arc length element \(ds\) from Chapter 4 does all the work.

Let \(C\) be a smooth curve parametrized by \(\mathbf{r}(t)=\langle x(t),y(t)\rangle\) for \(a\le t\le b\). Chop the curve into tiny pieces of length \(\Delta s\), multiply each by the value of \(f\) there, and add. In the limit:

\(\displaystyle\int_C f(x,y)\,ds=\int_a^b f\big(\mathbf{r}(t)\big)\,\big|\mathbf{r}'(t)\big|\,dt\)Substitute the curve into \(f\), multiply by the speed, integrate over the parameter. The answer is an ordinary single-variable integral.

The factor \(|\mathbf{r}'(t)|\) is the whole idea, and it is nothing new: it is the same arc length element you integrated in Chapter 4 to get the length of a curve.

\(ds=\big|\mathbf{r}'(t)\big|\,dt=\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{dx}{dt}\right)^{2}+\left(\dfrac{dy}{dt}\right)^{2}}\;dt\)Speed times elapsed time equals distance travelled. In space add \((dz/dt)^2\) under the root.

The four parametrizations you need

  • Segment from \(P\) to \(Q\): \(\mathbf{r}(t)=(1-t)P+tQ\), \(0\le t\le 1\). Equivalently \(\mathbf{r}(t)=P+t(Q-P)\). Then \(\mathbf{r}'(t)=Q-P\) is constant, so \(|\mathbf{r}'|=|Q-P|\) is the length of the segment.
  • Circle of radius \(a\) about the origin: \(\mathbf{r}(t)=\langle a\cos t,\,a\sin t\rangle\), \(0\le t\le 2\pi\). Then \(|\mathbf{r}'(t)|=a\), so \(ds=a\,dt\). Use \(0\le t\le\pi\) for the upper half, \(-\pi/2\le t\le \pi/2\) for the right half, \(0\le t\le \pi/2\) for the first-quadrant quarter.
  • Graph \(y=g(x)\): use \(x\) itself as the parameter, \(\mathbf{r}(x)=\langle x,\,g(x)\rangle\). Then \(ds=\sqrt{1+g'(x)^{2}}\,dx\).
  • Helix: \(\mathbf{r}(t)=\langle \cos t,\,\sin t,\,t\rangle\). Then \(\mathbf{r}'=\langle -\sin t,\cos t,1\rangle\) and \(|\mathbf{r}'|=\sqrt{2}\), a constant, which is why helices show up in every problem set.

What it means. If \(f\ge 0\), then \(\int_C f\,ds\) is the area of the curtain, or fence, that stands on \(C\) and reaches up to height \(f\). If instead \(C\) is a wire and \(f\) is its density, the same integral is the mass of the wire. Set \(f=1\) and you get the arc length formula from Chapter 4 back.

Orientation does not matter. Walking the curve backwards gives the same pieces \(\Delta s\) with the same values of \(f\), so \(\int_{-C}f\,ds=\int_C f\,ds\). Lengths are positive no matter which way you walk. Piecewise curves add: if \(C\) is \(C_1\) followed by \(C_2\), integrate over each and add.

Step by step: \(\int_C xy\,ds\) over a segment

    Notice that step 2 does all the work. Once the curve is parametrized, the rest is a one-variable integral you could have done in Calculus I.
